A network with nonlinear layers can, in principle, learn a lot with a single wide hidden layer.
In practice the networks that work best have many layers. The reason is **composition**: each
layer builds slightly more complex features out of the ones below it, so a deep network reuses
what it has already learned instead of matching every pattern from scratch. That's why the
models behind images, speech and language are deep.

## Simple to complex

The lecture's first argument is a picture. In face recognition, early units respond to edges in
small patches of the image. The next layers group edges into parts such as an eye or a nose,
looking at larger areas. Later layers combine parts into whole faces. Speech follows the same
ladder: waveform features (rising or falling pitch, hiss), then basic units of sound called
phonemes, then words, then phrases.

The brain is thought to work in a loosely similar way, which inspired some of this, but the
analogy is easy to push too far. The useful idea is narrower: **features computed once in a low
layer are reused by everything above it.**

## Worked example: parity, deep versus shallow

The second argument is a counting one. Take the parity of n bits: is the number of 1s odd?

**Deep version.** XOR the bits in pairs, then XOR the results in pairs, like a tournament
bracket. For 16 bits that's 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = **15 XOR units** in **4 levels** (log₂ 16 = 4). For
32 bits: 31 units in 5 levels. The size grows in step with n.

**Shallow version.** With one hidden layer, the network can't build partial answers to reuse.
It has to recognise input patterns more or less one by one, which takes on the order of 2^(n−1)
hidden units. For 16 bits that is 2¹⁵ = **32,768 units**; for 32 bits, 2³¹, about **2.1
billion**.

Same function, same answer. Depth turns an exponential cost into a small one. Ng is frank that
he finds this argument less useful for intuition than the face picture, but it shows that the
advantage of depth can be very large, not just a matter of taste.

## Depth is a setting, not a badge

Ng also notes that "deep learning" is partly a rebranding of neural networks with many hidden
layers. His working habit: on a new problem, start with logistic regression or one or two hidden
layers, and treat the number of layers as a hyperparameter you tune. Some problems do end up
needing dozens of layers; that's something you find out, not assume.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the deepest model you can find.** Without a baseline, you can't tell whether
  the depth is helping.
- **Treating the face hierarchy as guaranteed.** It's the typical pattern, and real units are
  often messier than clean edge or eye detectors.
- **Assuming more layers always train well.** Very deep networks need extra techniques, such as
  residual connections and normalization, to train reliably.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Language models** stack dozens of transformer blocks; studies of their internals find that
  earlier layers tend to handle surface patterns such as spelling while later ones track
  meaning (→ transformers-attention).
- **Embeddings** taken from a deep model's later layers capture meaning rather than surface
  form, which is why they work for search (→ embeddings-vector-databases).
- **Training deep networks** depends on passing a learning signal back through every layer,
  which the next lesson explains (→ backprop-intuition).
