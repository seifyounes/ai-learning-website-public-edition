Every layer computes z = W·x + b and then applies an activation g. This lesson is about choosing
g. The choice decides two things: what range of values the layer can output, and how easily
gradients pass back through it during training. The second one is why most hidden layers today
use ReLU or a close relative rather than the sigmoid early networks used.

## The four candidates

- **Sigmoid:** σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)), output between 0 and 1. Its slope peaks at 0.25 when
  z = 0 and fades towards zero on both sides.
- **Tanh:** output between −1 and 1, centred on zero. Mathematically a stretched and shifted
  sigmoid. In hidden layers it usually beats sigmoid, because outputs that average near zero are
  easier for the next layer to work with.
- **ReLU:** max(0, z). Slope 1 for any positive input, 0 for any negative input. Cheap to compute
  and it doesn't flatten out for large positive z.
- **Leaky ReLU:** max(0.01z, z). Like ReLU, but negative inputs keep a small slope, so a unit
  never goes completely flat.

## Worked example: how flat is flat?

Compute the slope of each function at a moderate input and a large one.

| z | sigmoid slope | tanh slope | ReLU slope |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.235 | 0.786 | 1 |
| 4 | 0.018 | 0.0013 | 1 |

At z = 4, a sigmoid passes back about 2% of the gradient it receives, and tanh about 0.1%. Now
stack layers. Backpropagation multiplies these slopes layer by layer, and even the sigmoid's best
case is 0.25. Through 10 layers that is at most 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.000001 from the activations alone.
The early layers get almost no signal, so they learn very slowly. ReLU's slope of 1 on positive
inputs avoids that shrinkage, which is a large part of why it became the default.

## A rule of thumb

- **Hidden layers:** ReLU by default; leaky ReLU is a reasonable alternative.
- **Output layer:** sigmoid for a yes/no prediction; a linear output for a number (→
  why-nonlinearity covers this case).
- **Unsure?** Train with two or three choices and compare them on a held-out dev set. It's hard
  to know in advance which one suits a given dataset.

ReLU's zero slope for negative inputs sounds like the same problem, but in practice enough
units are positive for any input to keep learning going.

## Common mistakes

- **Sigmoid in every hidden layer out of habit.** It works in tiny networks and stalls deep ones.
- **ReLU at the output of a classifier.** Its output isn't bounded to 0–1, so it can't be read
  as a probability.
- **Softmax for multi-label tasks.** Softmax makes the outputs compete; independent labels need
  independent sigmoids.
- **Reading a saturated sigmoid as confidence.** An output of 0.999 is also a unit whose
  gradient is nearly gone.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Language models** use smooth ReLU-like functions in their MLP blocks: GPT-2 used GELU, and
  several open model families use SwiGLU, a gated variant (→ transformers-attention).
- **Next-token prediction** ends in a softmax over the vocabulary, the one-of-many version of
  the output rule above (→ makemore-bigram-model builds one).
- **Classifiers and moderation scores** you call through an API end in a sigmoid or a softmax,
  and the threshold you set on those scores trades precision against recall
  (→ evaluation-metrics).
