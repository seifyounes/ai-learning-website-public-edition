task: |
  Translate vectors by hand, then show that rotating every embedding changes the numbers but not
  the search results.

  **On paper (10 minutes).** A basis has b₁ = (1, 1) and b₂ = (−1, 1) in our coordinates.

  1. A vector has coordinates (3, 1) in that basis. What is it in ours?
  2. Our vector (5, 1): what is it in that basis? (Use the inverse, or solve two equations.)

  **In Python (15 minutes, free Google Colab works).**

  ```python
  import numpy as np

  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  E = rng.normal(size=(5, 8))                    # 5 "embeddings", 8 dimensions

  def cos_table(M):
      U = M / np.linalg.norm(M, axis=1, keepdims=True)
      return U @ U.T

  R, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(8, 8)))   # a random rotation (orthogonal matrix)
  rotated = E @ R
  print("first vector, before:", E[0, :3].round(2), "after:", rotated[0, :3].round(2))
  print("cosines unchanged:", np.allclose(cos_table(E), cos_table(rotated)))

  S = np.diag([10, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])         # stretch one axis: not a rotation
  print("cosines after stretch unchanged:", np.allclose(cos_table(E), cos_table(E @ S)))
  ```

  Write one line on what the first two prints say about reading "dimension 3" of an embedding,
  and one on why the stretch breaks the cosines while the rotation doesn't.

  **No code?** Do the paper part, then rotate a = (1, 2) and b = (3, 1) by 90° (each (x, y)
  becomes (−y, x)) and check that a·b is 5 both before and after.

  Success: (2, 4) and (3, −2) on paper; the rotation keeps every cosine while the stretch
  doesn't; and you can say why.

Every list of numbers you've written so far assumed one basis: the standard unit vectors. This
lesson drops that assumption. The same arrow has different coordinates in a different basis,
and a matrix translates between them. That explains a practical fact about embeddings: their
individual coordinates are an accident of training, while the angles between them are what
carry meaning.

## Coordinates are a language

The coordinates (3, 2) mean "3 of the first basis vector plus 2 of the second". Everything about
what those numbers mean, which direction is first and how long one unit is, sits in the choice
of basis. Pick different basis vectors and the same arrow gets different numbers. Space itself
has no grid; each basis draws its own. Only the origin is shared.

## Translating between bases

Say a second basis has vectors b₁ and b₂, and you know them in your own coordinates. Put them as
the columns of a matrix P.

- **Their coordinates into yours:** multiply by P. Coordinates (c₁, c₂) in their basis mean
  c₁·b₁ + c₂·b₂, which is exactly P times (c₁, c₂).
- **Your coordinates into theirs:** multiply by P⁻¹.

It feels backwards at first: geometrically P moves your grid onto theirs, yet numerically it
translates their numbers into yours. And a transformation M you know in your coordinates becomes
**P⁻¹MP** in theirs: translate in, apply M, translate back.

## Worked example: two bases, one rotation

Take b₁ = (1, 1) and b₂ = (−1, 1), so P has those columns. Its determinant is 1·1 − (−1)·1 = 2,
and P⁻¹ = (1/2) × the matrix with rows (1, 1) and (−1, 1).

1. **Theirs to yours.** (3, 1) in their basis is 3·(1, 1) + 1·(−1, 1) = **(2, 4)**.
2. **Yours to theirs.** Your (5, 1) becomes (1/2)·(5 + 1, −5 + 1) = **(3, −2)**. Check:
   3·(1, 1) − 2·(−1, 1) = (5, 1).

Now a special kind of change: a pure rotation. Rotate a = (1, 2) and b = (3, 1) by 90°, so each
(x, y) becomes (−y, x): a becomes (−2, 1) and b becomes (−1, 3). Every coordinate changed, but
a·b = 3 + 2 = 5 before and (−2)(−1) + 1·3 = 5 after, and the lengths are unchanged. Rotations
keep all dot products, so they keep every cosine similarity.

## Why embedding dimensions rarely mean anything alone

A model's embedding space comes with whatever axes training happened to produce. Rotate the whole
space and adjust the next layer to match, and the model would work exactly as well, with every
single coordinate different. So "dimension 212 is sentiment" is usually wrong: a concept, if the
model represents it as a direction at all, is generally a mix of many coordinates. What survives
any rotation are the angles and lengths, which is why search compares whole vectors.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading one coordinate as a concept.** Look for directions, not single axes.
- **Comparing vectors from two models.** Same size doesn't mean same basis; re-embed with one
  model.
- **Using P where you need P⁻¹.** P takes their coordinates to yours.
- **Assuming every basis change preserves similarity.** Rotations do; stretching an axis
  doesn't.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Embedding stores and model upgrades:** a new model means a new basis, so re-embed
  (→ embeddings-vector-databases).
- **PCA and SVD:** both choose a new basis of directions ordered by importance
  (→ eigenvectors-eigenvalues).
- **Interpretability:** research looks for meaningful directions in a model's activations rather
  than meaningful single neurons (→ neural-networks).
