An **embedding** is a vector: a point in a space with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. The
whole trick of modern AI retrieval is that *similar meanings become nearby directions*. The last
lesson's dot product measures alignment, but it also grows with length. Divide by the two
lengths and you get **cosine similarity**, the cosine of the angle between two vectors and the
number most semantic search and RAG systems rank by.

## From word counts to angles

The simplest way to turn text into vectors is to count words. With two words, "hello world" is
(1, 1) and "hello" is (1, 0). The angle between their arrows is 45°, and cos 45° ≈ 0.71.

- **Same direction:** angle 0°, cosine 1.
- **Nothing in common:** angle 90°, cosine 0.
- **Repeating a word** pushes the point further out along the same line, so the score doesn't
  change. Cosine looks only at direction.

You don't need to draw anything: cosine = (a · b) ÷ (|a| × |b|), the dot product divided by the
two lengths. That works in five dimensions or 1,536.

Word counts have a weakness: "refund" and "money back" share no words, so they score 0.
Embedding models fix that by learning vectors where texts with related meanings point in
similar directions. The score is computed the same way.

> 💡 **Try it:** in your head — if cos(a,b) = 1, the texts mean nearly the same thing; what
> does cos(a,b) ≈ 0 mean? (Unrelated, not opposite — that would drift negative.) This is
> exactly the intuition you'll use to debug "why did my RAG retrieve garbage."

## Worked example: rank two documents by hand

Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions, but the arithmetic is identical in three. Say a
query embeds to **q = (1, 2, 0)** and two documents embed to **A = (2, 3, 1)** and
**B = (0, 1, 3)**.

1. **Dot products.** q·A = 1×2 + 2×3 + 0×1 = 8. q·B = 1×0 + 2×1 + 0×3 = 2.
2. **Lengths** (square root of the sum of squares). |q| = √5 ≈ 2.24, |A| = √14 ≈ 3.74,
   |B| = √10 ≈ 3.16.
3. **Cosine.** cos(q, A) = 8 ÷ (2.24 × 3.74) ≈ **0.96**. cos(q, B) = 2 ÷ (2.24 × 3.16) ≈
   **0.28**.

Document A wins: its arrow points almost the same way as the query. Notice what you did *not*
need: any idea what each coordinate "means". A vector database does exactly these three steps,
just across millions of documents, with indexing so it doesn't have to compare every pair.

## Common mistakes

- **Rankings look random** → the query and the documents were embedded with different models
  (or versions of one), so their spaces don't line up → re-embed everything with one model and
  store the model name next to each vector.
- **Long documents always win** → raw dot product without normalization, so bigger vectors
  score higher regardless of direction → use cosine, or check that your vector store's distance
  setting matches what the embedding model recommends.
- **The right document ranks fifth** → its chunk covers several topics, so its vector averages
  them and points nowhere in particular → split into chunks of one idea each and re-check the
  scores.
- **Reading cosine as a percentage** → 0.8 doesn't mean "80% the same", and score ranges vary
  by model → compare scores within one model, and calibrate any cutoff on your own examples.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Semantic search and RAG:** retrieval ranks chunks by cosine against the query
  (→ rag-fundamentals, → embeddings-vector-databases).
- **Deduplication and clustering:** near-duplicate texts have cosine close to 1.
- **Evaluating answers:** some evals compare an answer's embedding with a reference answer's
  (→ evals-and-testing).
