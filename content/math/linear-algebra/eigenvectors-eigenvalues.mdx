task: |
  Find eigenvectors by hand, then watch repeated multiplication and PCA find them for you.

  **On paper (10 minutes).** For the matrix with columns (4, 1) and (2, 3), write
  det(A − λI) = 0, solve for both eigenvalues, and find an eigenvector for each. Check one by
  multiplying.

  **In Python (20 minutes, free Google Colab works).**

  ```python
  import numpy as np

  A = np.array([[4.0, 2.0], [1.0, 3.0]])
  vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
  print("eigenvalues:", vals)
  print("eigenvectors (columns):", vecs.round(3))

  v = np.array([0.3, -0.9])                     # any start
  for _ in range(30):
      v = A @ v
      v = v / np.linalg.norm(v)                 # keep it unit length
  print("after repeated multiplication:", v.round(3))

  print("0.9^50 =", 0.9 ** 50, "  1.1^50 =", 1.1 ** 50)

  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  x = rng.normal(size=500)
  data = np.column_stack([x, 0.5 * x + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=500)])
  cvals, cvecs = np.linalg.eigh(np.cov(data.T))
  print("variance per direction:", cvals.round(3))
  print("main direction:", cvecs[:, -1].round(3))
  ```

Reading a matrix column by column tells you where the basis vectors go, but that depends on the
basis you happened to pick. **Eigenvectors** describe a transformation in its own terms: the
directions it only stretches or squashes, never turns. They explain what happens when a step is
repeated many times, and they are the core of PCA.

## Vectors that stay on their line

Apply a matrix and most vectors get knocked off the line they started on. A few special ones
land back on their own line, just scaled. Those are **eigenvectors**, and the scale factor is the
**eigenvalue**: 3 means stretched three times, 0.5 squashed by half, −1 flipped. For a 3D
rotation, the eigenvector is the axis, with eigenvalue 1.

## Finding them

The definition is **A·v = λ·v**. Move everything to one side: (A − λI)·v = 0, where I is the
identity. A non-zero v can only land on zero if A − λI squashes space, which means its
**determinant is zero** (→ determinant-area-scaling). So: find the λ values that make
det(A − λI) = 0, then for each one, find the vectors A − λI sends to zero (its null space,
→ rank-column-null-space).

Not every matrix cooperates. A 90° rotation turns every vector, so it has no real eigenvectors;
a shear has only one line of them.

## Worked example: a 2 × 2 matrix and its powers

Let A have columns (4, 1) and (2, 3), so its rows are (4, 2) and (1, 3).

1. **Eigenvalues.** det(A − λI) = (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − 2·1 = λ² − 7λ + 10 = (λ − 5)(λ − 2), so
   **λ = 5 and λ = 2**.
2. **Eigenvectors.** For λ = 5, A − 5I has rows (−1, 2) and (1, −2), which sends **(2, 1)** to
   zero. Check: A·(2, 1) = (8 + 2, 2 + 3) = (10, 5) = 5·(2, 1). For λ = 2, the eigenvector is
   **(1, −1)**: A·(1, −1) = (2, −2).
3. **Powers.** Write (3, 0) = 1·(2, 1) + 1·(1, −1). Then A¹⁰·(3, 0) = 5¹⁰·(2, 1) + 2¹⁰·(1, −1)
   = 9,765,625·(2, 1) + 1,024·(1, −1). The eigenvalue-5 direction completely dominates.

That's the **eigenbasis** idea: in the basis of eigenvectors, the matrix is diagonal (here 5 and
2 on the diagonal), so its powers just raise each eigenvalue to that power. Note also that
5 × 2 = 10 is the determinant.

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting every matrix to have real eigenvectors.** Rotations don't.
- **Treating the eigenvector's length as meaningful.** Any non-zero multiple is also an
  eigenvector; only the direction matters.
- **Ignoring eigenvalues near 1.** Over many steps, 1.1 grows about 117-fold in 50 steps, and 0.9
  shrinks to about 0.005.
- **Using eigenvectors of a non-square matrix.** They only exist for square ones; the SVD below
  handles every shape.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **PCA:** the eigenvectors of a dataset's covariance matrix are the directions of most spread,
  used to plot and compress embeddings (→ embeddings-vector-databases).
- **Exploding and vanishing gradients:** multiplying by similar matrices many times amplifies
  eigenvalues above 1 and kills those below (→ neural-networks).
- **Ranking and long-run behaviour:** repeated-step processes, from PageRank-style ranking to
  user-flow models, settle on an eigenvector.
- **The SVD and low rank:** the three optional lectures below extend this to any matrix and show
  the best low-rank approximation, the idea LoRA relies on (→ rank-column-null-space).
