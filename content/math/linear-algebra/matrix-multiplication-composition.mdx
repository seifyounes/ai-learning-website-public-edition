task: |
  Check order, grouping and layer collapse with a few lines of Python.

  **In Python (20 minutes, free Google Colab works).**

  ```python
  import time
  import numpy as np

  A = np.array([[2, 0], [0, 1]])   # stretch x by 2
  B = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])   # shear
  x = np.array([1, 1])
  print("A first, then B:", B @ A @ x)
  print("B first, then A:", A @ B @ x)

  # Two linear layers with nothing in between collapse into one matrix.
  W1, W2 = np.random.randn(4, 4), np.random.randn(4, 4)
  v = np.random.randn(4)
  print(np.allclose(W2 @ (W1 @ v), (W2 @ W1) @ v))                 # True
  print(np.allclose(W2 @ np.maximum(0, W1 @ v), (W2 @ W1) @ v))    # usually False

  # Same answer, very different cost.
  n = 2000
  M, N, y = np.random.randn(n, n), np.random.randn(n, n), np.random.randn(n)
  t = time.perf_counter(); (M @ N) @ y; t1 = time.perf_counter() - t
  t = time.perf_counter(); M @ (N @ y); t2 = time.perf_counter() - t
  print(f"(M @ N) @ y: {t1:.3f}s   M @ (N @ y): {t2:.4f}s")
  ```

The last lesson made a matrix a movement of space. This one asks what happens when you do
two movements in a row. The answer is the real meaning of **matrix multiplication**, and it
explains two things about neural networks: why layers need activations between them, and why
the order you multiply in can change the cost enormously.

## A product is "this, then that"

Rotate the plane, then shear it. The overall effect is still a linear transformation: grid
lines stay parallel and evenly spaced, the origin stays put. So it has a matrix of its own, the
**composition**, and you find it as usual by following the basis vectors through both moves.
That matrix is what we call the **product**.

Products read **right to left**. In B·A·x, A acts on x first and B acts on the result, exactly
like f(g(x)). Following the basis vectors gives the rule: each column of B·A is B times the
matching column of A.

## Worked example: order changes the answer

Let A stretch the x-direction by 2: columns (2, 0) and (0, 1). Let B be a shear: columns
(1, 0) and (1, 1).

**A first, then B (the product B·A).**

1. (1, 0) goes to (2, 0) under A, and B leaves it at 2·(1, 0) + 0·(1, 1) = (2, 0).
2. (0, 1) stays at (0, 1) under A, and B sends it to (1, 1).
3. So B·A has columns (2, 0) and (1, 1). Applied to (1, 1): (2, 0) + (1, 1) = **(3, 1)**.

**B first, then A (the product A·B).** (1, 0) stays put under B, then A sends it to (2, 0).
(0, 1) goes to (1, 1) under B, then A sends it to (2, 1). Applied to (1, 1): (2, 0) + (2, 1) =
**(4, 1)**. Same two matrices, different order, different answer.

## Grouping never matters, cost does

Applying C, then B, then A is the same chain however you bracket it, so (AB)C = A(BC). That's
**associativity**, and it has two practical uses:

- **Stacked linear layers collapse.** W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x: two layers with nothing between them
  are one matrix. Activations break the chain (→ neural-networks).
- **You can choose the cheap order.** For two n × n matrices and a vector, (MN)y costs about n³
  multiplications and M(Ny) about 2n². At n = 2,000 that's 8 billion against 8 million.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading products left to right.** In code, `B @ A @ x` applies A first.
- **Assuming steps commute.** Rotations, shears, normalizations and projections usually don't.
- **Confusing the two properties.** Order (commutativity) can matter; grouping
  (associativity) never changes the result.
- **Forming a big product for one vector.** Multiply the vector through instead.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Depth needs non-linearity:** without activations, a deep network equals one matrix
  (→ neural-networks).
- **Attention** chains products such as QKᵀ and then the result times V; implementations pick
  the order and grouping for speed (→ transformers-attention).
- **Merging adapters:** a LoRA update is a product of two thin matrices that can be multiplied
  out once and added to the weights (→ rank-column-null-space).
