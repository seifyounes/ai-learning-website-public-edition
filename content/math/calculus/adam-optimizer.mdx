task: |
  **Watch Adam give two very different weights the same step (25 minutes, Python).**

  Same stretched loss as the momentum lesson, `x² + 100y²`, where the y-gradient is 100 times
  steeper:

  ```python
  import math

  def adam(lr, steps=2000, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
      x, y = 3.0, 1.0
      v, s = [0.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0]
      for t in range(1, steps + 1):
          g = [2 * x, 200 * y]                 # gradient of x^2 + 100y^2
          p = [x, y]
          for i in range(2):
              v[i] = b1 * v[i] + (1 - b1) * g[i]
              s[i] = b2 * s[i] + (1 - b2) * g[i] ** 2
              v_hat = v[i] / (1 - b1 ** t)
              s_hat = s[i] / (1 - b2 ** t)
              p[i] -= lr * v_hat / (math.sqrt(s_hat) + eps)
          if t == 1:
              print(f"  step 1 moved x by {x - p[0]:.4f} and y by {y - p[1]:.4f}")
          x, y = p
          if x * x + 100 * y * y < 1e-4:
              return t
      return "never"

  for lr in (0.01, 0.1, 0.3):
      print(f"Adam lr={lr}: steps to a tiny loss =", adam(lr))
  ```

  Then delete the two bias-correction lines (use `v[i]` and `s[i]` directly) and rerun lr = 0.1.

  Success: at step 1 both coordinates move by exactly the learning rate, even though their
  gradients are 6 and 200; lr = 0.1 reaches the target in about 82 steps (momentum took about
  103, plain descent about 315); and without bias correction the first step jumps to about
  0.316, more than three times the learning rate.

**You need:** → momentum-optimizer.

The one idea of this lecture: **Adam combines momentum's average of gradients with a second
average, of squared gradients, and divides one by the root of the other.** The first sets the
direction; the second gives every weight its own step size. Add a correction for the averages'
cold start and you have the optimizer that's the usual default in training and fine-tuning tools.

## Why another optimizer

Andrew Ng opens with a caution: many optimizers have been proposed, shown to work on a few
problems, and then failed to generalise, so the field is sceptical of new ones. RMSprop and Adam
are exceptions that worked across a wide range of architectures.

## The two averages

On each step `t`, with `dW` from the current mini-batch:

```text
v = β1·v + (1 − β1)·dW
s = β2·s + (1 − β2)·dW²
```

`v` is the momentum average. `s` is the RMSprop part: a running average of the squared gradient,
element by element, so it tracks how big each weight's gradients usually are. Both start at
zero, so early on they're too small. **Bias correction** fixes that:

```text
v̂ = v / (1 − β1ᵗ)
ŝ = s / (1 − β2ᵗ)
W = W − α · v̂ / (√ŝ + ε)
```

Defaults: `β1 = 0.9`, `β2 = 0.999`, `ε = 10⁻⁸`. People tune `α` and rarely the rest. The name:
adaptive moment estimation, since `v` and `s` estimate the first and second moments of the
gradient.

## Worked example with real numbers

First step, one weight with gradient `0.5`:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| v = 0.1 × 0.5 | 0.05 |
| s = 0.001 × 0.25 | 0.00025 |
| v̂ = 0.05 / 0.1 | 0.5 |
| ŝ = 0.00025 / 0.001 | 0.25 |
| step = α × 0.5 / √0.25 | α |

A second weight with gradient `0.005` gives `v̂ = 0.005`, `√ŝ = 0.005`, and again a step of `α`.
Plain gradient descent would have moved them `0.5α` and `0.005α`, a hundredfold difference;
Adam moves both by about `α`. Without bias correction the first step would be
`0.05 / √0.00025 ≈ 3.16α`, more than three times too large, at the moment training is least
stable.

On the task's stretched loss, where one gradient is 100 times steeper than the other, Adam at
`α = 0.1` reaches the target in about 82 steps, against about 103 for momentum and 315 for plain
descent.

## Common mistakes

- **Tuning β2 or ε before the learning rate.** The learning rate is almost always the lever.
- **Forgetting the memory bill.** Two extra numbers per trainable weight: for a model with 7
  billion parameters in 32-bit precision, that's 56 GB of optimizer state alone.
- **Assuming Adam needs no learning-rate care.** It adapts per-weight *scale*, not the overall
  rate; too large still diverges.
- **Mixing up Adam and AdamW.** AdamW applies weight decay separately from the gradient
  averages, which is why it's the variant usually paired with weight decay.
