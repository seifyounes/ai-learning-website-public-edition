**You need:** the idea of slope (rise over run) from school algebra. Nothing else.

The one idea of this lecture: **a derivative is the ratio between a tiny change in a function's
output and the tiny change in input that caused it, taken as that nudge shrinks.** Training a
model asks this question millions of times: if I nudge this weight, how much does the loss move?
Every gradient you'll meet in this track is a list of these ratios.

## Why "instantaneous" is a trap

Speed is distance divided by time, and that needs two moments. A single frozen instant has no
change in it at all. Calculus gets around this by comparing two moments a small gap `dt` apart,
measuring the distance covered in that gap, `ds`, and asking what `ds / dt` settles toward as the
gap gets smaller. The answer is the slope of the tangent line at a single point on the distance
graph.

The gap never becomes zero. Setting `dt = 0` gives 0/0, which means nothing. The derivative is
the value the ratio *approaches*, and the lecture's suggested reading is: **the best constant
approximation of the rate near that point.**

## Worked example: shrink the nudge yourself

Take `s(t) = t³` and ask for the rate at `t = 2`. Expanding `(2 + dt)³` gives
`8 + 12·dt + 6·dt² + dt³`. Subtract 8, divide by `dt`:

```text
ratio = 12 + 6·dt + dt²
```

Now plug in real nudges:

| dt | ratio |
|---|---|
| 0.1 | 12.61 |
| 0.01 | 12.0601 |
| 0.001 | 12.006001 |

Every leftover term still contains `dt`, so as the nudge shrinks it fades and the ratio settles
on **12**. The same steps at any `t` give `3t²`. The messy details disappear in the limit,
which is why derivatives end up simpler than the ratios they come from.

Now the same move on a toy loss, `loss(w) = (w − 3)²`, at `w = 1`. The loss is 4. Nudge `w` to
1.01 and the loss becomes 3.9601: a change of −0.0399 for a nudge of 0.01, a ratio of about −4.
The sign says "increase w to lower the loss", and the size says the loss responds about four
times as strongly as the nudge. That is one entry of a gradient, found with nothing but
subtraction and division.

## The paradox, and the lesson for AI

The derivative of `t³` at `t = 0` is `3 × 0² = 0`. So is the car moving at the start? Over the
first 0.1 seconds it covers 0.001 m, so it does move, just very slowly compared with the time
passing. A zero derivative says the best constant estimate of the rate is zero, not that nothing
happens. Also note that `t³` has no minimum at 0 even though it is flat there. Keep both facts:
later, a flat spot in a loss can be a valley, a plateau or a saddle.

## Common mistakes

- **Plugging in dt = 0.** You get 0/0. The derivative is a limit, not a substitution.
- **Reading a zero derivative as "at the minimum".** `t³` is flat at 0 and keeps rising.
- **Trusting a big nudge.** With `dt = 0.5` the curvature leaks in; use small nudges, or the
  centred version `(f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h`, which is more accurate for the same `h`.
- **Forgetting the units.** The ratio is "output per unit of input": loss per unit of weight,
  metres per second.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Training:** each entry of the gradient is one of these ratios for one weight (→ gradient-vector-of-partials,
  → calculus-for-optimization).
- **Gradient checks:** engineers verify hand-written backward passes by comparing them to
  nudge-and-divide estimates (→ backprop-chain-rule-calculus).
- **Sensitivity analysis:** "which input moves the output most" is the same question, asked of
