The one idea of this lecture: **the gradient at a point is an arrow showing the direction in
which the function rises fastest, and its length says how steep that climb is.** Training wants
the loss to fall, so it steps the opposite way. That single sign flip is gradient descent.

## The bowl and the hiker

Take `f(x, y) = x² + y²`, a bowl. Its gradient is `[2x, 2y]`: at every point it's twice the
position vector, so the arrows all point straight away from the origin, longer the farther out
you go. Stand on the bowl's wall and ask which way climbs fastest: straight away from the bottom.
The arrow agrees. On a surface with two peaks, the arrows near each peak point toward it. Each
arrow answers the hiker's question for the spot it's drawn on.

The **length** is the steepness. Far up the bowl's wall the arrows are long; near the bottom
(or near a peak) they shrink toward zero because the surface flattens.

## Worked example with real numbers

At the point (3, 4), the gradient of `x² + y²` is `[6, 8]`, with length `√(36 + 64) = 10`.

How fast does f change if you walk one unit in a given direction? The answer is the dot product
of the gradient with that unit direction (this is the directional derivative, the subject of the
'Go deeper' video):

| direction | rate of change |
|---|---|
| along [0.6, 0.8] (the gradient's) | +10 |
| along the x-axis [1, 0] | +6 |
| perpendicular [−0.8, 0.6] | 0 |
| against [−0.6, −0.8] | −10 |

No direction beats +10, and the steepest descent is −10, straight against the gradient. One
descent step with learning rate 0.1 moves from (3, 4) to `(3, 4) − 0.1 × (6, 8) = (2.4, 3.2)`,
and f falls from 25 to 16.

## When steepest isn't straight to the bottom

On a bowl stretched in one direction, `x² + 10y²`, the gradient is `[2x, 20y]`: dominated by the
steep y-direction. Steepest-uphill isn't aimed at the minimum, so descent overshoots across the
narrow direction while creeping along the long one. Starting at (3, 1), a learning rate of 0.09
makes y flip sign every step but still shrink; at 0.11 the flips grow and the loss climbs. The
steep direction caps the learning rate, and the gentle direction pays for it. → momentum-optimizer
is the standard fix.

## Common mistakes

- **Stepping along the gradient.** That's uphill. Descent subtracts it.
- **Reading the length as the sum of entries.** It's the square root of the sum of squares.
- **Taking a short gradient as "done".** Flat ground can be a plateau or saddle, not a minimum.
- **Assuming the gradient points at the minimum.** Only on a perfectly round bowl.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Gradient descent** steps against this arrow on the loss surface (→ calculus-for-optimization).
- **Gradient norm** is logged in nearly every training run; **gradient clipping** shrinks the
  arrow when it's too long, a common guard against loss spikes.
- **Optimizers** like momentum and Adam exist largely because of the stretched-bowl problem
  (→ momentum-optimizer, → adam-optimizer).
