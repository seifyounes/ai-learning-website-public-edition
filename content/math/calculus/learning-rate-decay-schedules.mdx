task: |
  **Show that decay lets noisy training settle (20 minutes, Python).**

  This trains one weight on the loss `(w − 3)²` with a noisy gradient (as a small mini-batch
  would give), once with a fixed rate and once with the lecture's decay formula, and averages the
  final error over 20 runs:

  ```python
  import random

  def train(schedule, epochs=30, steps_per_epoch=50, seed=0):
      rng = random.Random(seed)
      w = 0.0                                    # loss = (w - 3)^2, minimum at w = 3
      for epoch in range(epochs):
          lr = schedule(epoch)
          for _ in range(steps_per_epoch):
              g = 2 * (w - 3) + rng.gauss(0, 4)  # true gradient + mini-batch noise
              w -= lr * g
      return w

  fixed = lambda epoch: 0.1
  decayed = lambda epoch: 0.1 / (1 + 0.5 * epoch)
  for name, sched in (("fixed", fixed), ("decayed", decayed)):
      errors = [abs(train(sched, seed=s) - 3) for s in range(20)]
      print(f"{name}: average distance from w = 3 over 20 runs: {sum(errors) / 20:.3f}")
  ```

  Then on paper, fill in a table of the learning rate for epochs 1–5 under the lecture's formula
  (α0 = 0.2, decay_rate = 1) and exponential decay (0.95 per epoch).

  0.11; your table reads 0.1, 0.067, 0.05, 0.04, 0.033 and 0.19, 0.181, 0.171, 0.163, 0.155.

**You need:** → mini-batches-and-epochs (why steps are noisy) and → adam-optimizer.

The one idea of this lecture: **shrink the learning rate as training goes on, so the noisy
mini-batch steps that make fast progress early stop throwing the weights around the minimum
later.** The right step size changes as you descend, and a schedule encodes that.

## Why a fixed rate wanders

With small mini-batches (the lecture's examples are 64 or 128), every gradient is a noisy
estimate. Early on that doesn't matter: the true downhill direction dominates. Near the minimum,
the true gradient is small but the noise isn't, so with a fixed step size the weights keep
bouncing around a region instead of settling in. Shrink the step and the bouncing region shrinks
with it.

## The common schedules

An **epoch** is one pass through the data (→ mini-batches-and-epochs). The lecture's main
formula, with `α0` the starting rate:

```text
α = α0 / (1 + decay_rate × epoch)
```

`decay_rate` is one more setting to tune. Alternatives:

- **Exponential:** `α = 0.95^epoch × α0` (any base below 1).
- **Square root:** `α = k / √epoch × α0`, or the same with the mini-batch number.
- **Staircase:** hold the rate, then halve it, hold, halve again.
- **Manual:** when training one model for days, watch the loss and lower the rate by hand.

Many modern recipes, especially for transformers, also start with a short **warmup** in which
the rate climbs from near zero over the first steps, and then decay it. The decay part is the
idea in this lecture.

## Worked example with real numbers

`α0 = 0.2`. Lecture's formula with `decay_rate = 1`, exponential with base 0.95, square root
with `k = 1`, and a staircase that halves every two epochs:

| epoch | 1/(1+e) | 0.95ᵉ | 1/√e | staircase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.100 | 0.190 | 0.200 | 0.200 |
| 2 | 0.067 | 0.181 | 0.141 | 0.200 |
| 3 | 0.050 | 0.171 | 0.115 | 0.100 |
| 4 | 0.040 | 0.163 | 0.100 | 0.100 |
| 5 | 0.033 | 0.155 | 0.089 | 0.050 |

The first formula has already halved the starting rate in epoch 1 and keeps falling fast;
exponential with 0.95 is gentle. Same starting point, very different runs: that's why the decay
setting needs tuning too.

In the task, a weight trained on a noisy gradient for 30 epochs ends on average about 0.44 away
from the minimum with a fixed rate of 0.1, and about 0.11 away when the same rate decays as
`0.1 / (1 + 0.5 × epoch)`.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding a schedule before fixing the base rate.** Andrew Ng puts decay lower on his list:
  get a good fixed rate first.
- **Decaying too fast.** The rate collapses before the model reaches the good region and the
  loss stalls early.
- **Comparing runs with different schedules by their final rate alone.** Compare the whole
  curve.
- **Forgetting the schedule exists.** A loss that suddenly settles at a certain epoch is often
  the scheduler at work, not the data.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Training and fine-tuning configs** nearly always set a scheduler, often warmup plus decay
  (→ fine-tuning-fundamentals).
- **Adam and momentum** take the rate from the schedule each step (→ adam-optimizer).
- **Loss-curve reading:** schedule changes explain many sudden bends in a curve
  (→ calculus-for-optimization).
