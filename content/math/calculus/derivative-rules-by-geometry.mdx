**You need:** → derivative-as-nudge-ratio (a derivative as the ratio a small nudge settles
toward).

The one idea of this lecture: **derivative rules aren't a table to memorise; each one falls out
of asking what a tiny nudge does to the quantity, and then ignoring anything that contains the
nudge squared.** For AI work, a few of these rules are enough to work out the gradient of the
losses you'll meet, starting with squared error.

## The square and the cube

Picture `x²` as a square with side `x`. Widen the side by `dx` and the new area is two thin
strips (`x·dx` each) plus a tiny corner (`dx²`). The corner is a small number squared, so it's
negligible: the change is about `2x·dx`, and the derivative of `x²` is `2x`.

A cube of side `x` works the same way. Nearly all the new volume sits in three thin slabs of
`x²·dx`; the edges and the corner carry `dx²` or `dx³`. So the derivative of `x³` is `3x²`.

The general pattern, the **power rule**: the derivative of `xⁿ` is `n·xⁿ⁻¹`. Expanding
`(x + dx)ⁿ`, there are `n` ways to pick a single `dx`, each giving `xⁿ⁻¹·dx`; every other term
holds `dx²` or more and drops out.

## Two more shapes

- **1/x** is the height of a rectangle of area 1 and width `x`. Widen it and the height must drop
  to keep the area at 1. Working it through (the lecture leaves it to you) gives `−1/x²`, which
  matches the power rule applied to `x⁻¹`.
- **sin θ** is a height on the unit circle. A tiny step `dθ` along the circle raises the height
  by about `cos θ · dθ`, because the tiny triangle is similar to the big one. So the derivative
  of `sin θ` is `cos θ`.

## Worked example with real numbers

**The square.** At `x = 3` with `dx = 0.01`, the exact new area is `3.01² − 9 = 0.0601`. The two
strips give `2 × 3 × 0.01 = 0.06`; the corner gives `0.0001`. The ratio `0.0601 / 0.01 = 6.01`
is within 0.01 of the derivative `2x = 6`, and the gap is the corner.

**1/x.** At `x = 2` the rule says `−1/x² = −0.25`. Check: `1/2.01 = 0.497512`, a change of
`−0.002488` for a nudge of 0.01, a ratio of `−0.2488`. Close, and closer for smaller nudges.

**A toy loss.** `L(w) = (w − 3)² = w² − 6w + 9`. Rule by rule: `w²` gives `2w`, `−6w` gives `−6`,
the constant 9 gives 0. So `L'(w) = 2w − 6`.

| w | L(w) | L'(w) | meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 9 | −6 | raise w |
| 3 | 0 | 0 | bottom |
| 5 | 4 | 4 | lower w |

The sign tells training which way to move; the size grows with the distance from the best value.
That's the whole reason squared error is popular: its gradient is simple and proportional to the
error.

## Common mistakes

- **Keeping the dx² terms.** They vanish in the limit; keeping them makes the rule look inexact.
- **Dropping the linear term.** Only constants differentiate to zero; `−6w` becomes `−6`.
- **Confusing "high" with "rising fast".** `sin θ` is highest at `π/2`, where its rate is 0.
- **Memorising without the picture.** When a rule slips your mind, rebuild it from a nudge.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Squared-error losses** in regression and in many evals: gradient `2 × error`
  (→ calculus-for-optimization).
- **L2 penalties / weight decay:** the gradient `2λw` shrinks big weights fastest
  (→ adam-optimizer touches on how optimizers apply it).
- **Activation functions** such as sigmoid and tanh are built from exponentials; their
  derivatives come from the same kind of rules (→ backprop-chain-rule-calculus).
