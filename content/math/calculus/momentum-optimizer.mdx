task: |
  **Race plain descent against momentum on a stretched bowl (25 minutes, Python).**

  The loss `x² + 100y²` is 100 times steeper in y than in x: a narrow valley.

  ```python
  def descend(lr, beta, steps=1000):
      x, y = 3.0, 1.0
      vx = vy = 0.0
      for t in range(1, steps + 1):
          gx, gy = 2 * x, 200 * y             # gradient of x^2 + 100y^2
          vx = beta * vx + (1 - beta) * gx
          vy = beta * vy + (1 - beta) * gy
          x, y = x - lr * vx, y - lr * vy
          if x * x + 100 * y * y < 1e-4:
              return t                        # steps to reach a tiny loss
      return "never"

  print("plain GD, lr 0.009:", descend(0.009, 0.0))
  print("plain GD, lr 0.011:", descend(0.011, 0.0))
  print("momentum 0.9, lr 0.1:", descend(0.1, 0.9))
  print("momentum 0.9, lr 0.2:", descend(0.2, 0.9))
  ```

  With `beta = 0` the averaging switches off and this is plain gradient descent. After running
  it, print `y` for the first 10 steps of each method and look at the sign pattern.

  Success: plain descent needs about 315 steps at lr 0.009 and never converges at 0.011, while
  momentum gets there in about 103 steps at lr 0.1 (and fails at 0.2, so it raises the limit
  rather than removing it). You can explain the difference using the y-direction gradients.

The one idea of this lecture: **step with a running average of recent gradients instead of the
latest gradient alone.** Components that flip sign from step to step (the bouncing across a
narrow valley) cancel out; components that agree (the direction along the valley) add up. The
result is a straighter, faster path, and it usually works better than plain gradient descent.

## Why plain descent zigzags

On a loss whose contours are long, thin ellipses, the gradient points mostly *across* the
valley. Each step overshoots to the other side, then back. You'd like slow steps across and fast
steps along, but there's one learning rate, and the steep direction sets its ceiling: go higher
and the bouncing grows until training diverges.

## The update

On each iteration, compute `dW` on the current mini-batch, then:

```text
v = β·v + (1 − β)·dW
W = W − α·v
```

(and the same for the biases). `v` is an exponentially weighted average of past gradients, and
`β = 0.9` averages over roughly the last `1 / (1 − β) = 10` of them. Start `v` at zero. The
physics picture: the gradient is an acceleration, `v` a velocity, and `β < 1` is friction that
keeps the ball from speeding up forever.

## Worked example with real numbers

Suppose that, in the valley, the vertical gradient alternates +10, −10, +10, … and the
horizontal gradient is a steady +2. With `β = 0.9` and `v` starting at 0:

| step | vertical v | horizontal v |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00 | 0.20 |
| 2 | −0.10 | 0.38 |
| 3 | 0.91 | 0.54 |
| 4 | −0.18 | 0.69 |
| 10 | −0.34 | 1.30 |
| 20 | −0.46 | 1.76 |

The vertical velocity settles into a small wobble around ±0.5, about 19 times smaller than the
raw ±10. The horizontal velocity climbs toward the full 2. The step now points mostly along the
valley. Notice the slow start: after 10 steps the horizontal velocity is only 1.30 of 2. That
lag is what bias correction removes; for momentum, Andrew Ng notes most people skip it because
the average warms up within about 10 steps.

On the stretched loss `x² + 100y²` from the task, plain descent diverges above a learning rate
of 0.01 and needs about 315 steps at 0.009. Momentum with `β = 0.9` and a learning rate of 0.1
gets there in about 103.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing the two formulas.** Without `(1 − β)`, `v` is about 10 times bigger at `β = 0.9`,
  so the learning rate must shrink to match.
- **Turning β up for "more smoothing".** At 0.99 the velocity remembers about 100 steps and
  overshoots when the landscape turns.
- **Thinking momentum removes the learning-rate limit.** It raises it; too large still diverges.
- **Reading the first few steps as "too slow".** That's the average warming up from zero.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **SGD with momentum** (usually 0.9) is a standard optimizer in vision and many other models.
- **Adam's first moment** is exactly this average with `β1 = 0.9` (→ adam-optimizer).
- **Plateaus and saddle points:** accumulated velocity helps carry training across flat regions
  where the raw gradient is tiny (→ calculus-for-optimization).
