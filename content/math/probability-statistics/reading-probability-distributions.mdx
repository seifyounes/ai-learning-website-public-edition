**You need:** fractions, percentages and reading a bar chart. Nothing else.

A probability distribution tells you how likely each outcome is. You'll meet one every time you
look at a pile of measurements: response times, answer lengths, eval scores. It is also the
object a language model builds at every step before it picks the next token. This lesson reads
one from the ground up: counts in bins first, then a smooth curve, with probability as a share
of the whole.

## From a histogram to a curve

Collect lots of measurements, for example how long your app takes to answer, sort them into
bins and stack them up. That histogram already is a distribution. Tall bins hold the common
values, the thin ends hold the rare ones, and the share of measurements in a bin is your
estimate of the chance the next measurement lands there. For a range that covers several bins,
add their shares.

With more data and narrower bins the bars smooth out into a curve. For a smooth curve,
probability is the **area** under it between two values, which is why a single exact value
(an answer exactly 812.000 ms long) has essentially zero chance. Curves also save you work: a
well-chosen curve gives sensible probabilities even for ranges where you happened to collect
nothing, and for ranges that don't line up with your bin edges.

There are two families:

- **Discrete**: a probability for each separate value. Counts, categories, and tokens.
- **Continuous**: probability spread over a range. Heights, times, scores.

## Bin width changes the picture, not the data

Halve the bin width and every bar holds roughly half as many measurements, so the chart looks
shorter and spikier while the data hasn't changed at all. Two rules keep you honest:

- **Compare shares over the same range, not bar heights.** The share of requests between 300 and
  400 ms is the same whether you drew one 100 ms bar or two 50 ms bars.
- **Narrow bins need more data.** As the lecture shows, more measurements *and* smaller bins
  give a sharper, more accurate picture. Narrow bins on a small sample mostly show noise.

## Worked example: 20 response times

Twenty requests to a hypothetical app took (in ms): 212, 268, 305, 318, 341, 356, 372, 389, 402,
415, 433, 447, 468, 491, 523, 556, 604, 688, 742, 915.

1. **Bin by 100 ms.** 200s: 2 · 300s: 6 · 400s: 6 · 500s: 2 · 600s: 2 · 700s: 1 · 800s: 0 ·
   900s: 1. Divide by 20 for shares: **10%, 30%, 30%, 10%, 10%, 5%, 0%, 5%**.
2. **A range is a sum.** Chance the next request takes 300 to 499 ms: 30% + 30% = **60%**.
   Chance it takes 600 ms or more: 10% + 5% + 0% + 5% = **20%**.
3. **Re-bin by 50 ms.** 300–349: 3 · 350–399: 3 · 400–449: 4 · 450–499: 2. The bars are shorter,
   but 3 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 12 of 20 is still **60%** for 300 to 499 ms.
4. **The empty bin.** Nothing landed in the 800s, but one request took 915 ms, so slow responses
   clearly happen. A curve drawn over the data gives the 800s a small chance instead of a hard
   zero.

Notice the shape: a hump around 300 to 500 ms and a long tail to the right. Response times are
usually lopsided like this, so a symmetric bell curve built from the mean and standard deviation
would understate the slow requests. Pick a curve shaped like your data.

> 💡 **Try it:** take any 20 or more numbers you have to hand (commute times, test-suite
> durations, word counts of your last emails), bin them two ways, and check that the share over
> one range doesn't change.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking for the probability of an exact measurement.** For continuous data, ask about ranges.
- **Reading bar heights across different bin widths.** Narrower bins make every bar shorter;
  compare shares over the same range.
- **Treating an empty bin as impossible.** It means you didn't see one in this sample.
- **Assuming equal bins mean equal chances.** The chance is the share of data in the bin, not
  one over the number of bins.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Next-token sampling.** At each step an LLM outputs a discrete distribution: one probability
  per token, summing to 1, made from raw scores (logits) by softmax. Temperature divides the
  logits first, so low values sharpen the distribution toward the top token and high values
  flatten it; top-p keeps only the top tokens whose probabilities add up to p. Each token is one
  draw, so a different answer on a rerun is sampling doing its job. More in → what-is-an-llm.
- **Latency and length histograms.** Response times, token counts and cost per request are read
  exactly like the worked example, usually with a long right tail.
- **Confidence scores.** Classifier outputs are distributions too, and whether they can be
  trusted is the calibration question in → conditional-probability-counts.
- **Noise in eval scores.** The bell curve that describes it comes next, in
  → normal-distribution-bell-curve.
