  Watch lopsided data produce bell-shaped averages.

  ```python
  import random
  from collections import Counter

  def mean_latency(n):
      # each request time is exponential with mean 2 seconds: lopsided, long right tail
      return sum(random.expovariate(1 / 2) for _ in range(n)) / n

  for n in (1, 5, 50):
      means = [mean_latency(n) for _ in range(2000)]
      bins = Counter(round(m * 2) / 2 for m in means)  # half-second bins
      print(f"n={n}")
      for b in sorted(bins):
          if bins[b] >= 20:
              print(f"{b:5.1f} " + "#" * (bins[b] // 20))
  ```

**You need:** the bell curve and the standard error. → standard-error-vs-deviation first.

Most data in AI work isn't bell-shaped. Pass/fail scores are two spikes at 0 and 1. Response
times and costs bunch up at the low end with a long tail of slow or expensive requests. Yet the
eval bands in this track treat averages as bell curves. The **central limit theorem** is why
that's allowed: averages of many independent values are close to normally distributed, whatever
the shape of the values themselves.

## What the theorem says

Take any distribution that has a mean. Draw a sample of n values, average them, and repeat many
times. The averages:

- pile up in a **bell curve**,
- centred on the true mean,
- with a width (the standard error) of σ ÷ √n.

You don't need to know where the data came from. That's what makes confidence intervals and tests
of means work in practice.

## Worked example: a pass rate and a latency

**Pass rate.** Each item scores 1 (pass) or 0 (fail), with a true pass rate of 0.8. A single item
is wildly non-normal. Its standard deviation is √(0.8 × 0.2) = 0.4. Average 100 items and the
pass rate follows a bell curve centred on 0.8 with standard error 0.4 ÷ √100 = **0.04**. So about
95% of 100-item evals land between **72% and 88%**.

**Latency.** Suppose single request times are lopsided with a mean of 2 s and a standard deviation
of 2 s (typical of an exponential shape). The mean of 50 requests has standard error
2 ÷ √50 ≈ **0.28 s**, so it usually lands between about 1.4 and 2.6 s, in a roughly symmetric hump.
Single requests still run up to 6 s, 8 s or more; the theorem says nothing about them.

## Where it breaks down

- **Single values.** The theorem is about averages. Tails and worst cases need their own
  measurement.
- **Dependent samples.** Ten turns from one conversation aren't ten independent values. The
  bell curve still forms, but it's wider than s ÷ √n says.
- **Heavy tails.** With rare, huge values, averages need far more than 30 samples to look normal.
  "n ≥ 30" is a rule of thumb, not a law.
- **No mean at all.** A few theoretical distributions have no mean, but you're unlikely to meet
  one in practice.

## Common mistakes

- **Rejecting a band because the raw data isn't normal.** The band is about the average.
- **Using the average's band to predict single outcomes.** Use percentiles for the user's view.
- **Counting related items as independent.** Group by conversation, user or document first.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Error bars on every eval score and benchmark (→ evaluation-metrics), A/B tests on averages like
conversion or satisfaction, and the averaged gradients of mini-batch training, which behave like
noisy estimates of the full-data gradient. When no formula fits your metric, resampling does the
same job, as → bootstrapping-eval-metrics shows next.
