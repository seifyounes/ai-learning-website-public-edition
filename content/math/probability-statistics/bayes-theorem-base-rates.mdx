task: |
  Build a Bayes calculator and watch the base rate drive the result.

  1. In a spreadsheet, put the base rate in B1 (start with 0.02), the detector's catch rate in B2
     (0.95) and its false-alarm rate in B3 (0.05).
  2. In B5, the share of flags that are real:
     `=B2*B1/(B2*B1+B3*(1-B1))`
  3. Try base rates of 0.005, 0.02, 0.10 and 0.30 and write down B5 each time.
  4. Set the base rate back to 0.02 and find the false-alarm rate needed for half of all flags to
     be real.

  ```python
  def share_real(base, catch=0.95, false_alarm=0.05):
      return catch * base / (catch * base + false_alarm * (1 - base))

  for base in (0.005, 0.02, 0.10, 0.30):
      print(base, round(share_real(base), 3))
  ```

  Success: four shares of real flags, one per base rate, and the false-alarm rate that gets you to
  50% at a 2% base rate.

**You need:** conditional probability. → conditional-probability-counts first.

**Bayes' theorem** reverses a conditional probability. A detector's maker tells you how often it
flags real problems, P(flag | problem). What you need is the reverse: given a flag, how likely is a
real problem, P(problem | flag)? Bayes connects the two, and the piece people forget is the **base
rate**: how common the problem is to begin with. When the problem is rare, even an accurate
detector produces mostly false alarms.

## The theorem

From the last lesson, both conditionals share the same joint probability:

- P(A and B) = P(A | B) × P(B)
- P(A and B) = P(B | A) × P(A)

Set them equal and divide:

**P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) ÷ P(B)**

With a full table of counts, you don't need it: just divide within the row. Its value shows up
when you only have partial information, like a detector's catch rate and a guess at how common the
problem is. The answer is then only as good as that guess.

## Worked example: a hallucination detector

The detector catches 95% of hallucinations and wrongly flags 5% of clean answers. Hallucinations
are 2% of traffic. Work in counts, not formulas:

1. **Imagine 10,000 answers.** 200 are hallucinations; 9,800 are clean.
2. **True flags:** 95% of 200 = 190.
3. **False flags:** 5% of 9,800 = 490.
4. **Share of flags that are real:** 190 ÷ (190 + 490) ≈ **28%**.

The formula gives the same thing: 0.95 × 0.02 ÷ (0.95 × 0.02 + 0.05 × 0.98) = 0.019 ÷ 0.068 ≈ 0.28.

Roughly seven in ten flags are false alarms, from a "95% accurate" tool. That's not a broken
detector; it's arithmetic. Your options:

- **A stricter threshold**, which lowers false alarms at the cost of some missed cases.
- **A second-stage check on flagged items.** The 28% becomes the new base rate, and a second
  independent check with the same rates lifts it to about 88%.
- **A review queue sized for the real flag volume**, not for the number of real problems.

## The base rate moves everything

| Base rate | Share of flags real |
|---|---|
| 0.5% | about 9% |
| 2% | about 28% |
| 10% | about 68% |
| 30% | about 89% |

Same detector, four different experiences. That's why a filter that works well in one product can
flood another with false alarms, and why precision must be re-measured when traffic changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the catch rate as the share of real flags.** They're reverse conditionals.
- **Ignoring the base rate.** Ask how common the problem is before trusting any flag.
- **Muting a noisy tool.** Tune the threshold or add a second check instead.
- **Treating a guessed base rate as known.** Estimate it from a random, hand-labelled sample.
