applyIt: |
  **Before you trust any quality number, name the population it is supposed to describe and ask
  whether your sample came from it.** Take one number you or your team quote: an eval pass rate,
  a "users like it" rating, a vendor's benchmark score.

  1. Write one sentence: "This is an estimate of [the true rate] for [which inputs, which users,
     which time period]."
  2. Write where the sample actually came from and how many items it has.
  3. List one way the sample differs from the population (older traffic, one language, only easy
     cases, hand-picked examples).
  4. Decide: keep the number, re-sample, or quote it with the caveat.

  **If you build software:** sample eval items at random from recent real inputs, and record the
  date range next to the score. **If you don't code:** when a tool or vendor quotes a score, ask
  what data it was measured on before comparing it with your own experience.

  Success: one quality number now has a written population, a sample description and a verdict.

There are two kinds of number in every measurement. There is the **true value** for everything
you care about, which you almost never see, and there is the **estimate** you compute from the
data you managed to collect. An eval score, a satisfaction rating and a benchmark result are all
estimates. Keeping the two apart is the first habit of anyone who evaluates AI systems.

## Population and parameters

The **population** is the full set you want to describe: every question your chatbot will ever
get, every document next quarter, every user of a feature. Its true mean and standard deviation
are its **parameters**, usually written with Greek letters (μ, σ). For a pass/fail check the
parameter is the true pass rate.

You can't measure the whole population: future traffic doesn't exist yet, and grading everything
costs too much. If you come from machine learning, the mapping is direct: your data is the
sample, and the population is what the model is supposed to work on.

## Samples and estimates

So you measure a **sample** and compute statistics from it: a sample mean (x̄), a sample
standard deviation (s), a pass rate p̂. These are **estimates** of the parameters. The
different notation is a reminder that they are guesses.

Two facts follow:

1. **Different samples give different estimates.** Grade 50 random conversations, then another
   50, and the two pass rates won't match even though nothing changed.
2. **Bigger samples give estimates closer to the truth.** The rest of this track measures how
   close: standard error, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests.

## Worked example: three samples of one prompt

Suppose a prompt's true pass rate on your traffic is 85% (in real life you wouldn't know this).
You draw three random samples:

| Sample size | Passes | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 16 | 80% |
| 100 | 87 | 87% |
| 400 | 338 | 84.5% |

All three are honest estimates of the same 85%. The 20-item one is 5 points off, which is
ordinary for so few items. The 400-item one is half a point off. If you'd compared a new prompt
against the 20-item result, you could have "found" a 5-point improvement that was only sampling.

## The sample must come from the right population

Size isn't everything. An eval set built from hand-picked hard cases, from one month's traffic or
from one language estimates the pass rate **for that population**. When real traffic shifts (new
users, new kinds of question), the score stops describing it, usually in the flattering
direction. This is **distribution shift**, and it's a common reason quality drops while a
dashboard stays green.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting an estimate as a fact.** "The bot is 87% accurate" should be "87% on these 100
  examples from March".
- **Reading disagreement between two samples as a mistake.** Some disagreement is guaranteed.
- **Hand-picking the sample.** Interesting cases make good tests but a biased estimate of
  overall quality. Keep them separate from a random sample.
- **Never refreshing the eval set.** Re-sample from current traffic on a schedule.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Every eval and benchmark (→ evaluation-metrics), every A/B test of prompts or models, and every
claim from a leaderboard. Training data is itself a sample: a model learns the patterns in the
data it saw, which is why a mismatch between training data and real use hurts performance. Next,
→ mean-variance-standard-deviation shows how to compute the estimates themselves.
