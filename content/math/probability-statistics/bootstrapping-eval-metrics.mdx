  **When an eval reports a metric without error bars, bootstrap it before you act on it.** Take

task: |
  Bootstrap the difference between two prompts scored on the same items.

  ```python
  import random

  # 1 = pass, 0 = fail, for prompts A and B on the same 40 items (replace with your own)
  a = [1]*29 + [0]*11
  b = [1]*33 + [0]*7
  random.seed(0)
  random.shuffle(a); random.shuffle(b)

  n = len(a)
  diffs = []
  for _ in range(10_000):
      idx = [random.randrange(n) for _ in range(n)]  # resample items, keep pairs
      diffs.append(sum(b[i] - a[i] for i in idx) / n)
  diffs.sort()
  print("observed gap:", (sum(b) - sum(a)) / n)
  print("95% interval:", diffs[250], "to", diffs[9750])
  ```

  1. Run it as is, then replace `a` and `b` with real pass/fail results from two versions of one
     of your prompts (at least 20 items, in the same item order).
  2. Read the interval. If it includes 0, you can't tell the prompts apart yet.
  3. Change the metric: compute a median of scores or an F1 inside the loop instead of the mean
     difference. Nothing else in the method changes.

  **No-code path:** paste the two columns of results into a chat assistant that runs code and ask
  for exactly this paired bootstrap, with the interval for B minus A.

**You need:** means and standard errors. → central-limit-theorem first.

A metric computed once is one draw. You'd like to know how much it would move if you collected a
fresh eval set, but you can't afford to keep doing that. **Bootstrapping** gets the answer from
the data you already have: resample it many times and watch how much the metric wobbles. It
works for any metric, which matters in AI work, where many metrics have no textbook formula for
their standard error.

## The method in four steps

1. **Make a bootstrapped dataset.** Draw n rows at random from your n rows, *with replacement*:
   some rows appear twice, some not at all. Keep the same size as the original.
2. **Calculate the metric** on it: a mean, a median, F1, a win rate.
3. **Record** the result.
4. **Repeat** thousands of times.

The recorded results form a histogram of "what the metric might have been". Its standard
deviation is the **standard error**, and the range covering the middle 95% is a **95%
confidence interval** (the topic of → confidence-intervals-for-evals).

## Worked example: eight judge scores

A judge rates eight answers: 2, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9. The mean is **6.25**.

- One bootstrapped dataset: 5, 5, 8, 9, 9, 2, 6, 8 → mean **6.5**.
- Another: 2, 2, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9 → mean **5.75**.

Repeat 100,000 times (a computer takes about a second) and the means have a standard deviation of
about **0.74**: that's the standard error. The middle 95% runs from about **4.75 to 7.6**. With
only eight answers, a "6.25 average" is compatible with anything from mediocre to good.

The same loop with the median instead of the mean gives an interval of about 5 to 8, with no new
formula needed.

## Two rules for AI evals

- **Resample the unit that's independent.** If an eval has many turns per conversation or many
  questions per document, resample conversations or documents, not single rows. Otherwise the
  interval comes out too narrow.
- **Pair comparisons.** To compare prompt A and B on the same items, resample item indices once
  per round and compute B minus A on that resample. Items that are hard for both cancel out, and
  the interval for the difference is much tighter than two separate intervals suggest.

## Common mistakes

- **Resampling without replacement.** That just shuffles the data and every "resample" has the
  same mean.
- **Changing the size.** Smaller resamples exaggerate the wobble; larger ones hide it.
- **Treating the bootstrap as new data.** It measures uncertainty in your sample; it can't fix a
  sample drawn from the wrong population (→ probability-statistics-for-ai).
- **Too few rounds.** Use thousands; a hundred rounds gives a jumpy interval.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Error bars on eval leaderboards and research results, intervals on F1, BLEU-style scores and
judge win rates (→ evaluation-metrics), and paired comparisons of prompts or models. The same
resampling idea appears in bagging, where many models are trained on bootstrapped copies of the
training data and their predictions are combined, as random forests do.
