**You need:** squares and square roots. → probability-statistics-for-ai first.

Two numbers summarize most data you'll report: where the values sit (the **mean**) and how much
they spread out (the **standard deviation**). For AI work the spread matters as much as the
average, because users never see an average. They each get a single run, and the standard
deviation tells you how far that run can land from the mean.

## The mean

Add the values, divide by how many there are. With the whole population it's the true mean (μ);
with a sample it's the sample mean (x̄), an estimate that moves with the sample.

## Variance: average squared distance

For each value, take its distance from the mean and **square it**. Squaring stops positive and
negative distances from cancelling and weighs big misses more heavily. Averaging those squares
gives the **variance**.

The catch is units: if the values are percentage points, the variance is in "points squared",
which you can't compare with anything directly.

## Standard deviation: back to real units

Take the square root of the variance and you're back in the data's own units. That's the
**standard deviation**, the width of the bell curve from → normal-distribution-bell-curve.

## Why a sample divides by n − 1

When all you have is a sample, you measure distances from the sample mean, and the sample mean
is computed from those same values, so it sits right in their middle. The distances come out
smaller than distances to the true mean would be, and the variance comes out too small. Dividing
by **n − 1** instead of n corrects that bias. With 5 values the difference is about 25% in
variance; with 1,000 it's negligible.

| Formula | Spreadsheet | Python |
|---|---|---|
| Sample (÷ n − 1) | `STDEV.S` | `statistics.stdev`, `np.std(x, ddof=1)` |
| Population (÷ n) | `STDEV.P` | `statistics.pstdev`, `np.std(x)` |

pandas' `.std()` uses n − 1 by default and NumPy's `np.std` uses n, so the same data can report
two different spreads.

## Worked example: five reruns of an eval

Scores: 72, 75, 78, 80, 85.

1. **Mean:** 390 ÷ 5 = **78**.
2. **Differences from 78:** −6, −3, 0, 2, 7.
3. **Squares:** 36, 9, 0, 4, 49. Sum = **98**.
4. **Sample variance:** 98 ÷ 4 = **24.5**. Sample SD = √24.5 ≈ **4.95** points.
5. **Population formula, for comparison:** 98 ÷ 5 = 19.6, SD ≈ 4.43.

Report it as "78 ± 5 over 5 runs". Anyone reading that knows a single run could easily land at
73 or 83, so a 2-point difference between two prompts at this spread means little.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting a mean without a spread.** "78%" and "78 ± 5%" lead to different decisions.
- **Using the population formula on a sample.** It makes small samples look more consistent than
  they are.
- **Reading variance as if it were in data units.** Take the square root first.
- **Ignoring outliers.** One extreme value inflates the SD a lot because distances are squared.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Layer normalization and batch normalization inside neural networks compute a mean and variance
of activations and rescale them. Standardizing input features subtracts the mean and divides by
the standard deviation. The Adam optimizer keeps running averages of gradients and of their
squares. And every honest eval report gives "mean ± SD over n runs". Next,
→ standard-error-vs-deviation separates the spread of the data from the uncertainty of the mean.
