**You need:** averages and square roots. → reading-probability-distributions first.

The normal distribution is the bell curve: values pile up around a centre and thin out evenly
on both sides. It matters for AI work because it describes how **averages** wobble, and an eval
score, a pass rate or a mean latency is an average. Learn to read one bell curve and you can
tell a real change from ordinary noise.

## Two numbers describe the whole curve

- **The mean (μ)** puts the peak. The most common values sit near it.
- **The standard deviation (σ)** sets the width. Small σ: tall and narrow, values packed close
  to the mean. Large σ: short and wide.

The total area under the curve is always 1, so a narrow curve has to be taller. Two groups can
share the same mean and still look completely different: newborn heights cluster tightly,
adult heights spread out more.

## The 68-95-99.7 rule

For any normal curve, roughly:

- **68%** of values fall within 1 σ of the mean,
- **95%** within 2 σ,
- **99.7%** within 3 σ.

That turns "is this surprising?" into arithmetic. Measure how many standard deviations a value
sits from the mean, which is called its **z-score**: z = (value − mean) ÷ σ. Beyond 2 is
unusual, beyond 3 is rare.

## Worked example: is this rerun a breakthrough?

You rerun the same eval 20 times with sampling switched on. Scores average **82.0%** with a
standard deviation of **1.5 points**.

1. **The 95% band:** 82.0 ± 2 × 1.5 = **79% to 85%**. That's the normal wobble.
2. **A run at 84%:** z = (84 − 82) ÷ 1.5 ≈ 1.3. Inside the band. Nothing happened.
3. **A run at 86%:** z = (86 − 82) ÷ 1.5 ≈ 2.7. Outside the band. Worth asking what changed
   (the input set, the model version, a config) before claiming a win.
4. **A run at 77%:** z ≈ −3.3. Rare. Something likely broke.

The same logic works for a latency, a cost per request, or a judge's score, as long as the
values look roughly bell-shaped.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing only means.** Two prompts with the same average can differ hugely in spread, and
  users get single draws, not averages.
- **Using a fixed threshold.** "5 points is noise" is wrong on a narrow curve and too strict on a
  wide one. Measure σ.
- **Assuming everything is normal.** Response lengths, latencies and costs often have a long
  right tail. Averages of them still tend toward a bell, as → central-limit-theorem shows, but
  single values may not.
- **Expecting zero false alarms.** A 2-σ alert fires on about 1 in 20 ordinary readings.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Noise bands around eval scores (→ standard-error-vs-deviation and
→ confidence-intervals-for-evals). Network weights are often initialized by drawing small
random numbers from a normal distribution, and diffusion image models are trained by adding
Gaussian (normal) noise to images and learning to remove it. Standardizing features to z-scores
before training is the same subtraction and division as above.
