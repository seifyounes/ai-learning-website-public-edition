A **conditional probability** is the probability of something *given* that you already know
something else. Almost every practical question about an AI system has that shape. How likely is a
flagged message to be harmful? How often is the model right when it says it's sure? Answering
those correctly mostly comes down to dividing by the right total.

## Work from a table of counts

Sort cases by two yes/no questions and count each combination. That's a **contingency table**.
Then:

- an **unconditional** probability divides a count by everyone;
- a **conditional** probability divides by only the row or column you know you're in.

In symbols, P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B): the share of the B group that is also A. Read the bar
"|" as "given".

## Direction matters

P(A | B) and P(B | A) share the same top number (the people who are both) but divide by different
totals, so they can be wildly different. In AI work this is the difference between two metrics:

- **Precision** = P(actually positive | flagged): how much to trust a flag.
- **Recall** = P(flagged | actually positive): how much of the problem gets caught.

## Worked example: a triage bot

1,000 support tickets, 100 truly urgent. The bot flags 150 tickets.

| | Urgent | Not urgent | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flagged | 90 | 60 | 150 |
| Not flagged | 10 | 840 | 850 |
| Total | 100 | 900 | 1,000 |

- **P(urgent)** = 100 ÷ 1,000 = **10%**.
- **P(flagged | urgent)** = 90 ÷ 100 = **90%** (recall).
- **P(urgent | flagged)** = 90 ÷ 150 = **60%** (precision).

"Catches 90% of urgent tickets" and "only 60% of flags are urgent" are both true. Which one matters
depends on whether a missed urgent ticket or a wasted look costs you more.

## Calibration: does 80% mean 80%?

A model or judge is **calibrated** if, among all the times it says "80% confident", it's right
about 80% of the time. That's a conditional probability: P(correct | says 80%). LLM judges and
stated confidence are often miscalibrated, usually overconfident. To check: group verdicts by
stated confidence, count how many in each group were right, and compare.

## Common mistakes

- **Flipping the conditional.** "Catches 98% of spam" is not "98% of flags are spam".
- **Dividing by everyone.** A conditional divides by the group you know you're in.
- **Trusting "I'm 90% sure".** Check it against outcomes before treating it as a probability.
- **Calling a non-zero rate a cause.** If conditioning doesn't change the rate, the events are
  independent.

## Where you'll see it in AI

Precision and recall for every classifier, filter and retrieval step (→ evaluation-metrics),
calibration of model and judge confidence, and next-token probabilities, which are conditional on
all the text so far (→ reading-probability-distributions). Reversing a conditional properly, when
you don't have the full table, is what → bayes-theorem-base-rates does next.
