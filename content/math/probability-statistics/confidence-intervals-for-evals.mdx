task: |
  Turn three eval results into intervals and compare them correctly.

  Results: prompt A passed 70 of 100, prompt B 82 of 100, prompt C 90 of 100 (or use three
  results of your own).

  1. For each, compute SE = √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n) and the 95% interval p ± 2 SE.
  2. Draw the three intervals as horizontal bars on paper or in a spreadsheet chart.
  3. For each pair, note whether the bars overlap.
  4. For the overlapping pair, compute the interval of the difference: gap ± 2 × √(SE₁² + SE₂²).
     Does it include 0?

  ```python
  from math import sqrt
  res = {"A": (70, 100), "B": (82, 100), "C": (90, 100)}
  se = {k: sqrt((x / n) * (1 - x / n) / n) for k, (x, n) in res.items()}
  for k, (x, n) in res.items():
      p = x / n
      print(k, round(p - 2 * se[k], 3), round(p + 2 * se[k], 3))
  gap = 0.90 - 0.82
  half = 2 * sqrt(se["B"] ** 2 + se["C"] ** 2)
  print("C - B:", round(gap - half, 3), "to", round(gap + half, 3))
  ```

  Success: three intervals, one overlap verdict per pair, and the difference interval for B and C.

**You need:** standard errors and bootstrapping. → bootstrapping-eval-metrics first.

An eval score on its own invites over-reading: 84% looks better than 82% even when the two are
indistinguishable. A **confidence interval** puts the uncertainty on the page, as a range of
values the true score could reasonably take. It is the cheapest change you can make to how you
report AI results, and it prevents most false wins.

## What a 95% interval is

Bootstrap your eval data many times and compute the metric each time. The range covering the
middle 95% of those results is a **95% confidence interval**. For a mean or a pass rate there's a
shortcut that gives nearly the same thing: **estimate ± 2 standard errors**.

The careful reading: if you repeated the whole eval many times and built an interval each time,
about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. A 99% interval is wider, because it
has to catch the truth more often. Higher confidence doesn't buy precision; only more data does.

## Intervals as a visual test

Values outside a 95% interval are implausible at the 5% level, so the interval doubles as a test
you can read off a chart:

- **Against a target.** If your pass-rate interval is 74% to 90%, you can't claim you've met an
  80% target. If it's 83% to 91%, you can.
- **Two intervals that don't overlap.** The difference is significant.
- **Two intervals that overlap.** Undecided. They may still differ, so compute the interval of the
  difference itself.

## Worked example: three prompts, 100 examples each

| Prompt | Passed | SE | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 70 | 4.6 | 61% to 79% |
| B | 82 | 3.8 | 74% to 90% |
| C | 90 | 3.0 | 84% to 96% |

- **A vs C:** no overlap. C is better, significantly.
- **B vs C:** overlap from 84% to 90%. Compute the difference: 8 points, with standard error
  √(3.8² + 3.0²) ≈ 4.9, so its interval is about −2 to +18. It includes 0: not settled.
- **A vs B:** overlap, and the difference (12 ± 12) just touches 0. Borderline.

If B and C had been run on the **same** 100 examples, a paired interval (→ bootstrapping-eval-metrics)
would usually be much narrower and could settle the question without more data.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting the point estimate alone.** Always give the range and n.
- **Reading overlap as "no difference".** Overlap means undecided, not equal.
- **Asking for 99% to get "better" numbers.** You get wider ones.
- **Building an interval on the wrong population.** A tight interval on old or hand-picked data
  is still about old or hand-picked data (→ probability-statistics-for-ai).

## Where you'll see it in AI

Error bars on benchmark charts and model cards, eval reports (→ evaluation-metrics), A/B tests of
prompts and product changes, and any dashboard of rates over time. The formal version of "does
the interval include zero?" is the hypothesis test, next in → hypothesis-testing-null.
