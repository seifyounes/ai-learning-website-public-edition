Every model needs a number for how wrong it is. **Least squares is the simplest one: measure
each miss, square it, add them up, and choose the line that makes the total smallest.** That
total is a *loss function*, and the move of picking parameters to minimise a loss is the same
one used to train every neural network, LLMs included.

## Residuals and the sum of squares

A **residual** is the real value minus the predicted value. Some are positive, some negative,
so adding them directly lets misses cancel. Squaring fixes that and makes the maths smooth.
The total, the **sum of squared residuals (SSR)**, is the loss. Divide it by the number of
points and you get the **mean squared error (MSE)**, the same quantity under the name most ML
libraries use.

A line is y = intercept + slope × x. Least squares chooses the two numbers that give the
lowest SSR. At that point the SSR curve is flat, so its derivative is zero, which gives a
closed-form answer:

```text
slope = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)²
intercept = ȳ − slope × x̄
```

## Worked example

Four points: (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4). Averages: x̄ = 2.5, ȳ = 3.5.

- **Flat line y = 3.5.** Residuals −1.5, −0.5, 1.5, 0.5. SSR = 2.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 =
  **5.0**.
- **Best line.** Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) = 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.75 + 0.75 = 4. Σ(x − x̄)² = 5. Slope =
  4 / 5 = **0.8**; intercept = 3.5 − 0.8 × 2.5 = **1.5**.
- **Its predictions:** 2.3, 3.1, 3.9, 4.7. Residuals −0.3, −0.1, 1.1, −0.7. SSR = 0.09 + 0.01 +
  1.21 + 0.49 = **1.8**.

The line removes (5.0 − 1.8) / 5.0 = **64%** of the flat line's error. That ratio is **R²**. Try
slope 1.0 through the same average point and the SSR rises to 2.0; the formula's slope really
is the bottom.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading SSR on its own.** It grows with the number of points and the units of y. Compare it
  with the flat-line baseline or report MSE or R².
- **Forgetting outliers.** Squaring makes one large miss dominate; check extreme points before
  trusting the line.
- **Extrapolating.** The line describes the range of x you fitted. Far outside it, the trend may
  not hold.
- **Scoring on the fitting data.** A low SSR on the points you fitted says little about new
  points; the held-out habit from → machine-learning-fundamentals still applies.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Regression losses.** MSE is the standard loss whenever a model predicts a number: prices,
  durations, scores.
- **Training in general.** Classifiers and LLMs use a different loss (cross-entropy, from
  → logistic-regression onward), but the job is the same: choose parameters that minimise it.
  Most models have no closed-form answer, so they walk downhill instead
  (→ fitting-by-gradient-descent).
- **Linear probes.** Fitting a simple linear model on top of embeddings is a standard way to test
  what they capture (→ embeddings-vector-databases).
