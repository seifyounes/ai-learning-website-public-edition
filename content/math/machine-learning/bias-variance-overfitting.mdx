applyIt: |
  **Every "the model is bad" conversation should start by deciding which side of the trade-off
  you're on.** Two numbers settle it: the error on training data and the error on held-out data.
  Find a model, prompt or forecast in your own work and write both numbers down before
  proposing a fix.

  - **Both errors high and close together:** bias. Give the model more to work with: more
    flexible model, better input features, longer training.
  - **Training error low, held-out error much higher:** variance. Give it less freedom or more
    evidence: more data, regularization, a simpler model, stopping training earlier.
  - **Both low and close:** you're done tuning; spend the time on better test data instead.

  same check works for a spreadsheet forecast: fit it on last year, score it on this year, and

task: |
  Watch overfitting happen in numbers. Run this in a free Google Colab notebook (or any Python
  with NumPy). It fits polynomials of rising flexibility to 15 noisy training points and scores
  each on 5 held-out points:

  ```python
  import warnings
  import numpy as np
  warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")  # degree 12 triggers a fit warning; that's part of the lesson

  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, 20))
  y = 3 * np.sqrt(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.8, 20)  # a curved truth plus noise
  test = np.arange(20) % 4 == 0                 # every fourth point is held out
  train = ~test

  for degree in [1, 2, 5, 12]:
      coefs = np.polyfit(x[train], y[train], degree)
      mse = lambda m: np.mean((np.polyval(coefs, x[m]) - y[m]) ** 2)
      print(f"degree {degree:2}: train {mse(train):.2f}   test {mse(test):.2f}")
  ```

  1. Write the four rows in your notes. Training error should fall every time; test error should
     fall, then explode at degree 12.
  2. Change the noise from `0.8` to `0.2` and rerun. Which degree wins now, and why does less
     noise make flexibility less dangerous?
  3. Change every `20` to `200` (three places) and rerun. Does degree 12 still blow up? Your
     exact numbers may differ a little from a classmate's; the pattern is what matters.

The first lesson's straight line beat the wiggly curve on new data. This lesson explains why:
**a model can fail by being too rigid (bias) or too sensitive to its particular training data
(variance), and you fix those two failures in opposite ways.** Get the diagnosis wrong and you
spend a week on the cure that makes it worse.

## Two ways to be wrong

**Bias** is a model's built-in inability to represent the true pattern. A straight line through
data that curves will always miss, no matter how carefully you fit it. High bias shows up as
**underfitting**: poor on the training data, poor on new data, with the two errors close.

**Variance** is how much a model's fit changes when you change the data. A curve flexible
enough to pass through every training point also passes through its noise, so a different
sample would give a very different curve. High variance shows up as **overfitting**: great on
training data, much worse on new data.

The goal is low bias and low variance at once: a model flexible enough for the real pattern
and stable enough to ignore the noise.

## Worked example: three points, two models

Training data: (1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2). Testing data: (1.5, 2), (2.5, 2.5), (4, 3).

**The straight line.** Least squares gives y = 1 + 0.5x. Its training predictions are 1.5, 2
and 2.5, so the squared errors are 0.25 + 1 + 0.25 = **1.5**. On the testing points it predicts
1.75, 2.25 and 3: squared errors 0.0625 + 0.0625 + 0 = **0.125**.

**The flexible curve.** A parabola through all three training points,
y = −4 + 6.5x − 1.5x², has training error **0**. On the testing points it predicts 2.375,
2.875 and −2. The last one is 5 away from the real 3, so its testing error is about
0.14 + 0.14 + 25 ≈ **25.3**.

On training data the curve wins, 0 against 1.5. On testing data the line wins by a factor of
about 200. Nothing about the curve looked wrong until it met a point it hadn't seen.

## Reading the two numbers

| Training error | Held-out error | Diagnosis | Try |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | High, close to training | Bias | More flexible model, better features, longer training |
| Low | Much higher | Variance | More data, regularization, simpler model, early stopping |
| Low | Low, close to training | Fine | Better test data, then ship |

The three classic variance cures each get a lesson in this track: regularization
(→ ridge-regularization), bagging (→ random-forests-bagging) and boosting, which works on
bias as well (→ gradient-boosting-trees).

## Common mistakes

- **Diagnosing from one number.** "Error is 15%" says nothing about bias or variance; you need
  the training error beside it.
- **Adding data to a biased model.** If it can't fit the data it has, more of the same data
  won't help much.
- **Making the model bigger to fix overfitting.** Extra flexibility usually widens the gap.
- **Judging on training loss alone.** It falls almost every epoch; the validation curve is the
  one that tells you when to stop.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Fine-tuning** is where most practitioners first meet overfitting: training loss falls,
  validation loss turns up, and the checkpoint before the turn is the one to keep
  (→ fine-tuning-fundamentals).
- **Neural networks** are very flexible models, so they lean on the variance cures: more data,
  dropout, weight decay and early stopping (→ neural-networks).
- **Prompt engineering** can overfit too: a prompt tweaked until it passes every case in a
  small eval set may fail on the next real input. Keep a held-out set of cases you don't tune
  against (→ evals-and-testing).
