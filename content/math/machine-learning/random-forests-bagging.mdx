task: |
  Measure what bagging buys you. In a free Google Colab notebook (scikit-learn is preinstalled):

  ```python
  import numpy as np
  from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
  from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
  from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
  from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

  X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)

  # 1. How much of the data does one bootstrap sample leave out?
  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  n = len(y)
  picked = rng.integers(0, n, size=n)          # sample n rows with replacement
  print("left out:", round(1 - len(set(picked)) / n, 3))

  # 2. One tree versus a forest, 10-fold cross-validation
  for name, model in [("one tree", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0)),
                      ("forest", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0))]:
      s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=10)
      print(f"{name}: mean {s.mean():.3f}  min {s.min():.3f}  max {s.max():.3f}")

  # 3. The out-of-bag score, from a single fit
  forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, oob_score=True, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
  print("out-of-bag accuracy:", round(forest.oob_score_, 3))
  ```

  1. Is the left-out share close to the "about a third" from the lecture?
  2. How do the forest's mean and min-to-max spread compare with the single tree's?
  3. Is the out-of-bag accuracy close to the forest's cross-validated mean?
  4. Add `max_features=1` and then `max_features=None` (all columns) to the forest and rerun
     part 2. Which setting does best, and why might "all columns" make the trees too alike?

One decision tree is high-variance: change a few training rows and the splits change, so the
predictions change. **A random forest grows hundreds of trees, each on a resampled copy of the
data and with random columns at each split, and lets them vote; their individual mistakes
partly cancel.** It's often a strong model on tabular data with very little tuning.

## Building the forest

1. **Bootstrap.** Draw as many rows as the dataset has, at random, *with replacement*. Some rows
   appear more than once; about 37% don't appear at all.
2. **Grow a tree** on that sample, but at each split consider only a random subset of the columns
   (a common start is the square root of the number of columns).
3. **Repeat** hundreds of times.
4. **Predict** by majority vote (classification) or average (regression).

Bootstrapping plus aggregating is **bagging**. The random columns are the forest's extra
ingredient: they stop every tree from making the same first split.

## Worked example: why votes help, and why variety matters

Suppose each tree, on its own, is right 60% of the time on some kind of case, and the trees'
mistakes were fully independent. A majority vote is then right when more than half the trees
are:

| Trees voting | Majority right |
|---|---|
| 1 | 60% |
| 11 | 75% |
| 101 | 98% |

(Those are binomial probabilities: the chance that at least 6 of 11, or 51 of 101, are right.)

Real trees are trained on overlapping data, so their mistakes are correlated and the gain is
smaller. That's exactly why the forest injects randomness: **the less alike the trees, the more
the vote helps.** If every tree were identical, 101 votes would be worth one.

**The out-of-bag check.** With a few hundred trees, every row is left out of roughly a third of
them. Let only those trees vote on it, and you get a prediction for every row from models that
never saw it. The share wrong is the **out-of-bag error**: a held-out score at no extra cost.

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting a forest to explain itself.** Hundreds of trees can't be read like one. Feature
  importances help, but they are rankings, not reasons.
- **Too few trees.** With a handful of trees the vote is noisy; add trees until the out-of-bag
  error levels off.
- **Duplicate records.** If the same record appears twice in the data, a tree can train on one
  copy and be "tested" on the other, so the out-of-bag score flatters it. Dedupe first.
- **Treating out-of-bag as a final test.** If you tuned settings on it, keep a separate test set
  for the final number.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Tabular baselines.** A random forest is a common first serious model on a table, before
  → gradient-boosting-trees.
- **Ensembles of LLM calls.** Sampling several answers and taking the majority (often called
  self-consistency) uses the same logic: varied predictors, one vote.
- **Bootstrapping eval scores.** The same resampling trick puts a confidence band around an eval
  score (→ bootstrapping-eval-metrics).
