task: |
  Compute per-class rates for a three-class model.

  A hypothetical support-ticket router was tested on 150 tickets. Rows are the true queue,
  columns the predicted queue (order: billing, technical, account):

  ```python
  labels = ["billing", "technical", "account"]
  m = [[40,  5,  5],   # truly billing
       [ 3, 60,  7],   # truly technical
       [10,  2, 18]]   # truly account

  total = sum(map(sum, m))
  for i, name in enumerate(labels):
      tp = m[i][i]
      fn = sum(m[i]) - tp                      # rest of the row
      fp = sum(m[r][i] for r in range(3)) - tp # rest of the column
      tn = total - tp - fn - fp
      print(f"{name}: sensitivity {tp/(tp+fn):.2f}, specificity {tn/(tn+fp):.2f}")
  ```

  1. On paper, compute sensitivity and specificity for "account" first. Remember the axes here
     are the opposite of the lecture's: truth is on the rows.
  2. Run the code (any online Python runner) or rebuild it in a spreadsheet, and check your
     numbers.
  3. Write one sentence: which queue would you improve first, and which cell tells you what to
     fix?

  Success: account comes out at sensitivity 0.60 and specificity 0.90, and you named the cell
  (true account, predicted billing: 10 tickets) that drives it.

**You need:** the four boxes from → confusion-matrix.

A confusion matrix gives you counts, and counts are hard to compare when two test sets have
different sizes. **Sensitivity** and **specificity** turn the table into two rates that answer
the two questions anyone affected by a model actually asks: "if I'm a real case, will it find
me?" and "if I'm not, will it leave me alone?"

## The two formulas

- **Sensitivity = TP ÷ (TP + FN).** Of everything that really is positive, the share the model
  caught. Machine-learning code usually calls this **recall** or the **true-positive rate**.
- **Specificity = TN ÷ (TN + FP).** Of everything that really is negative, the share the model
  correctly cleared. One minus specificity is the **false-positive rate**, which you'll plot in
  → evaluation-metrics.

Each rate uses one "truth" group only. Sensitivity ignores the negatives completely, and
specificity ignores the positives. That's why you need both.

## Worked example: two models, two strengths

The lecture's two heart-disease models, on the same test patients:

1. **Logistic regression:** TP = 139, FN = 32, TN = 112, FP = 20.
   Sensitivity = 139 ÷ 171 ≈ **0.81**. Specificity = 112 ÷ 132 ≈ **0.85**.
2. **Random forest:** TP = 142, FN = 29, TN = 110, FP = 22.
   Sensitivity = 142 ÷ 171 ≈ **0.83**. Specificity = 110 ÷ 132 ≈ **0.83**.

The forest finds 3 more sick patients; logistic regression wrongly flags 2 fewer healthy
ones. Neither is "better" in general. If the model is a first screen and a miss means a sick
person goes home, the forest wins. If a false flag means an invasive follow-up, logistic
regression wins.

## More than two classes: one rate per class

With three or more labels there's no single sensitivity. You treat each class as "this one
vs. all the others" and compute its own pair:

- Its **TP** is its diagonal cell.
- Its **FN** is the rest of its true row or column (the real cases sent elsewhere).
- Its **FP** is everything else predicted as this class.
- Its **TN** is everything left.

In the lecture's movie example, one class gets a sensitivity of just 12 ÷ 207 ≈ **0.06**: the
model almost never finds those people. A single overall number would have hidden that.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading specificity as "how often a flag is right".** It's the share of negatives cleared.
  How trustworthy a flag is comes next, in → precision-recall-tradeoff.
- **Reporting one class's rates for a multi-class model.** Every class needs its own pair, and
  the rare classes are usually the weak ones.
- **Judging a change by one rate.** A threshold change that lifts sensitivity almost always
  costs specificity. Report both, before and after.
- **Forgetting volume.** A 2% false-positive rate on a million negatives is 20,000 false
  flags.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Classifier reports:** recall per class is sensitivity per class; it's the first thing to
  check on an intent router or moderation model.
- **ROC curves:** the y-axis is sensitivity and the x-axis is one minus specificity
  (→ evaluation-metrics).
- **Checking an LLM judge:** its agreement with human labels is measured as a true-positive
  rate and a true-negative rate, exactly these two numbers (→ eval-from-real-traces).
- **Detectors sold on one number:** AI-text detectors and safety filters need both rates before
  you can say who gets hurt.
