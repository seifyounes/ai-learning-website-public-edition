  **Give one AI workflow you rely on a small test set, so you can tell whether a change helped.**
  It could be a prompt you reuse every week, a custom GPT or Gem, an AI feature in your app, or
  an automation that drafts emails.

  1. Collect 15 inputs: about 8 normal ones, 4 awkward edge cases, and 3 that try to make it

A benchmark tells you how a model does on someone else's exam. Your app is more than the model:
it's the model plus your prompts, your retrieval, your tools and your business rules. Only an
eval built from **your** tasks tells you whether **your** system works, and whether the change
you made yesterday helped or broke something.

## What an eval is for

Evals aren't a separate chore; they answer product questions:

- **Experiments:** is prompt A better than prompt B? Is the new model worth switching to?
- **Red-teaming:** can tricky or hostile inputs make it do something unsafe?
- **Monitoring:** once it's live, are users getting stuck, seeing hallucinations, being refused
  wrongly?
- **Regression testing:** before a change ships, does everything that worked still work?

## Why LLM apps are harder to test

Ordinary code gives the same output for the same input. LLM apps don't. Prompts are brittle, so
a punctuation change can shift behaviour. Tasks are open-ended: there are many good emails and
many bad ones. And there are new failure kinds: made-up facts, jailbreaks, leaked data. So
you test *ranges* of inputs, and you test qualities, not just functions.

## Two kinds of check

- **Reference-based:** you have an expected answer and compare against it. Ideal for regression
  tests: extract the invoice total, and check it equals 1,240.00.
- **Reference-free:** no single right answer, so you check a quality: grounded in the retrieved
  text, polite, under a length, refuses what it should. This is what you run on live traffic.

Deterministic code checks (does the generated code run, is the JSON valid) are the cheapest and
most honest. Where judgement is needed, an LLM can grade, which → llm-as-judge covers.

## Worked example: totals hide a regression

A 60-case test set: 40 normal inputs, 15 edge cases, 5 adversarial ones.

| Version | Normal | Edge | Adversarial | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| v1 | 36/40 | 9/15 | 4/5 | 49/60 |
| v2 | 39/40 | 7/15 | 2/5 | 48/60 |

1. Totals: 81.7% vs 80.0%. It looks like a wash.
2. By group: v2 fixed 3 normal cases and broke 2 edge and 2 adversarial ones.
3. Adversarial cases are where harm happens, so v2 doesn't ship as it is.

That's why test sets are built in groups and read in groups.

## Start manual, then automate

Read outputs yourself first. It's how you learn what "good" means for this task, and if experts
can label a few dozen cases, that's worth a lot. Automated evals then scale your judgement; they
can't invent it. Keep reading logs as you go: the manual and the automated feed each other.

## Common mistakes

- **Borrowing someone else's criteria.** Pick checks from failures you see, not a vendor's list.
- **One run per case.** Outputs vary; run important cases a few times.
- **Only happy-path cases.** Edge and adversarial cases are where problems hide.
- **Not logging inputs and outputs.** Without logs there's nothing to build tomorrow's tests from.
