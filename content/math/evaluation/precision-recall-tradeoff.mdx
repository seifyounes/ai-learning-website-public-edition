**You need:** the four boxes from → confusion-matrix and sensitivity from
→ sensitivity-specificity. Fractions and percentages, nothing more.

Accuracy is the number everyone reaches for, and on rare events it is close to useless. This
lesson replaces it with two questions you can ask of any filter: **of what you flagged, how much
was real?** (precision) and **of what was real, how much did you flag?** (recall). Then it shows
the knob that trades one for the other.

## Three formulas on the same four boxes

- **Accuracy = (TP + TN) ÷ everything.** The share of all calls that were right.
- **Precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP).** Of everything flagged, the share that was real. Low precision
  means a flood of false alarms.
- **Recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN).** Of everything real, the share you caught. It's the same number as
  sensitivity. Low recall means misses.

Precision is the only one of the three that never looks at the true negatives. That's why it
stays honest when negatives vastly outnumber positives.

## The accuracy trap

If 98% of emails are fine, a filter that never flags anything is 98% accurate and catches
nothing. Accuracy rewards agreeing with the majority, so on imbalanced data it mostly measures
the class balance.

## Worked example: one spam filter, two thresholds

A hypothetical inbox gets 10,000 emails; 500 are spam. The filter gives every email a spam
score from 0 to 1, and you choose the cutoff.

**Cutoff 0.5.** It catches 450 spam (TP), misses 50 (FN), and wrongly flags 100 real emails
(FP). The other 9,400 are TN.

1. Accuracy = (450 + 9,400) ÷ 10,000 = **98.5%**.
2. Precision = 450 ÷ (450 + 100) ≈ **82%**.
3. Recall = 450 ÷ 500 = **90%**.

**Cutoff 0.8.** It now catches 400 spam, misses 100, and wrongly flags only 20 real emails.

1. Accuracy = (400 + 9,480) ÷ 10,000 = **98.8%**.
2. Precision = 400 ÷ 420 ≈ **95%**.
3. Recall = 400 ÷ 500 = **80%**.

Accuracy barely moved, yet the experience changed a lot: 80 fewer real emails lost to the spam
folder, 50 more spam emails in the inbox. A filter that flags nothing at all would still score
95%. For a spam filter, losing real mail is usually the costlier mistake, so the 0.8 cutoff
wins. For a guardrail that blocks harmful replies, you'd lean the other way.

> 💡 **Try it:** say this pair out loud until it's reflexive — "expensive misses → maximize
> recall; expensive false alarms → maximize precision." Now classify: airport security,
> spam filter, a chatbot's harmful-reply guardrail, a fraud-refund flagger. (Recall, precision,
> recall, depends on volume — and that "depends" is where engineering judgment lives.)

## Common mistakes

- **A glowing dashboard and unhappy users** → accuracy reported on imbalanced data → report
  precision and recall on the class you care about.
- **Confusing the two denominators.** Precision divides by what you flagged; recall divides by
  what was real. Say the denominator out loud.
- **Inheriting the cutoff.** A default of 0.5 is a guess, not a decision. Pick it from the
  costs and write the reason down.
- **Quoting precision without recall.** A filter that flags one obvious case has 100%
  precision and catches almost nothing.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Guardrails and moderation:** recall on harmful content is usually the number that matters.
- **Retrieval for RAG:** "precision@k" and "recall@k" ask how many retrieved chunks are relevant
  and how many relevant chunks were retrieved (→ rag-fundamentals).
- **Hallucination and fact checks:** a detector is judged on the hallucination class, never on
  overall accuracy.
- **One number for a team:** F1 combines the two, covered in → single-number-metric. The full
  curve of trade-offs is in → evaluation-metrics.
