**You need:** precision and recall from → precision-recall-tradeoff.

By now you can describe a model with half a dozen numbers. That's good for understanding one
model and bad for choosing between ten. Improving an AI system is a loop of try, measure,
compare, and the loop only runs fast when **one agreed number** says which version won. This
lecture gives two ways to build that number: F1 for yes/no tasks, and an average across groups.

## Why one number

If version A is better on recall and version B on precision, you have a debate, not a
decision. With a dozen versions the debate never ends. Agreeing a single number up front (and
an evaluation set to measure it on) turns each experiment into a quick verdict. You still look
at the other numbers; they just don't decide the ranking.

## F1: the harmonic mean

**F1 = 2 ÷ (1/P + 1/R) = 2PR ÷ (P + R).**

The harmonic mean behaves like an average when the two numbers are close and sinks towards the
smaller one when they're far apart. That's the point: a model can't buy a good F1 with perfect
precision and almost no recall.

## Worked example

1. **Classifier A:** precision 0.95, recall 0.90.
   F1 = 2 × 0.95 × 0.90 ÷ 1.85 = 1.71 ÷ 1.85 ≈ **0.924**.
2. **Classifier B:** precision 0.98, recall 0.85.
   F1 = 2 × 0.98 × 0.85 ÷ 1.83 = 1.666 ÷ 1.83 ≈ **0.910**.
3. A wins. With equal error costs, that settles it in one line.
4. **The extreme case:** precision 1.0, recall 0.02. The plain average is 0.51; F1 is
   0.04 ÷ 1.02 ≈ **0.039**. F1 tells the truth about a filter that almost never fires.

## Averages across groups

Many results come per segment: per region, per language, per kind of question. Keep each one
visible, but rank by a summary. Made-up error rates for three prompts on four kinds of support
question:

| Prompt | Billing | Tech | Account | Other | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3% | 7% | 5% | 9% | 6.0% |
| B | 5% | 6% | 5% | 10% | 6.5% |
| C | 2% | 3% | 4% | 5% | 3.5% |

C has the lowest mean error, so C is the one to keep iterating from. If one kind of question
matters more (it's most of your traffic, or the riskiest), weight it more, and decide that
before you look at results.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing the number after seeing results.** That's picking a winner and then a reason.
- **Using F1 when the costs are lopsided.** F1 weights precision and recall equally. If misses
  are far worse, rank by recall with a precision floor, or use a recall-weighted version
  (F-beta with beta above 1).
- **Letting the average hide a collapse.** A rising mean can hide one group getting much
  worse. Watch the groups too.
- **Comparing numbers from different test sets.** One number only ranks versions measured on the
  same data.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Prompt and model comparisons:** one agreed score on a fixed eval set is how teams decide
  between versions (→ llm-app-evals).
- **Leaderboards:** benchmark suites average many tasks into one score, with the same hidden
  trade-offs (→ reading-llm-benchmarks).
- **Retrieval and extraction:** F1 is the usual summary for extraction and many classification
  tasks.
- **Next step:** when cost or latency must also be met, one number isn't enough, which is
  where → satisficing-optimizing-metrics comes in.
