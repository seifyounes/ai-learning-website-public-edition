applyIt: |
  **Before you trust any automated grader, measure it against your own labels.** That applies to
  an LLM judge you built, an AI feature that scores essays or tickets, or a vendor tool that
  promises to "detect bad responses".

  1. Take 40 outputs you have labelled yourself for one specific failure (yes, it happened / no,
     it didn't).
  2. Run the grader on the same 40.
  3. Fill in the four boxes: grader and you both say "failure", both say "fine", and the two
     kinds of disagreement.
  4. Compute the true-positive rate (failures it caught) and the true-negative rate (fine
     outputs it cleared). Ignore the overall agreement figure.
  5. Decide whether it's good enough for the job: monitoring, blocking a release, or just
     sorting what to read next.

  **If you build software:** keep the labelled set in your repo and rerun the judge on it
  whenever you edit the judge prompt, as a test of the test.
  **If you don't code:** do it in a spreadsheet with three columns (output, your label, grader
  label) and four COUNTIFS formulas.

  Success: a grader's true-positive and true-negative rates on your own labels, and a decision
  about where it may be used.

task: |
  Build and validate a binary judge for one failure.

  1. Pick one failure type from your error analysis (→ llm-error-analysis), or use "the answer
     claims something the source text doesn't say".
  2. Collect 40 outputs and label each yourself: failure or fine. Try to include at least 8
     failures. Split them into a **dev** set (20) and a **test** set (20).
  3. Write a judge prompt and run it on each dev output in a chat assistant (or via an API if
     you code):

  ```prompt
  You are checking one thing only: [describe the failure precisely, with 2–3 examples of what
  counts and what doesn't]. Here is the output: [paste]. First write one sentence of reasoning,
  then answer exactly TRUE (the failure is present) or FALSE.
  ```

  4. Fill a confusion matrix for the dev set, compute the true-positive and true-negative rates,
     and refine the prompt where it disagrees with you.
  5. When you're happy, run it **once** on the test set and compute both rates again.

  Success: dev and test confusion matrices with both rates, and a sentence on whether the
  judge is good enough to monitor this failure.

This lesson puts the whole track to work. You start from raw traces of a real AI assistant and
end with an automated check you can prove agrees with you. The maths is the confusion matrix
from lesson one. The new part is applying it to a grader instead of a product.

## From traces to a target

A **trace** is the full record of one interaction: the system prompt, the user's messages, tool
calls and their results, and the replies. The lecture's loop:

1. Read about 100 traces and note what went wrong in each. Keep going until new traces stop
   teaching you anything new.
2. Group the notes into categories and count them.
3. For each big category, choose the cheapest check that works. A missing date in the prompt is
   fixed directly and guarded by a code test. A fuzzy failure like "should have handed off to a
   person" needs an LLM judge.

## Make the judge binary

Write a judge prompt for **one** failure type, spell out what counts and what doesn't, and ask
for a short reason and then **true or false**. A 1–5 score feels richer, but nobody can say what
a move from 3.2 to 3.7 means, and it's much harder to check against human labels.

## Worked example: agreement hides a useless judge

You labelled 100 traces yourself; 10 contain a missed handoff. Two candidate judges:

**Judge X** never flags anything.

1. TP = 0, FN = 10, FP = 0, TN = 90.
2. Agreement = 90 ÷ 100 = **90%**.
3. True-positive rate = 0 ÷ 10 = **0**. It catches nothing.

**Judge Y** is a real attempt.

1. TP = 8, FN = 2, FP = 6, TN = 84.
2. Agreement = 92 ÷ 100 = **92%**, barely above X.
3. True-positive rate = 8 ÷ 10 = **0.80**. True-negative rate = 84 ÷ 90 ≈ **0.93**.

Two points of agreement separate a useless judge from a useful one. The two rates show the
difference at once. Whether Y is good enough is a business call: if a person reviews every flag
in a minute, 6 false alarms per 100 are cheap and the 2 misses matter more.

## Don't grade the judge on its own homework

If you put labelled traces in the judge prompt as examples, measure it only on traces it hasn't
seen. Tune on a dev set; test once on a held-out set.

## Where the judge runs

- **In CI:** on a fixed labelled set, every time the prompt or model changes.
- **In production:** on a sample of live traces (or all of them, at small scale) to track the
  failure rate.
- **Fewer, better checks:** a handful of judges you trust beats dozens you don't. Keep labelling
  traces by hand on a regular rhythm, and after big changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting agreement.** Always give both rates, or the matrix.
- **Generic scores as the eval.** Use them at most to sort which traces to read next.
- **Judging everything with an LLM.** Code checks are cheaper wherever there's a right answer.
- **Evaluating too much too early.** Before real users, use the product yourself and read
  outputs; build judges when volume outgrows your reading.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Release gates and monitoring** for any LLM product (→ evals-and-testing,

- **Judges everywhere:** every → llm-as-judge check deserves this validation.
- **Synthetic test inputs:** the lecture generates them from combinations of user types and
