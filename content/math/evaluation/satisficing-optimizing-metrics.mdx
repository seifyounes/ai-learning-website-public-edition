**You need:** the single deciding number from → single-number-metric.

A model can be accurate and too slow, cheap and too wrong, sensitive and too noisy. You care
about several numbers, and a single number can't hold them all. The fix in this lecture is a
decision rule you'll use for the rest of your career: **optimize one metric, and set pass/fail
limits on the others.**

## Two kinds of metric

- **Optimizing metric:** the one you want as much of as possible. Quality, accuracy, pass rate,
  F1.
- **Satisficing metrics:** the ones that only need to be good enough. Latency under a limit,
  cost under a budget, false alarms below a rate. Beyond the limit, you don't care.

The rule then picks for you: throw out every option that breaks a limit, and take the best of
the rest on the optimizing metric.

## Why not a weighted formula?

You could score "accuracy − 0.5 × running time". The trouble is the 0.5: nobody can defend it,
and it lets a big win on one number cancel a failure on another. A model that is wonderful
and takes 6 seconds per reply can win a weighted contest and still lose your users. A limit
says plainly what "too slow" means.

## Worked example: choosing a model under a budget

Made-up numbers for four options behind a support chatbot, all measured on the same eval set:

| Option | Pass rate | p95 latency | Cost per 1k chats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large | 91% | 6.5 s | $12.00 |
| Mid | 87% | 2.4 s | $4.00 |
| Small | 79% | 1.1 s | $0.80 |
| Mid + retrieval | 89% | 2.9 s | $5.00 |

Rule: maximize pass rate, subject to p95 latency ≤ 3 s and cost ≤ $6 per 1,000 chats.

1. Large breaks both limits, so it's out despite the best pass rate.
2. Mid, Small and Mid + retrieval all pass.
3. Of those, Mid + retrieval has the highest pass rate (89%), so it wins.

Change one limit and the answer changes: with a budget of $1, only Small passes. That's the
rule working. The limits encode what the product needs, so changing a need changes the pick.

## Where the limits come from

Set each limit from users, budget or risk: how long people will wait, what a month of traffic
can cost, how many false alarms a reviewer can handle. Don't set it from whatever your current
model happens to score.

## Common mistakes

- **Optimizing two things at once.** If two numbers are both "maximize", you're back to an
  unresolved debate. Make one of them a limit.
- **Giving credit beyond a limit.** Faster than required isn't a tiebreaker unless you said so in
  advance.
- **Too many limits.** Every limit shrinks the field. Keep the ones that would really sink the
  product.
- **Limits measured on different data.** Measure every option on the same eval set and the same
  traffic sample.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Model choice:** quality on your own eval set, subject to latency and cost per request
  (→ choosing-models-providers, → cost-latency-caching).
- **Guardrails:** maximize helpfulness, subject to recall on harmful content above a floor
  (→ precision-recall-tradeoff).
- **Leaderboards:** a high benchmark score is a candidate's optimizing number, not proof it
  meets your limits (→ reading-llm-benchmarks).
- **Release gates:** a change ships only if every pass/fail check holds and the main score
  doesn't drop (→ llm-app-evals).
