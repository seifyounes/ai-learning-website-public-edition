applyIt: |
  **Any time you ask an AI to grade or compare work, you're using a biased classifier, so test it
  before you trust its scores.** It might be a chat assistant ranking two drafts of your essay or
  cover letter, an AI reviewer on your code changes, or an LLM-as-judge script scoring a
  chatbot's answers. All of them can carry position bias (favoring whatever's shown first),
  verbosity bias (longer reads as better) and self-preference: the failure modes this lesson
  names, wearing a friendly "9/10".

  Run the position-bias check now, in about ten minutes. Paste two drafts of something you've
  written into a chat assistant and ask which is better and why. Open a fresh chat, paste them in
  the reverse order and ask again. Repeat once more with a different pair.

  ```prompt
  Here are two versions of [what they are]. Which is better for [the reader and goal]? Answer
  "A" or "B" first, then give two reasons.
  ```

  If the winner flips with the order, write one rule in your notes for every future AI
  comparison: always run both orders, and treat a flip as "no clear winner".

  Success: two pairs tested in both orders, and a written verdict on whether that judge flips.
toolkit: |
  - **Bake judge hygiene into your saved evaluation prompt.** Whatever grades your work (a saved

    normalize length, and list a few items for you to check by hand each time.
  - **Prefer a yes/no rubric over a 1–10 score.** Ask "does this meet [one specific
    criterion]? Answer yes or no, then explain", one criterion per question. Binary verdicts are
    easier to check and to act on.
  - **Use a different model family to judge.** When comparing outputs from two assistants, grade
    them with a third, or check a sample yourself, so neither gets a home-team advantage.

task: |
  Test a chat-assistant judge for verbosity bias.

  1. Take 5 short answers you know are good: answers to questions from your studies or work,
     or replies from an AI you've checked.
  2. For each, make a padded copy: same facts, plus two sentences of filler ("This is an
     important question that many people ask…").
  3. In a fresh chat for each pair, paste both versions (short one as A in half the pairs, as B
     in the others) and ask:

  ```prompt
  Which answer is better for a reader who wants a correct, clear answer? Reply "A" or "B" first,
  then one sentence of reasoning.
  ```

  4. Count how often the padded version wins. Then add "Do not reward length; extra words that
     add no information are a flaw" to the prompt and rerun all 5.

  Success: two counts out of 5 (before and after the instruction), and a note on whether this
  judge rewards length.

When there's no single correct answer, someone has to judge each output, and people don't
scale. An **LLM judge** reads an output with an evaluation prompt and returns a verdict. It's
fast and flexible, and it's also a classifier with its own biases. Treat it like any other
model you ship: measure it before you trust it.

## Two ways to use a judge

- **Direct assessment:** a rubric for one output at a time. "Is this summary faithful to the
  source? Yes or no." Best for rules and compliance, where each output passes or fails on its
  own.
- **Pairwise comparison:** show two outputs and ask which is better. Best for subjective
  quality, or for choosing between two prompts or models. Many pairwise results can be combined
  into a ranking.

Some teams combine them: direct assessment to filter out outputs that break the rules, then
pairwise to pick the best of what's left.

## Why it's popular

It **scales** to thousands of outputs. It's **flexible**: as you see more data and your
criteria shift, you edit a prompt instead of relabelling everything. And it handles **nuance**
that word-overlap metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE can't, like "does this sound natural?" when
there's no reference to compare with.

## Three biases

- **Position bias:** favouring whichever answer appears in one slot, regardless of content.
- **Verbosity bias:** favouring the longer answer, even when the extra length is repetition.
- **Self-enhancement bias:** favouring outputs that read like the judge's own model.

## Worked example: a position swap

You compare prompt A and prompt B on 100 questions, and judge each pair **twice**, once in each
order.

1. In 58 pairs, the same prompt wins both times: A in 34, B in 24.
2. In 42 pairs, the winner flips with the order. Those verdicts measure position, not quality,
   so count them as ties.
3. Among decided pairs, A wins 34 of 58 ≈ **59%**.
4. Had you shown A first every time, A would have collected its 34 real wins plus all 42
   position-driven ones: 76%, a much bigger and false lead.

A flip rate of 42% also tells you this judge is unreliable on these questions. Tighten the
rubric, or hand-check before relying on it.

## Common mistakes

- **One ordering only.** Always randomize or run both orders.
- **1–10 scores.** Hard to calibrate and hard to act on; yes/no on one criterion is clearer.
- **The judge grades its own family's work.** Use a different family, or audit by hand.
- **Trusting it without measuring it.** A judge needs its own eval against human labels, which
  → eval-from-real-traces shows step by step.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Prompt and model comparisons:** pairwise judging is how many teams pick a winner
  (→ single-number-metric for turning results into one number).
- **Arena-style leaderboards:** pairwise votes, by people instead of a model, turned into
  rankings (→ reading-llm-benchmarks).
- **Production monitoring:** a direct-assessment judge on a sample of live traffic
  (→ observability-tracing).
- **Checking the judge:** its verdicts against yours form a → confusion-matrix.
