applyIt: |
  **Spend one hour reading what your AI actually produced, before you decide what to measure.**
  It works on any AI output you can collect: chatbot conversations, AI-drafted emails, meeting
  summaries, generated code reviews, answers from a custom GPT or Gem.

  1. Gather 30–100 recent real outputs (or your own past chats with an assistant on one kind of
     task).
  2. Read each one. If something is wrong, write one short line about what you see ("ignored the
     date the user gave", "invented a feature"). Don't diagnose yet.
  3. When you're done, group the lines into 4–6 categories (an AI assistant can propose them;
     you decide).
  4. Count each category, and pick the biggest one that matters to fix first.

  **If you build software:** log full traces (inputs, tool calls, outputs) so this review is a
  query, not an excavation (→ observability-tracing).
  **If you don't code:** copy outputs into a spreadsheet, one row each, with a "note" column and
  a "category" column; a pivot table does the counting.

  Success: a count of failures by category from real outputs, and the one category you'll fix
  first.

**You need:** the eval vocabulary from → llm-app-evals.

Most teams start evaluating an AI system by picking metrics. Error analysis starts one step
earlier: **find out what actually goes wrong**, then measure that. It's simple enough to do in a
spreadsheet, and it's the step most often skipped. The output is a ranked list of real failure
types, which tells you what to fix and what your evals should test.

## The method

1. **Look at the data.** Read real outputs (whole traces if you have them: the user's message,
   any tool calls, the reply).
2. **Write a note** on each one that goes wrong. Describe what you see; don't stop to debug.
3. **Group the notes** into a handful of categories. An LLM can propose groupings; you decide.
4. **Count** each category, and work on the biggest one that matters.

In the lecture, this showed one team problems no generic score would have found: tours booked
for the wrong date, and conversations where the AI should have handed off to a person and
didn't.

## Worked example: counting sets priorities

You read 100 conversations from a booking assistant and note 30 failures:

| Category | Count |
|---|---|
| Wrong date resolved | 12 |
| Missed handoff to a human | 8 |
| Wrong price quoted | 6 |
| Formatting (markdown in SMS) | 4 |

1. Failure rate now: 30 ÷ 100 = **30%**.
2. Fix every date failure, and the best case is 18 ÷ 100 = **18%**.
3. Fix every formatting failure instead, and the best case is 26 ÷ 100 = **26%**.

That ceiling, the most a fix can possibly help, tells you where an afternoon of work pays off.
Weigh it by severity too: one wrong price may cost more than four stray asterisks.

## What it gives you besides a fix list

- **A roadmap:** you see what users ask for that the product can't do yet.
- **Wrong assumptions:** users rarely use a product the way its builders imagined.
- **A feel for the model:** after enough reading, you can predict how it will respond, which
  helps you design prompts, tools and retrieval.
- **Your eval set:** each category is a candidate check; the noted examples are its first test
  cases (→ eval-from-real-traces).

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with generic scores.** "Helpfulness" or "conciseness" scores rarely match your real
  failures.
- **Debugging during the first pass.** Note and move on; analyse after you've seen the pattern.
- **Letting the AI decide the categories.** It proposes, you decide.
- **Doing it once.** Repeat after big changes and on a regular rhythm; new failure types appear.

## Where you'll see it in AI

- **Before building any eval:** categories here become the checks in → llm-app-evals.
- **Judges:** a frequent, fuzzy category is a good candidate for an → llm-as-judge check.
- **Tracing tools:** trace viewers exist to make this reading fast (→ observability-tracing).
- **Classic ML:** the same move, counting mistakes by cause, appears in the optional Andrew Ng
  lecture below.
