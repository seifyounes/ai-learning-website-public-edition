Many "AI in the business" projects fail for the same reason: they start with the tool, not the
problem. Someone reads about an AI feature, buys it, and then hunts for somewhere to use it. This
lesson flips that. You **map the business first**, find where work genuinely piles up or repeats,
and only then decide what (if anything) AI should touch. It works the same whether you're mapping
your own team, a client's company, or a business you only see from the outside.

## Start with processes, not tools

A business is a set of **processes**: repeatable sequences of steps that turn an input into an
output. Sales takes a lead and turns it into a customer. Support takes a question and turns it
into a resolution. Finance takes an invoice and turns it into a paid bill. Walk one of these end
to end and write down every step, every handoff, and every place work *waits*. The waits and the
copy-paste glue between systems are where time leaks, and where AI tends to help most.

The best source is the person who does the work. Ask them to walk you through last Tuesday, not
"the process." Real days surface the workarounds and exceptions that never make it into a
diagram.

## Score on value × feasibility

For each step, ask two questions:

- **Value if improved**: does fixing this save real hours, cut cost, lift revenue or raise
  quality? Be concrete: "support spends ~10 hrs/week tagging tickets" beats "support is slow."
- **Feasibility**: is the task repetitive and rule-ish? Is the data already available? Is there
  a clear notion of a *correct* output? And crucially: **how bad is it if the AI is occasionally
  wrong?** Low blast-radius tasks are safe places to start.

Score each 1–5 and multiply. Then plot the steps on a simple 2×2 with value up the side and
difficulty along the bottom (difficulty is feasibility flipped, so easy sits on the left). The
**top-left corner, high value and easy, is your launchpad.** The boring, high-volume, repetitive
tasks win first. The exciting moonshot usually sits top-right, high value but hard, and should
wait until you've built skills and trust.

## The shape of good first use-cases

In practitioners' reports, early wins tend to cluster into a few patterns: **drafting**
(emails, docs, replies), **extracting** (pulling fields out of documents, PDFs and forms),
**classifying/routing** (tag this ticket, triage this lead), **searching** (answering "where's the
doc that says…") and **summarizing** (turning a long thread or report into a decision). If your
mapped step looks like one of those, it's a strong candidate.

## Worked example: an online furniture shop's support team

A six-person customer-service team. After interviewing two of them, you list five processes and
score them (scores are judgment calls; that's fine as long as you write down why):

| Process | Value | Feasibility | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Replying to order-status emails | 4 | 5 | 20 |
| Writing the weekly complaints report | 3 | 4 | 12 |
| Processing returns | 4 | 3 | 12 |
| Updating the FAQ | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| Predicting which customers will leave | 5 | 1 | 5 |

Order-status replies win: high volume, the data sits in the order system, and a person can
approve each draft. Returns score well on value but touch money, so they wait. The churn
prediction is the moonshot: valuable, but there's no clean data yet.

The brief: "Draft replies to order-status emails from the order system; the team spends an
estimated 8 hours a week on these (to confirm with a one-week count); good = correct status and
tracking link, on-brand tone, approved by an agent before sending."

> 💡 **Try it:** Write down five things you did more than twice last week. Give each a value
> score and a feasibility score from 1 to 5, and multiply. Three minutes, and you have your
> own first ranking.

## Common failure modes

- **Everything scores 4 or 5** → no anchor for the scores → tie value to hours or money, and
  force a strict ranking with no ties.
- **The list names tools, not work** → "use ChatGPT for emails" → rewrite each item as an input
  and an output ("customer email in, sent reply out").
- **The pilot surprises the people who do the work** → you mapped from the org chart → interview
  the person doing the task before scoring it.
- **The first pick is the moonshot** → excitement beat feasibility → start top-left and earn the
  moonshot with an early win.
- **Hours figures were never checked** → estimates treated as facts → count for a week before
  the pilot; you'll need that baseline for ROI anyway (→ measuring-roi).

## The deliverable

You don't leave this stage with a tool. You leave with a **ranked opportunity list** and a
one-line brief for the top pick: the task, who does it today, how many hours a week it takes, and
what "good" output looks like. That brief is the spec for the rest of this track.
