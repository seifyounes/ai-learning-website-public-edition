You can build the perfect automation and the smartest assistant, and still fail, because the
tools sit unused. The hardest part of AI in a company usually isn't technical; it's **getting
people to actually change how they work.** A common pattern: an organization buys AI tools and
captures far less value than it expected, because adoption, not capability, is the bottleneck.

## It's a people problem

When a rollout fails, it's rarely the model. It's that people:

- **don't trust it** (they got a wrong answer once and quit),
- **weren't trained on their real work** (the demo used someone else's example),
- **fear being replaced** (so they quietly resist), or
- **were handed a mandate** instead of a reason.

Every one of those is a *human* problem with a human fix. Treat the rollout as change management
first, software deployment second.

## The playbook

**1. Lead with the pain, not the tech.** Nobody wants "GenAI." They want their Friday afternoon
back. Frame every rollout as the specific annoyance it removes.

**2. Pilot, don't blanket.** Pick one team with a clear, painful use-case. Win there. A real
internal success story is the most persuasive argument you have for the next team.

**3. Recruit champions.** One enthusiastic person per team who goes deep and helps peers. Adoption
spreads sideways, peer to peer, far better than it flows down from the top. Give champions real
time for it; it's work, not a hobby.

**4. Train on their real tasks.** Thirty minutes using the team's *own* recurring work beats a
polished webinar about a stranger's workflow. People adopt what they can use the same afternoon.

**5. Make beginnerhood safe.** Say out loud that AI output needs checking, that strange results
are normal and not the user's fault, and that "dumb" questions are welcome. People who are afraid
of looking foolish won't experiment, and people who don't experiment don't adopt.

## Address the elephant

People will wonder if AI is here to replace them. Be honest and specific about what changes:
which boring tasks go away, and what higher-value work that frees them for. Vague reassurance
breeds suspicion; a concrete "you'll stop doing X so you can do more Y" earns buy-in. Only say it
if it's true. Framing AI as **augmentation** and then actually redeploying the saved time into
better work is what keeps that trust.

## Worked example: a small real-estate office

A five-person office wants AI for listing descriptions and inbox triage. Here's the rollout:

1. **Find the pain.** In a 15-minute chat, agents say listing write-ups take an evening each
   and the shared inbox buries buyer enquiries.
2. **Pick the pilot.** Listing descriptions first: visible, frequent, and low-risk because an
   agent reviews every one.
3. **Recruit the champion.** The agent who already experiments with ChatGPT on her phone. She
   gets an hour a week, protected, to help others.
4. **Train on real work.** A 30-minute session where each agent drafts a description for one of
   *their* current listings, using a saved prompt with the office's style and a fact checklist
   (square metres, rooms, no promises about the area that aren't true).
5. **Make checking normal.** The champion shows a draft with a wrong detail and how she caught
   it.
6. **Week-one feedback.** A two-question message on Friday: what did you use it for, and what
   annoyed you? The answers shape week two.
7. **Measure one thing.** Minutes per listing description, before and after.

> 💡 **Try it:** For a tool you use (or wish your team used), write one sentence that starts "This
> means you'll stop having to…" If you can't finish it with a specific chore, you're not ready to
> roll it out.

## Common failure modes

- **Big launch, little use** → company-wide announcement with no pilot → start with one team and
  one painful task.
- **One bad answer ends adoption** → nobody set expectations → teach "always check" in the first
  session and show a caught error.
- **The champion burns out** → helping peers on top of a full job → give them protected time and
  visible credit.
- **Training is forgotten by lunch** → a generic demo → rebuild the session around the team's own
  documents and tasks.
- **Quiet resistance** → the replacement fear was never addressed → name the tasks that change
  and what people will do instead.
