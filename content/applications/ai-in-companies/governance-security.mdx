The moment a company starts using AI seriously, it takes on new risks: confidential data leaking
into someone's personal chatbot, a hallucinated number landing in a client report, a model making
a decision nobody can explain. **Governance** is how you get the upside of AI without the nasty
surprises, and for a small business it's far simpler than the enterprise jargon suggests.

## The three things to control

**1. Data.** This is the big one. The default risk is staff pasting confidential information
(customer PII, contracts, credentials, source code) into tools where it may be retained or used
for training. Your rule: a clear **"never paste this" list**, plus a sanctioned tool for
sensitive work (a business tier that excludes your data from training, or a local model). Match
the tool to the data. Data terms differ between consumer and business plans of the same product,
so check the plan you're actually on rather than the vendor's homepage.

**2. Accuracy & accountability.** AI is confidently wrong sometimes. So **a named human owns any
output that reaches a customer, a financial record or a legal document.** The human-in-the-loop
checkpoint from the automation lesson isn't just a quality measure; it's your core governance
control. AI assists; a person is responsible.

**3. Access & oversight.** Know which tools are in use, who can use them and what data they
touch. Give each person and each tool only the access it needs (least privilege). You can't
govern what you can't see.

## Security risks specific to AI

Two risks catch teams that are new to this. **Leaked keys**: an API key in a shared spreadsheet,
a browser app or a public repo can be copied and used on your bill. Keep keys server-side and
scan commits for secrets. **Prompt injection**: when an AI reads outside content (emails, web
pages, uploaded files) and can take actions, a hidden instruction in that content can steer it.
Limit what such agents can do, and keep a human approval on anything that sends, pays or deletes
(→ llm-security-prompt-injection).

## Shadow AI is the real threat

If you *ban* AI, people don't stop; they use their personal accounts where you have zero
visibility. That's **shadow AI**, and it's where many of the worst leaks happen. The fix is
counterintuitive: **make the approved path the easy, attractive one.** Give people a sanctioned,
private tool that's genuinely good, and most of the reason to go around it disappears.

## Keep it proportionate

You don't need an enterprise AI office to start. For most organizations, governance is:

1. A **one-page policy**: approved tools, the never-paste list, the human-approval rule, who to
   ask.
2. A **sanctioned, private tool** people actually want to use.
3. A **habit of reviewing** what's in use as you grow.

Larger frameworks (the **EU AI Act**, the **NIST AI RMF**) are worth knowing as you scale. One
part of the EU AI Act reaches small organizations too. Its Article 50 transparency rules have
applied since 2 August 2026 (check for later amendments), according to the published text
([Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). In short, AI systems that talk
to people must make clear they're AI, and deployers must disclose deepfakes and AI-generated text
published to inform the public, unless a person reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility.
EU rules are still being adjusted, so check the current status before relying on dates. Right-size
the guardrails to the risk: heavier where AI touches money, health or legal decisions; lighter
for drafting marketing copy.

## Worked example: a quick audit of a 12-person agency

A small marketing agency asks you to "check our AI use." In one afternoon:

1. **Inventory.** You ask everyone what AI tools they use for work. The list: personal ChatGPT
   and Gemini accounts, an AI note-taker that joins client calls, and an automation that drafts
   client reports using an API key.
2. **Data.** Client briefs are going into personal accounts with unknown settings → move to a
   business plan with training excluded (verify the terms) and add client documents to the
   never-paste list for personal accounts.
3. **Keys.** The automation's API key is pasted in a shared spreadsheet → revoke it, issue a new
   one, and store it in the automation tool's credential store.
4. **Consent.** The note-taker joins client calls unannounced → announce it at the start of each
   call and let clients opt out.
5. **Accountability.** Client reports contain AI-drafted numbers → a named account manager checks
   every figure against the source before sending.
6. **Policy.** The fixes become a one-page policy, reviewed again in three months.

> 💡 **Try it:** Open the data or privacy settings of the AI assistant you use most and find out
> whether your conversations can be used for training on your current plan. Under five minutes,
> and you'll know whether it's safe for work material.

## Common failure modes

- **The policy exists but nobody follows it** → it's long and full of jargon → cut it to one page
  of plain rules and walk the team through it.
- **Leaks continue after a ban** → shadow AI → offer an approved tool that's as easy as the
  personal one.
- **A key shows up somewhere public** → secrets kept in docs or code → keep keys server-side and
  add a secret scanner to commits.
- **"The vendor doesn't train on our data" turns out to be wrong** → terms assumed, not checked →
  verify the terms for your exact plan and record them.
