Everything in this track converges here. You mapped opportunities, automated a department, built
an assistant, rolled it out and put guardrails on it. **Measuring ROI** is how you prove it was
worth doing, and it's the difference between AI being a renewed budget line and a quietly
cancelled experiment. Inside a company, a real, measured result is what gets your next project
funded; for a consultant or agency, it's one of the strongest sales assets you can have.

## ROI is after minus before, so capture before

The most common ROI failure is having no **baseline.** You can only prove improvement against a
number you recorded *before* you changed anything. That's why the earlier lessons kept saying
*measure the manual process*: hours a week, cost per task, error rate, turnaround time. Skip the
baseline and your ROI is a guess, and guesses rarely survive a budget review.

Normalize the numbers. Total hours rise and fall with volume, so measure **per unit**: minutes
per ticket, cost per invoice. Otherwise a quiet month looks like an AI success and a busy one
looks like a failure.

## Measure outcomes, not activity

**Usage is a vanity metric.** Logins, messages sent, "seats active": they show motion, not
value. Real ROI ties to a number the business already cares about:

- **Operations** → hours saved per week, throughput.
- **Finance** → cost per processed item, error or rework rate.
- **Support** → response time, tickets resolved per agent, customer satisfaction.
- **Marketing/Sales** → conversion, pipeline, revenue per campaign.

Pick the one metric that maps to your use case and measure *that.* Then pick one **quality**
guard metric (error rate, reopen rate) so a faster process that produces worse work doesn't
count as a win.

## Count the full cost

Honest ROI subtracts everything: subscriptions and model or API spend, the time spent building,
training and change-management effort, and, easy to forget, the **ongoing human-review time**
that human-in-the-loop requires. A tool that saves 5 hours but needs 4 hours of checking isn't
the win the licence-fee math suggested.

Be careful with "hours saved." Saved hours only become money if they're used for something else
(more tickets, new work) or genuinely reduce cost. Often the honest description is "capacity
freed," and that's still a result worth reporting.

## Worked example: AI-drafted support replies

An online shop's support team starts using AI-drafted replies that agents review and send. All
numbers are hypothetical.

1. **Metric.** Minutes per routine ticket, from the help-desk export. Guard metric: the share of
   tickets reopened.
2. **Baseline.** Two weeks before launch: about 200 routine tickets a week at about 4 minutes
   each, roughly 13.3 hours a week.
3. **After.** Two comparable weeks after launch: the same volume, about 1.5 minutes each to
   review and send, so 5 hours a week. The reopen rate is unchanged.
4. **Value.** About 8.3 hours saved a week × $30 an hour ≈ $250 a week, or about $12,000 a year
   over 48 working weeks.
5. **Full cost.** Tool and API spend of $80 a month ($960 a year), plus 20 hours of setup at
   $50 an hour ($1,000): about $1,960 in the first year.
6. **Result.** About $10,000 net in year one, if the freed time goes to useful work.
7. **Caveat.** Two weeks is a small window; keep measuring for a quarter before quoting it widely.

> 💡 **Try it:** Pick one AI tool or prompt you use weekly. Write down, honestly, how many
> minutes the task took before and how many it takes now, including checking. If you can't
> answer the "before," you've found your first baseline to start.

## Adoption is a leading indicator

If ROI is disappointing, check usage before you blame the tool. **A capable tool nobody uses has
zero return.** Low adoption sends you back to the rollout lesson (training, champions, framing),
not to a different model. High adoption plus a moving outcome metric is the signal that it's
working.

## Common failure modes

- **The ROI uses the vendor's estimate** → no measurement of your own → time the task yourself,
  before and after.
- **The baseline comes from memory** → nobody logged it → run a one- or two-week time log before
  launch.
- **A seasonal dip looks like an AI win** → totals instead of per-unit numbers → normalize per
  ticket or per invoice and compare like periods.
- **Speed went up, quality went down** → no guard metric → track errors or reopens alongside
  time.

## The one-sentence result

End every AI initiative with a sentence a non-technical decision-maker can act on:

> "Cut invoice processing from ~6 hrs to ~1 hr/week — about \$X saved a year, at a tool cost of \$Y."

That sentence renews budgets, justifies the next project and, for a consultant, helps close the
next client.
