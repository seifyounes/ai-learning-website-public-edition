Custom work doesn't scale. Every project negotiated from scratch means unpredictable time,
unpredictable price, and you personally trapped in delivery. **Productized services** fix that:
you package a service as a repeatable **product** with a set scope, a set price and a set
process, so you sell the same defined thing over and over. For an AI freelancer, this is the
model that turns skills into a business that runs.

## Service vs. product

A **service** is "tell me what you need and I'll quote it." A **productized service** is "here's
exactly what you get, here's the price, here's how long it takes. Buy it." The difference is
**fixed scope.** The moment scope is fixed, three good things happen: delivery becomes
predictable, you can standardize and delegate it, and clients can say yes without a long
negotiation.

Examples (prices are placeholders, set your own):

- ❌ "AI consulting, hourly" → ✅ "AI automation audit + one built workflow, 7 days, fixed price."
- ❌ "Content help" → ✅ "8 SEO articles a month, managed, fixed monthly fee."
- ❌ "I'll build your app" → ✅ "MVP in 2 weeks, fixed scope, fixed price."

## Why AI makes this the right moment

Productized services have existed for a long time, but they used to need a team to keep margins
healthy at a fixed price. **AI changes the math.** What you've learned in this course is the
production line: AI drafts, builds, researches and automates labor that used to require hiring.
A solo operator can now deliver a packaged product profitably, *if* the scope is fixed and the
process is standardized.

## Standardize the delivery

A product needs a repeatable **process**, not heroics. Document the delivery as a checklist or
SOP (the scaling lesson goes deep on this): intake → build → review → handoff. Standardization is
what keeps quality consistent across clients, and what lets you eventually hand delivery to
someone else or an automation. Without it you've just renamed custom work.

## Tier it

One product, three price points: a **starter** (a taste, such as one automation or an audit), a
**core** (the full package) and a **retainer** (ongoing). Tiers capture different budgets, create
an obvious upsell path, and let a small client grow into a big one without you building anything
new. Three is usually enough; more tiers mostly make the buyer's decision harder.

## Worked example: productizing a support-reply automation

Imagine you've built one AI automation that drafts replies to routine customer emails for a small
online shop. Here's how it becomes a product:

1. **Name the outcome.** "Routine customer emails get a drafted reply within minutes; your team
   approves and sends."
2. **Write the deliverables checklist.** Connection to one inbox; drafts for up to five
   question types (order status, returns, shipping, sizing, opening hours); a review queue; a
   one-page handover guide; one 30-minute training call.
3. **Write the "not included" list.** Other inboxes, refunds or any action that moves money,
   integrations beyond the shop platform, new question types after sign-off. This list prevents
   more scope fights than any other line in the spec.
4. **Fix the price and turnaround.** One price, delivered in ten working days.
5. **Track your delivery time.** The first build might take you several days. By the third
   client, with templates and a saved prompt, it should take much less. That gap is your margin
   growing, at the same price.
6. **Attach the tiers.** Starter: an inbox audit that counts how many emails fall into each
   type. Retainer: monthly tuning plus one new question type each month.

> 💡 **Try it:** Take any offer you've written and spend five minutes on its **"not included"**
> list alone. If you can't think of three exclusions, your scope isn't fixed yet.

## Common failure modes

- **Every delivery runs over** → scope described in adjectives ("a full automation") rather
  than a checklist → rewrite deliverables as items a stranger could tick off.
- **Clients haggle the fixed price** → there's no smaller tier to point them to → add a starter
  tier and trade scope, never price.
- **The "product" is custom work with a price tag** → each client still gets a bespoke build →
  standardize 80% of the build and allow only named, limited options.
- **Margins shrink over time** → you never measured delivery hours, so you can't see what's
  growing → log hours per delivery and update the SOP whenever a step drags.
