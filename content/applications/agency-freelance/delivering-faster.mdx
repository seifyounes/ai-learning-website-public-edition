The profit in a service business hides in **delivery speed.** On fixed-price and productized
work, every hour you shave off fulfillment is margin you keep, and every project that ships
faster is a client more likely to refer you. This lesson is about building a **delivery
pipeline** where AI does the heavy lifting and you do the judgment, so client work ships fast
*and* stays high-quality. The same thinking applies if you deliver internally: a recurring report
or campaign at your job is a pipeline too.

## Map your fulfillment

You can't speed up a process you haven't drawn. Lay out how a typical project actually flows:
**intake → plan → draft/build → review → deliver → follow-up.** Then walk each stage and ask:
*where does AI do the labor, and where do I add the judgment?* Usually the labor (first drafts,
research, scaffolding, formatting) takes most of the clock, and it's exactly what AI compresses.
Judgment (what the client really needs, whether it's right, what to cut) is what they pay you
for.

## Never start from zero

Speed comes from two habits:

1. **Templatize the repeatable parts.** Onboarding questionnaires, project scaffolds, report
   layouts, email scripts. Every new project should start at 60%, not a blank page. Keep them in
   one notes system you actually use (the second-brain pillar uses Obsidian; Notion or Google
   Docs work too).
2. **AI for the first draft, you for the final call.** Let AI produce the rough version quickly
   (the article, the automation, the proposal, the code), then spend your time editing,
   correcting and raising it. The blank page is what's slow; AI removes it.

The quality of the draft depends on what you feed in. A good intake stage (client goals, voice
samples, facts to use, things to avoid) is what turns a generic AI draft into one that needs a
light edit.

## Automate the glue

Beyond the creative work, a lot of delivery time is *coordination*: creating folders, sending
kickoff emails, moving files, updating the client. Wire that up with the workflows pillar:
intake form submitted → project created → workspace set up → kickoff email sent, all
automatically, in Zapier, Make or n8n. Coordination overhead is pure waste; automate it away.

## The quality guardrail

One non-negotiable rule: **AI drafts, you decide.** Speed must never mean shipping unchecked
output to a client. Your review is what separates a professional using AI from someone forwarding
chatbot output. Clients are paying for *your* judgment, accelerated by AI. Make the review
concrete with a checklist, so it doesn't shrink when you're busy. And check your client contracts
and your AI tools' data terms before pasting client material in (→ governance-security).

## Worked example: a monthly client report

Say you produce a monthly marketing report for a client. Here's the pipeline, rebuilt:

1. **Intake (automated).** A form on the 1st of the month asks the client for anything notable
   (a launch, a sale, a site outage). Answers land in the project folder.
2. **Data pull (automated).** An automation exports last month's numbers from the analytics
   tool into a sheet in a fixed layout.
3. **Draft (AI).** A saved prompt receives the sheet, the client's notes and last month's report,
   and drafts the commentary: what moved, likely reasons, and three suggested actions, with
   every figure quoted from the sheet.
4. **Review (you).** Your QA checklist: every number matches the sheet, every "likely reason"
   is plausible and labelled as a hypothesis, the actions fit the client's goals, the tone
   matches previous reports.
5. **Deliver and follow up.** Send, then log any client questions as improvements to next
   month's template.

Time the old way and the new way for one cycle. The point isn't a specific number; it's knowing
which stage still eats your hours, so you fix that one next.

> 💡 **Try it:** Pick one deliverable you made recently and write down, from memory, how many
> minutes each stage took. Circle the biggest number. That's your first stage to speed up.

## Common failure modes

- **AI drafts need heavy rewriting** → the brief is thin → improve intake: goals, two voice
  samples and the facts to use go in before any drafting.
- **Quality varies from client to client** → review lives in your head → turn it into a written
  checklist or a saved QA reviewer you run every time.
- **An automation fails and nobody notices** → no error alert → add an error branch that emails
  or messages you when a step fails.
- **The client recognizes "AI tone"** → generic prompts with no style guide → give the drafting
  prompt the client's own past writing and a short list of words to avoid.
