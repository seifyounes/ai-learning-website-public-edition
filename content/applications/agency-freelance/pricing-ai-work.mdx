Pricing is where AI freelancers either thrive or quietly undercut themselves. The instinct is to
bill by the hour, and with AI that instinct works against you. If AI lets you deliver in 2 hours
what used to take 20, hourly billing means a 90% pay cut for getting *better.* This lesson is the
mindset shift that fixes it: price the **value you create**, not the time you spend.

## The hourly trap

Hourly pricing has a built-in punishment for efficiency: the faster and more skilled you get, the
less you earn for the same result. AI is a large efficiency multiplier, so it makes the trap
much worse. The client doesn't care whether the automation took you two hours or two weeks; they
care that it works. **Stop selling time.**

## Value-based pricing

Anchor the price to the client's **outcome**, not your effort:

- An automation that saves a client **$50,000 a year** → pricing it at **$8,000** is an easy yes,
  and the build time is irrelevant. (Illustrative numbers.)
- A content engine that drives new sales pipeline → priced against that pipeline, not per
  article.

To price on value you have to *know* the value, which is why the AI-in-companies track hammers
on baselines and ROI (→ measuring-roi). Walk into the conversation able to say "this saves or
earns you $X." That number, not your hours, sets the price.

Value pricing has a **floor** and a **ceiling**. The floor is your cost: your time, your tools
and the margin you need. The ceiling is the value to the client. Your price sits between them,
at a fraction of the value that gives the client an obvious payback.

How big a fraction? There is no standard. The video's rule of thumb is a tenfold return in the
first year, which means pricing at about a tenth of the yearly value; the worked example below
charges a larger share and still pays back within months. Whatever ratio you pick, be ready to
walk the client through the math.

## Choose the right model

- **Fixed price**: for productized, well-scoped deliverables. The client knows the cost up
  front; your margin grows as you get faster.
- **Retainer**: for ongoing work such as managed automations, content, or an internal assistant
  you maintain. Predictable **recurring revenue** for you, continuous value for them.
- **Hourly**: almost never for AI delivery. At most for genuinely open-ended advisory where scope
  can't be defined, and even then a day rate is usually cleaner.

## Don't forget maintenance

AI systems drift: models change, the client's needs evolve, edge cases appear. That ongoing
monitoring and tuning is real value, and a **retainer** is how you charge for it. Selling only
the one-time build leaves recurring revenue (and the client's long-term success) on the table.
Define what the retainer includes, such as a monthly accuracy check, prompt fixes and a set
number of small changes, so it doesn't turn into unlimited free work.

## Who pays for model usage

Every AI feature you hand over keeps costing money each time it runs: the API calls to the model
provider, billed by usage. While you build and test, that cost is yours, so include it in a fixed
price. After go-live, decide in writing who carries it. There are three clean options:

- **Pass it through at cost.** You keep the provider account and re-bill the actual monthly usage,
  with the provider's invoice attached (some freelancers add a small handling fee; say so up
  front). Transparent and fair, but the client's bill varies and you front the cash.
- **Include a usage cap, with an overage rate.** The retainer covers usage up to a stated amount
  per month; anything above it is billed at a stated rate. The client gets a predictable bill, and
  you're protected if volume grows. Price the cap with headroom over normal usage.
- **The client holds the API account and keys.** The client signs up with the provider, pays the
  provider directly and gives you a key with limited access. Usage never touches your books, the
  client owns the account when you part ways, and it's the safest choice when usage is large or
  hard to predict. You still set the spending limits and alerts in their dashboard.

**The risk to plan for is a usage spike.** A busy season, a marketing campaign, a bug that loops,
or staff pasting in much longer documents can multiply the bill in a single month. If you promised
"usage included" in a flat retainer with no cap, that spike comes out of your margin. Set a
monthly spending limit and budget alerts with the provider, and state in the contract who pays when
a spike comes from the client's volume (the client) versus from your bug (you).

**Worked numbers** for the support-inbox automation below. Every figure is an assumption to swap
for your own; check your provider's current per-token prices before quoting.

| | Normal month | Spike month (holiday sale) |
|---|---|---|
| Tickets drafted by AI (assumed) | 600 | 1,800 |
| Model cost per drafted reply (assumed) | $0.02 | $0.05 (longer threads) |
| Usage cost | 600 × $0.02 = **$12** | 1,800 × $0.05 = **$90** |

With a usage cap of $30 a month inside the retainer and overage at cost plus 10%, the normal
month is covered by the cap, and the spike month bills ($90 − $30) × 1.10 = **$66** extra. With
"usage included" and no cap, you'd have priced the retainer around a $12 month and would absorb
the extra $78 yourself, and a spike from a looping bug could run far higher. With the client-held account, the $90 lands on the client's
provider bill, and your job is the alert that fired at, say, $50.

## Worked example: pricing a support-inbox automation

All numbers here are hypothetical; the method is the point.

1. **Define the metric.** "Hours per week staff spend answering routine tickets," measured from
   the shop's help-desk export or a two-week time log.
2. **Get the baseline from the client.** Discovery shows about 12 hours a week on routine
   tickets, and the shop values staff time at $25 an hour.
3. **Estimate the change conservatively.** With AI drafting and staff approving, you expect
   about 7 of those hours back. Label it an estimate to be confirmed in a pilot.
4. **Turn it into a yearly value.** 7 hours × $25 × 50 working weeks ≈ $8,750 a year.
5. **Pick the price.** A fixed build at $3,000 pays back in roughly four months
   ($8,750 ÷ 12 ≈ $730 a month), which is an easy decision. Add a retainer for monthly tuning.
6. **Check the floor.** If the build takes you 25 hours, is $3,000 above what you need? If not,
   shrink the scope rather than drop the price.
7. **Write the one sentence.** "This frees about 7 staff hours a week, roughly $8,750 a year,
   for a one-off $3,000."

> 💡 **Try it:** Take one offer and write only step 7, the one justification sentence. If you
> can't fill in the outcome number, you've found what to ask in your next discovery call.

## Common failure modes

- **The client rejects your value number** → you estimated it alone → get the baseline numbers
  from the client in discovery, so it's *their* number.
- **You discount on the call** → nothing to trade except price → offer a smaller tier; change
  scope, never the price of the same scope.
- **The retainer becomes unlimited support** → no defined monthly scope → list what it covers
  and what triggers a new quote.
- **A usage spike wipes out your retainer margin** → "usage included" with no cap → add a cap
  with an overage rate, or move the API account to the client, and set provider spending alerts.
- **You price low because it was quick** → anchoring on your hours → reprice against the
  outcome, and keep the speed as your margin.
