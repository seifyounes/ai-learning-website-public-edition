SEO is how people find you without paying for ads, and AI has changed both *how you do it*
and *what you're optimizing for*. Done right, AI makes SEO faster; done wrong, it fills your
site with pages search engines learn to ignore. Here's the right way.

## What the search engines actually reward

Google's published position is that it rewards helpful, reliable content no matter how it
was produced, and that its spam policies target **scaled content**: large numbers of pages
made mainly to rank rather than to help, whether a person, a template or an AI wrote them.
So "is AI content allowed?" is the wrong question. The right one is "would this page still be
worth publishing if search engines didn't exist?"

The quality frame Google's guidelines use is **E-E-A-T**: Experience, Expertise,
Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness. In practice: first-hand detail only you could know,
accurate facts, a clear author or business behind the page, and nothing misleading.

## Research with AI (and real data)

Start with what people actually search, and the intent behind it: are they learning
(*how to…*), comparing (*best… for…*), ready to buy (*… near me*, *… price*), or looking for
a specific site?

AI is excellent at **clustering** and **question mining**: hand it a list of queries and it
groups them into topics and lists the follow-up questions each group implies. It is poor at
**numbers**. A chat model without a live data tool will happily invent search volumes. Get
data from real sources:

- **Google Search Console** (free): the queries your site already appears for, with
  impressions, clicks and average position. The best starting point if you have a site.
- Search autocomplete and "People also ask" boxes for question ideas.
- **Google Keyword Planner**, or a paid SEO tool (Ahrefs, Semrush, Surfer), for volumes and
  competition.

Paste that data into your assistant and let it build the topic map.

## On-page, without the spam trap

AI can draft the on-page layer in minutes: title tag, H1, a first paragraph that answers the
query directly, subheadings that cover the follow-up questions, internal links to related
pages and one clear next step. You make it **helpful, accurate and original**: real examples,
real numbers you can stand behind, real experience.

## Worked example: one page, one query

A hypothetical yoga studio wants more beginners from search.

1. **Find the query:** Search Console shows the site already gets impressions for "beginner
   yoga classes [city]" but sits on page two, with few clicks.
2. **Map the questions:** the assistant, given the related queries, lists what beginners ask:
   do I need to be flexible, what to bring, how much it costs, which class to start with.
3. **Audit the page:** the title is just the studio name; the first paragraph talks about the
   studio's philosophy; prices are hidden in a PDF.
4. **Fix in impact order:** a title and H1 carrying the query; a first paragraph that answers
   "yes, complete beginners welcome; here's the class to start with"; subheadings for each
   question; prices on the page; a short note from the teacher who runs the beginner class.
5. **Measure:** note the date, then compare impressions, clicks and position in Search Console
   after a few weeks. Change one thing at a time so you know what worked.

> 💡 **Try it:** Ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity the question your best page should answer.
> Write down which sources each one cites. That's your AI-search baseline, and it takes three
> minutes.

## Optimizing for AI search

People increasingly get answers from **ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews and Perplexity**, not just
ten blue links. Modern SEO includes **AEO/GEO** (answer- or generative-engine optimization):
making your content the thing AI engines cite. What helps is mostly good SEO done well: a
direct answer near the top, clear headings, specific facts, and being mentioned on other
reputable sites. Also check that your robots.txt isn't blocking the crawlers of engines you
want to appear in. Measure it the simple way: ask the engines your query periodically and
log who gets cited.

## If you promote a mobile app: ASO

For an app, the store listing is the page that matters. The levers, in rough impact order:
title and subtitle keywords; the iOS keyword field (100 hidden characters, no spaces after
commas, no words repeated from the title); screenshots, which barely affect ranking but decide
the install, so the first two show the result the app delivers; and a steady flow of fresh
ratings, requested right after a moment of success, never after a failure.

## Common failure modes

- **Symptom:** traffic spikes, then collapses. **Cause:** mass-produced pages with no real
  value. **Fix:** fewer, deeper pages with first-hand detail.
- **Symptom:** you target keywords nobody searches. **Cause:** volumes taken from a chat model.
  **Fix:** use Search Console or a keyword tool for numbers.
- **Symptom:** a page ranks but nobody clicks. **Cause:** a title that doesn't match the
  intent. **Fix:** rewrite the title to promise exactly what the searcher wants.
- **Symptom:** you can't tell what helped. **Cause:** changing everything at once. **Fix:** one
  dated change per page, then measure.

## The principle

AI handles the **research and drafting** at speed; **you** guarantee **quality and trust**.
Thin AI pages flood the zone and fade; genuinely helpful, well-structured content wins with
humans *and* AI engines. Next: showing up where attention lives, on social media.
