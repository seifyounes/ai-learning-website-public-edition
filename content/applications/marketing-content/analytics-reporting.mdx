Marketing without measurement is guessing with a budget. **Analytics** tells you what
actually works, and AI turns the overwhelming pile of data into clear decisions in minutes.
This is the loop that makes everything else in the track pay off.

## The point: data → decisions

Most marketers have *too much* data and too little insight: numbers across web analytics, ad
platforms, email and social, none of it adding up to "so what do I do?" The job isn't
collecting data; it's **deciding** from it. That's where AI shines, as long as you ask the
right questions and check its working.

## Two ways to connect your data

- **No code:** export a CSV from your analytics, email platform, store or social dashboard and
  upload it to ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. Ask it to calculate with code rather than "in its
  head"; all three can run analysis code on uploaded files, and the shown working is what makes
  the numbers checkable.
- **With a database:** connect a coding agent (**Claude Code, Cursor** or any MCP client) to
  your data through an **MCP server** and *converse* with it: it writes the query, runs it and
  explains the result. Use read-only credentials so a misunderstood request can't change
  anything (→ mcp-servers).

Either way, mind privacy: strip names and emails from exports you don't need, and check the
tool's data terms before uploading customer data.

## Warm up before you analyze

Before you trust any number, run the three checks from the video:

- **Describe:** ask the assistant to list every column with a few sample values and flag
  missing, duplicated or odd entries. If it misreads a column here, every later number is
  wrong.
- **Ask what the data can answer:** which questions it supports, which columns back each one,
  and which questions it *can't* answer (for example, revenue when the export has no prices).
- **State the goal:** the decision you need to make and who will read the result.

## The four questions, in order

1. **Funnel:** of the people who took the first step, what percentage reached the goal, out of
   how many? This tells you the *size* of the problem.
2. **Cohort:** does that rate differ by traffic source, campaign, week or device? This points
   to a *driver*.
3. **Drop-off:** what's the last step people take before they leave? This tells you *where* to
   act.
4. **Decision:** given all three, what ONE change do you test first?

## Worked example: an online course shop

Here's a hypothetical small shop selling an online course, with made-up numbers for
illustration.

1. **Funnel:** last 30 days, 300 people signed up for the free lesson and 24 bought the
   course: 8% of signups.
2. **Cohort:** split by source. Newsletter signups: 12 of 80 bought (15%). Social signups: 12
   of 220 bought (about 5.5%). The underlying query is simple:

   ```sql
   SELECT source,
          COUNT(*) AS signups,
          COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE purchased) AS buyers
   FROM signups
   WHERE created_at > now() - interval '30 days'
   GROUP BY source;
   ```

3. **Drop-off:** ranking the last event before leaving, the most common among non-buyers is
   "viewed pricing page".
4. **Decision:** social signups convert at about a third of the newsletter rate and stall at
   pricing. Test one change: send social signups a short email sequence that answers the
   pricing objection before they see the price. Re-measure in a month.

Notice the decision uses all three numbers. Starting from "people leave on the pricing page"
alone would have led to a pricing-page redesign that might not touch the real gap.

## Signal or noise?

Small groups produce big swings by chance. If 100 people convert at 20%, the plausible range
for the "true" rate is roughly 12% to 28% at 95% confidence, so a 5-point change from one
week to the next can easily be noise. Before reacting, check the base, wait to see whether the
change persists, and look for a driver (a release, a campaign, a price change) in the cohort
split (→ standard-error-vs-deviation).

## What a useful report contains

The few numbers that matter, each with its base and its change from last period; one or two
sentences on why it moved; **one** recommended change; and every claim traceable to a query
or calculation someone could re-run. For a freelancer, that report is a sellable weekly
deliverable.

> 💡 **Try it:** Export any small CSV you have (email campaign results, store orders, social
> post stats), upload it to your assistant and ask: "What's the conversion rate from the first
> step to the goal, out of how many people? Show your calculation." Check one number by hand.
> Five minutes.

## Common failure modes

- **Symptom:** confident numbers that turn out wrong. **Cause:** the model calculated without
  code, or misread a column. **Fix:** demand the query or code and spot-check one number.
- **Symptom:** a big reaction to a small swing. **Cause:** no base size. **Fix:** always report
  "out of how many" and wait for persistence.
- **Symptom:** effort spent on a step that doesn't matter. **Cause:** starting at drop-off.
  **Fix:** run funnel and cohort first.
- **Symptom:** reports nobody acts on. **Cause:** charts with no recommendation. **Fix:** every
  report ends with one dated decision.

## Close the loop

This is the marketing twin of **evals** and **iterating from feedback**: measure → learn →
improve → repeat. Make one data-driven change at a time and watch the number move.
