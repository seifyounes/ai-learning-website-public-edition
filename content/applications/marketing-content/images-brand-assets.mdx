Visuals carry a brand, and AI image tools now produce striking ones in seconds. The catch
isn't making *a* good image; it's making a **consistent set** that looks like one brand, and
using it honestly. That consistency is the real skill.

## Match the tool to the image job

Image models have different strengths, and those strengths shift with each release, so test
on your own use case. Roughly:

- **Stylised or photographic imagery with a strong look:** Midjourney is the long-standing
  favourite of designers; Flux-based tools are a popular alternative.
- **Images with readable text** (a poster, a quote card, a mockup with a headline): ChatGPT's
  image generation, Gemini and Ideogram have become much better at spelling, but still check
  every letter.
- **Editing a real photo** (extend a background, remove an object, change a colour): Photoshop
  and Firefly, Canva's AI tools, and the editing modes in ChatGPT and Gemini.
- **When commercial licensing is a worry:** Adobe positions Firefly as trained on licensed and
  public-domain content. For any tool, read its terms on commercial use before a client job.

## The workflow

1. **Prompt with a text model.** Ask ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini to turn your idea into a
   detailed image prompt: subject, style, mood, composition, palette. Your prompting skills
   directly raise image quality.
2. **Generate with an image model.** Social graphics, headers, mockups, illustrations.
3. **Refine and pick.** Generate several, choose, then adjust the prompt rather than rolling
   the dice endlessly.

## The real challenge: consistency

One beautiful image is easy; ten that look like the *same brand* is hard. The fixes:

- **Style references** (Midjourney's `--sref`, or an attached reference image in other tools)
  to lock a look across images. Most tools can also take a reference image of a product or
  character to keep it recognisable.
- A **fixed palette and mood** you reuse word for word.
- **Reusable prompt recipes**, so every asset starts from the same DNA.

A recipe has slots. A useful set: **subject** | **composition** (close-up, flat lay, wide
shot) | **medium and style** (soft editorial photo, flat vector illustration) | **lighting** |
**palette** (hex codes or named colours) | **mood** | **aspect ratio** | **never** (what the
brand avoids). You change only the subject slot from image to image.

## Worked example: a three-image set

A hypothetical online tea shop wants a header, a social post and a blog image for a new
autumn blend.

1. **DNA:** palette of deep green, cream and copper; mood "calm, warm, unhurried"; never
   "bright stock-photo smiles". Medium: soft natural-light still life.
2. **Recipe:** the assistant turns that into a template with a subject slot, a 16:9 or 4:5
   ratio, and "match the attached reference" for style.
3. **First render:** the header (a teapot on a wooden table, steam, autumn leaves) comes out
   well; it becomes the style reference.
4. **The set:** the social post (a cup held in two hands) and the blog image (loose leaves in a
   copper scoop) both use the same recipe and reference.
5. **Judge side by side:** the blog image is too saturated. The fix goes into the recipe
   ("muted, low saturation"), not just that one prompt.

The real product photos still come from a phone shoot; the AI images are mood and scene
setting, which the shop never passes off as product shots.

> 💡 **Try it:** Take a photo or screenshot of something that represents your brand's look and
> ask an assistant: "Describe this image's style as a reusable prompt: medium, lighting,
> palette, composition, mood." That's the start of your recipe, in under five minutes.

## Honesty, rights and logos

- **Don't fake reality.** Never present a generated image as a photo of a real product,
  premises, team or customer. If it's illustrative, it should be obvious or labelled.
- **Check the terms.** Commercial-use rights differ by tool and plan.
- **Logos are the exception.** Use AI to **explore logo concepts** fast, but **finalize in a
  real design tool**. Pure-AI marks tend to look generic, can resemble existing marks, and in
  some places purely AI-generated work may not get copyright protection, which matters for a
  mark you want to own.

## Common failure modes

- **Symptom:** a set that looks like five brands. **Cause:** prompting each image from
  scratch. **Fix:** one recipe and one style reference for the whole set.
- **Symptom:** garbled text in images. **Cause:** a model weak at typography, or too much text.
  **Fix:** use a text-strong model, keep text short, or add text afterwards in Canva or a
  design tool.
- **Symptom:** the same composition every time. **Cause:** a recipe with no composition slot.
  **Fix:** vary composition deliberately while the style stays fixed.
- **Symptom:** a customer complains that the product doesn't look like the picture. **Cause:**
  generated imagery used as product photos. **Fix:** real photos for real products.

You can now produce on-brand visuals, video, social, copy and SEO content at a pace that used
to need a team. Two lessons left: nurturing with email and funnels, then measuring it all
with analytics.
