Welcome to Applications. This track is about using everything you've learned to do
**marketing**, and it starts by mapping the toolkit. The danger isn't too few tools; it's
drowning in too many. New AI marketing tools launch constantly, and many are a thin layer over
the same few models you can already reach through a general assistant. So you'll build a
**lean stack** around the jobs that matter, then wire it together.

## The jobs a marketing stack covers

Think in jobs, not products. A complete stack covers eight slots:

- **Content & copy:** ideas, drafts, rewrites, headlines.
- **Images:** social visuals, headers, mockups, simple brand assets.
- **Video:** editing, captions, cutting long recordings into short clips.
- **SEO:** keyword and question research, on-page structure (and ASO if you promote an app).
- **Social scheduling:** batching posts and publishing them on time.
- **Email:** a list, sequences, newsletters.
- **Analytics:** turning numbers into decisions.
- **Glue:** the automations that move work between the others.

One solid tool per slot, not five overlapping ones. Some tools legitimately fill two slots; a
general assistant covers content, copy and first-pass analysis on its own.

## The stack this course assumes (a decision, not a directory)

You may already have most of it, so commit instead of browsing categories:

1. **A general assistant.** ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini for content, copy and analysis. Your
   prompting skills are the multiplier here. Nothing in this track needs code; if you already
   use a coding agent, it can also work with your files or data.
2. **Canva** (or Adobe Express) or your image model of choice, for social visuals and brand
   assets.
3. **One AI video editor** (CapCut- or Descript-class) for demo clips, captions and short-form.
4. **One scheduler** (Buffer-class) to batch a week of posts and stay consistent.
5. **n8n, Zapier or Make** as the glue, wiring draft → review → publish.

SEO research borrows your assistant plus a keyword tool when needed, and email rides on
whichever email platform you pick in the funnels lesson. Every pick is swappable; the point
is that each slot is filled and you stop shopping.

## How to choose a tool for a slot

When two tools look equally capable (they usually do), these questions decide:

- **Does something you already pay for cover most of the job?** If so, use it until it
  genuinely blocks you.
- **Does it connect to your glue?** A native n8n, Zapier or Make integration, or at least an
  API, means it can join a workflow. A tool you can only use by hand stays a manual step
  forever.
- **Can you get your work out?** Export matters when you switch, and you will switch.
- **How does the price scale?** Per seat, per use or per credit changes the maths fast once a
  client or teammate joins.
- **What happens to what you upload?** Before you paste client material, check whether the
  plan you're on uses inputs for training and what its data terms say.

## Worked example: a one-person bakery

Take a hypothetical home bakery that sells through a simple website and Instagram. Here's
its stack, built step by step.

1. **List the month's real tasks:** six product posts, two short videos of a bake, a monthly
   email to past customers, replies to DMs, a seasonal landing page, and a quick look at which
   posts drove orders.
2. **Map them to slots:** content (captions, email copy, landing page), images (product shots
   tidied up), video (two short edits), social scheduling, email, analytics. No SEO push this
   month, so that slot stays empty.
3. **Fill each slot from what's already there:** the baker already uses ChatGPT, Canva and her
   phone's video editor, and the website builder includes a basic email tool. That covers
   content, images, video and email.
4. **Add only what's missing:** a scheduler's free plan for the posts. The website's own stats
   plus a monthly CSV export cover analytics for now.
5. **Cut overlaps and total it:** she had a second AI writing app she'd stopped opening;
   cancelling it pays for the scheduler if she ever upgrades.

The result fits on one line per slot, and every tool on it gets used weekly.

> 💡 **Try it:** Open your bank or card statement and list every software subscription you
> pay for. Mark each with the marketing slot it fills, or "none". Any two in the same slot
> are your first cut. It takes five minutes and often frees budget for something you'll use.

## Wire it together

The win is a **small, connected stack**: a handful you actually use, wired so content flows
from idea to published with minimal manual work. A typical first wire: when a draft is marked
"approved" in your doc or sheet, an n8n, Zapier or Make workflow sends it to the scheduler and
logs it in a tracking sheet. The next lesson builds that pipeline properly (→ content-workflow).

## Common failure modes

- **Symptom:** you pay for tools you rarely open. **Cause:** buying for a demo rather than a
  job. **Fix:** map jobs first, fill one slot at a time, and cancel anything unused for a
  month.
- **Symptom:** every piece still takes hours. **Cause:** the tools aren't connected, so you're
  the copy-paste glue. **Fix:** automate one handoff (approved → scheduled) before buying
  anything new.
- **Symptom:** a client never adopts the stack you set up. **Cause:** it ignored the tools they
  already license. **Fix:** inventory their tools first and build around them.
- **Symptom:** you rebuild your stack every few months. **Cause:** chasing each new launch.
  **Fix:** keep a dated stack log and only swap a tool when it blocks a real task.

## Why this matters now

AI has flattened marketing. A solo operator or tiny agency with a sharp stack can now cover
work that used to need several specialists: content, design, SEO and distribution, all
amplified. For a freelancer, that's leverage you can **sell**. This track turns these tools
into results, job by job, starting with the content engine.
