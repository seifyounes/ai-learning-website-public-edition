Your idea passed validation. Now resist the urge to build *everything*. The MVP is the
**smallest version that delivers the core value and tests your biggest assumption.** Getting
this scope right is the difference between learning in days and disappearing for months.

## What an MVP is (and isn't)

- It **is** one core feature, done well enough to give real users real value, so you can
  learn.
- It **isn't** a shrunken version of the whole vision. Ten half-built features teach you
  nothing, because when nobody comes back you can't tell which one failed.

The job of the MVP is to **learn**, fast. Impressing people is a later problem.

## An MVP doesn't have to be software

People hear "product" and think "app". But the first version only has to test the assumption,
and several shapes do that with little or no code:

- **Fake door.** A landing page that describes the product with a signup button (the last
  lesson). Tests demand.
- **Concierge MVP.** You deliver the result by hand to a handful of users. If your product is
  "personalized meal plans", you write them yourself in a doc. Tests whether people value the
  outcome.
- **Wizard of Oz.** It *looks* automated to the user (a form, an email), but a human does the
  work behind the curtain. Tests the experience before you build the engine.
- **Single-feature product.** Real software that does exactly one job. Tests whether people
  use it again without you nudging them.

Pick the cheapest shape that answers your riskiest question. **If you don't code,** the first
three are fully open to you, and plenty of products started that way.

## Scope by cutting

1. **List everything** you imagine the product doing. Get it all out; this list is usually
   long.
2. **Name the riskiest assumption**: the belief that, if wrong, kills the idea. It's usually
   about behaviour ("people will paste their data in every week") more than technology.
3. **Find the one feature that tests it.** Only that feature goes on the build list.
4. **Move everything else to "not yet".** Write the cut list down. It's as important as the
   build list, because it stops future-you (and your AI assistant) from quietly re-adding
   things.
5. **Define success** as something you can count with your first ten users, set before you
   build.

If your scope doesn't feel **uncomfortably small**, cut more.

## Worked example: an invoice-chasing tool for freelancers

Idea: help freelance designers get paid on time.

1. **Full list:** invoice creation, client portal, automatic reminders, late fees, payment
   links, accounting sync, dashboards, team accounts, mobile app. Nine features.
2. **Riskiest assumption:** "Freelancers will let a tool email their clients for them." Many
   may worry it looks pushy. If they won't, the product is dead.
3. **One feature:** forward an unpaid invoice to an email address, and a polite reminder goes
   to the client after a due date you set.
4. **Not yet:** the other eight. Creating invoices can wait; they already have a way to do
   that.
5. **Success:** at least 6 of the first 10 users forward a second invoice within a month. That
   number is a judgment call you write down in advance.
6. **Cheapest shape:** a Wizard of Oz first. You read the forwarded emails and send the
   reminders by hand for two weeks before automating anything.

Notice that the spec now fits on half a page, and the cut list is longer than the build list.

## Using AI without letting it pad the scope

Assistants are good scoping partners when you aim them well. They spot hidden assumptions,
draft the user flow and write success criteria. Left alone, though, they tend to suggest
"sensible" extras: authentication, settings, analytics. Steer them: give a hard limit of one
feature, ask them to argue *against* each feature, and ask "what's the cheapest way to test
this without software?" The fuller spec format (user stories, acceptance criteria) is its own
lesson (→ idea-to-spec).

> 💡 **Try it:** paste your feature list into any assistant with: "Which ONE of these tests my
> riskiest assumption, which is [assumption]? Argue against keeping each of the others." Read
> the arguments and cross out at least three features.

## Common failure modes

- **Months pass, still not shipped** → the MVP is a miniature of the full vision → cut to one
  feature and set a ship date.
- **Users try it once and leave, and you don't know why** → too many features, so no clear
  signal → strip back to the core and measure only that.
- **The MVP proves the tech works but not that anyone cares** → you tested feasibility instead
  of the riskiest *behaviour* → rewrite the assumption around what users must do.
- **Cut features sneak back in** → no written "Not yet" list → keep it in the spec and in your
  assistant's instructions.

A sharp, tiny MVP spec is the launchpad. Next: building it, which in the AI era can genuinely
happen in a weekend.
