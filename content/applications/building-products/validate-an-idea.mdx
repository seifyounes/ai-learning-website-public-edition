The most expensive mistake in product building is the same in 2026 as ever:
**building something nobody wants.** AI didn't fix that. It made it *easier* to do,
because now you can build the wrong thing in a weekend. So before you build, you
**validate**. Nothing in this lesson needs code: every method works with a browser, a chat
assistant and a few conversations.

## What validation has to prove

Validation answers three questions, in this order:

1. **Is the problem real and painful?** Not "would this be nice", but "does it cost these
   people time, money or stress often enough that they already try to fix it?"
2. **Will they pay for a fix?** In money, or in real effort: switching tools, giving you an
   hour, handing over their data.
3. **Can you reach them?** A painful problem among people you have no way to find is still a
   dead end.

Until you have evidence for all three, your idea is a guess. That's fine. The aim is to turn
the guess into evidence as cheaply as possible.

## Not all signals are equal

Rank what you collect from weakest to strongest:

- **Compliments and predictions** ("great idea, I'd use that"). Almost worthless: people are
  polite and bad at forecasting their own behaviour.
- **Public complaints.** Forum threads, app-store reviews of rivals, Reddit posts asking "how
  do you deal with…". Real, but you don't know how many people they stand for.
- **Interviews about past behaviour.** "Tell me about the last time this happened. What did
  you do? What did it cost you?" Concrete stories beat opinions.
- **Commitment from strangers.** A waitlist signup from someone you've never met, a booked
  call, a beta request.
- **Money.** A pre-order, a deposit, a paid pilot. Someone paying before the product exists
  is the strongest signal there is.

The interview rule comes from Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test*: ask about their life and
what they already did, never whether they like your idea, because even your mum will say yes.

## Where AI helps, and where it can't

AI is genuinely good at the grunt work. A research mode can sweep forums and reviews and
cluster complaints into themes. A chat assistant can draft non-leading interview questions,
spot the leading ones you wrote, build the fake-door page in an hour, and summarize ten
interview notes into patterns.

What it can't be is the customer. An assistant asked "is this a good idea?" will usually find
reasons it is. Market-size numbers it produces without links may be invented. So use it to
**collect and organize evidence**, never as the evidence. Always ask it to quote its sources
and to mark which claims are assumptions.

## Worked example: an order-tracking idea for small bakeries

Say your idea is a simple tool that turns a small bakery's custom-cake orders (arriving by
phone, Instagram DMs and WhatsApp) into one weekly prep list.

1. **Write the riskiest assumption.** "Bakery owners lose orders or over-prep often enough to
   pay to stop it." If that's false, nothing else matters.
2. **Sweep for free evidence (20 minutes).** Search baking and small-business forums for
   "missed order" and "order tracking", and read reviews of existing order tools. Paste the
   best quotes into your assistant and ask it to cluster them.
3. **Draft five questions with AI**, then cut any that mention your tool. Keep ones like "Walk
   me through how last Saturday's orders reached you."
4. **Talk to three to five bakers.** Visit local shops or message them. Write down their exact
   words.
5. **Set the fake-door threshold before launching it.** For example: "If 15 of the first 200
   targeted visitors join the waitlist, build the MVP." The number is your call; deciding it
   *first* is what keeps you honest.
6. **Decide.** Go, no-go, or test one more thing. Write it down with the evidence beside it.

> 💡 **Try it:** spend five minutes searching Reddit for "how do you [the task your idea
> helps with]". Copy three posts where someone describes the problem in their own words. If
> you can't find any, that is a finding too.

## Common failure modes

- **Everyone loves it, nobody signs up** → you asked about the idea instead of their past
  behaviour → rewrite your questions around "the last time…" and look for commitments.
- **The research says the market is huge** → the assistant produced numbers without sources →
  ask for links and treat anything unlinked as an assumption.
- **A fake door with no verdict** → no threshold set in advance, so any result feels like a
  yes → write the go number before you send traffic.
- **Validation that never ends** → you keep testing to avoid deciding → time-box it to one or
  two weeks and accept "good enough evidence".
- **Interviewing the wrong people** → friends and fellow builders instead of the people with
  the problem → recruit from where the problem is discussed.

## The mindset

Fall in love with the **problem**, not your solution. Validate cheaply, kill bad ideas fast,
and only pour your (now very fast) building energy into something people have shown they
want. Once an idea earns a green light, you scope the smallest version that tests it, the MVP
(→ idea-to-mvp).
