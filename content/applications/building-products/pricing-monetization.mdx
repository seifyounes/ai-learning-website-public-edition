A product that doesn't make money is a hobby. **Pricing** is where a product becomes a
business, and it's the highest-leverage number you'll set. Get it roughly right and
everything gets easier. None of this needs code: you can sell with a payment link and a
spreadsheet long before you build billing.

## Price on value, not cost

The biggest mistake is pricing from *your* costs ("it costs me $2, I'll charge $5").
Instead, price from the **value to the customer**: if your tool saves a small agency ten
hours a month, the question is what those hours are worth to *them*. Your costs set a floor
you must stay above. They don't set the price.

A practical way in: list the two or three alternatives your customer uses today (another
tool, a spreadsheet, a freelancer, doing nothing) and what each costs them in money and time.
Your price should sit comfortably below the value you add and make sense next to those
alternatives.

## Choose a model

- **Flat subscription**: one price, simple to sell. Risky when usage costs vary a lot.
- **Tiers (good / better / best)**: an entry tier, a middle tier most buyers pick, a premium
  tier for teams or power users. The middle option does much of the selling.
- **Per seat**: price per user. Fits team tools.
- **Usage-based or credits**: pay for what you use. Fits AI-heavy products.
- **Hybrid**: a base subscription that includes an allowance, plus paid top-ups. Common for AI
  products because it keeps the price simple while protecting margin.
- **One-time**: templates, courses, small tools. Simple, but no recurring revenue.

Then decide how people try it: a **free trial** (time-limited), a **free tier**
(feature-limited, forever), or **no free option** with a refund policy. A free tier costs you
real money if it includes AI calls, so cap it.

## The AI twist: variable costs

AI features cost you **per token**, so unlike classic software, **a heavy user costs you real
money every time they use it.** Flat "unlimited" pricing can quietly destroy your margins. You
need to know your cost per user before you set a price.

## Worked example: a meeting-notes app

All numbers here are illustrative assumptions; plug in current prices from your model
provider's pricing page.

1. **Price the action.** Summarizing one meeting uses roughly a transcript's worth of input
   tokens plus a short output. Say that works out to about 3 cents with the model you've
   chosen.
2. **Model a typical user.** 20 meetings a month → about 60 cents of AI cost.
3. **Model a heavy user.** 120 meetings a month → about $3.60. Still fine under, say, a $15
   plan.
4. **Add the risky feature.** "Ask anything across all your meetings" re-reads lots of text
   per question. Say 10 cents a question; a heavy user asking 400 questions → $40. Now that
   user costs more than they pay.
5. **Fix the design, not just the price.** Include a monthly question allowance in each tier,
   sell top-ups, cache answers to repeated questions, and use a cheaper model for simple
   lookups (→ cost-latency-caching).
6. **Write the ceiling down:** "AI cost per user must stay under X% of the plan price." The
   percentage is your decision; having one is the point.

## Getting paid without building billing

**If you don't code,** you can still charge from day one. Stripe Payment Links, Gumroad, Lemon
Squeezy and Paddle let you sell a subscription or product from a link. Merchant-of-record
services (such as Paddle and Lemon Squeezy) also handle sales tax for you. Apps sold through
the Apple and Google stores pay the store a commission, so include it in your maths.

> 💡 **Try it:** open the pricing pages of two products you use. For each, write one line:
> what's the anchor tier, and how do they limit heavy users? You'll start seeing the same
> handful of patterns everywhere.

## Common failure modes

- **Margins shrink as you grow** → flat unlimited plan on a variable-cost feature → add
  allowances, credits or caps.
- **Lots of signups, almost no revenue** → a generous free tier with no reason to upgrade →
  move one valuable limit (volume, seats, a key feature) behind the paid tier.
- **Nobody picks the top tier, everyone picks the bottom** → tiers differ in features nobody
  values → rebuild them around usage or outcomes buyers care about.
- **You can't explain the price** → it came from costs or a guess → write the one-sentence
  value reason, and test it on a real buyer.

## Don't underprice

Low prices signal low value, attract the churniest customers and starve you of the cash to
improve. It's far easier to **discount from a high price** than to raise a low one. With a
product that's built and priced, the last step is getting it in front of people: the launch.
