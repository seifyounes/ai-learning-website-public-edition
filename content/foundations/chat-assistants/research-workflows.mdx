Research is the AI use case where a wrong answer costs the most. Quote an invented statistic in
an essay, a client report or a team meeting and your credibility takes the hit, not the
model's. The good news: the tools have got much better at **sourcing**, and a simple workflow
gives you research that's both fast *and* trustworthy. The skill is demanding evidence and
verifying the parts that matter. This lesson applies the verification habits from the mental
models pillar (→ verification-and-grounding) to everyday research.

## Citation-first beats answer-first

For factual research, prefer tools built around **sources**: **Perplexity** (designed to cite
every claim) and the search and research modes in **ChatGPT**, **Gemini** and **Claude**. They
don't just answer; they show *where the answer came from*, with links. That's the difference
between "the AI said so" and "here's the page it came from." When facts matter, don't use a mode
that won't show its sources.

## Pick the right mode for the question

Most assistants now offer three kinds of research, and choosing well saves time:

- **Quick search** — a cited answer in seconds. Good for "what's the current version of X?" or
  "when does this policy take effect?"
- **Deep research** — the tool runs many searches for several minutes and writes a long, cited
  report. Good for a literature scan, a market overview or "what are the main positions on X?"
- **Your own sources** — tools that answer only from documents you upload: Gemini Notebook
  (formerly NotebookLM), or a Project with files in ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. Good when the truth lives in your readings,
  your company's documents or a set of papers you trust.

A good research question also sets limits: the time window ("since 2024"), the source types
("official government or university sources only"), and what to exclude ("no vendor marketing").
Narrow questions get better sources.

## A citation is permission to verify, not proof

The trap: a citation *looks* authoritative, so it's tempting to trust it. But AI can cite a real
source that **doesn't actually support the claim**, quote a number from a different year, or
summarize an opinion piece as if it were a finding. Citations make verification possible; they
don't make it unnecessary. For any **load-bearing** claim — a number you'll put in a proposal, a
rule you'll follow, a stat you'll repeat — open the source and confirm it says what the AI claims.

This is the same check evaluation teams run on AI systems at scale, usually called
**faithfulness** or groundedness: is the answer actually supported by the sources it retrieved?
You're doing it by hand.

## The two-pass workflow

Fast and trustworthy aren't opposites if you split the work:

1. **Broad pass** — run a deep-research or citation-first query to get a wide, cited overview in
   minutes.
2. **Verify pass** — list the few claims you'll actually rely on and check *those* sources
   directly.

You get the speed of automated research and the safety of human verification, without
fact-checking every sentence. Verify what's load-bearing; skim the rest.

> 💡 **Try it:** ask any assistant a factual question in your field and add "give me the source
> for each claim." Open the first link and find the exact sentence that supports the first claim.
> If you can't find it in two minutes, you've just learned how often that matters.

## Worked example: checking a statistic before you use it

Say a student writing an essay on remote work asks a research mode: "What share of workers in
the UK work from home at least part of the week?" It answers with a percentage and three links.
Here's how the check typically plays out:

1. **Open the most authoritative link first.** An official statistics office page beats a news
   article. Suppose the number is there, but for a different year than the summary implied.
2. **Check the definition.** The source counts "hybrid workers" only, which is narrower than
   "work from home at least part of the week."
3. **Check the date.** There's a newer release, and the figure has moved.
4. **Write it precisely.** The essay cites the newer official figure, with its definition and
   date, and links the source.

Ten minutes of checking turned a vague, outdated number into a precise, defensible one. That's
the whole workflow, and it works the same way for a market-size figure at work or a performance
claim in a technical doc.

## Common failure modes

- **A citation links to a real page that says something else** → the summary over-reached →
  find the exact supporting sentence or drop the claim.
- **A number is right but outdated** → the tool found an older source first → ask for the most
  recent figure and check the publication date.
- **Every source is a blog quoting another blog** → no primary source in the chain → ask for
  official, academic or original-data sources only.
- **A deep-research report reads beautifully and you trust all of it** → polish isn't
  verification → pick the claims you'll act on and check those.

## Make "show your sources" a reflex

Even in an ordinary chat, add "**give me the source for each claim**" to factual questions. If the
assistant can't produce one, treat the statement as a **hypothesis to check**, not a fact to use.
That one habit prevents most AI research mistakes.
