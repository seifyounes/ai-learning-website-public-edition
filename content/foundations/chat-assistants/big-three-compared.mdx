Most people ask which $20 subscription is "the best". Ask a better question: **which assistant
do I send this particular job to, and how would I know?** ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are all
excellent and all flawed in different places. Treat them as a **dispatch board**, not a
favorite: keep two or three a click away and send each job to the one that fits. This lesson is
the routing spine for the whole pillar, whether your jobs are essays, spreadsheets, research or
code.

## No universal winner — route by job

The one durable fact: **the lead leapfrogs.** Whoever tops a benchmark this quarter gets passed
next release, so any "best AI" ranking is stale soon after it publishes. Rough lanes, drawn from
the video's tests and many users' reports rather than measurement, to verify against your own
tasks rather than memorize:

- **Claude** — careful planning and multi-step reasoning, polished design output such as slides,
  and strong coding.
- **ChatGPT** — the broadest tool surface: voice, image generation, browser use, data analysis,
  custom GPTs, and strong coding too.
- **Gemini** — deep Google Workspace reach (Docs, Sheets, Gmail, Drive), very large documents and
  video generation.

Writing is the warning row. In the video, writing goes to ChatGPT, reversing the reviewer's
earlier preference for Claude; a second comparison from the same month (in Backup resources)
still gives Claude the edge on prose. Two careful reviewers, one month, opposite verdicts: that is
why you run your own.

Two related knobs live one level down and are *not* this lesson: which **tier** inside one
vendor, and how hard it thinks (→ models-and-effort); and which provider to **build an app on**,
with latency, privacy and region tradeoffs (→ choosing-models-providers). Here the axis is
simple: which assistant, for which job.

## The bake-off is the only benchmark that counts

Reputation is a weak signal; a side-by-side on **your** kind of work is a strong one. Give all
three the *same* real task, keep the one that wins **for that job class**, and write the verdict
down. Do it ten times and you own a routing table no leaderboard can hand you.

> 💡 **Try it:** take one real task you'd do this week anyway: an essay or report outline, a
> spreadsheet formula you can't quite get right, a tricky bug, a summary of a long article. Paste
> the identical prompt into all three. Judge the outputs, not the logos. The disagreements are
> where you learn each assistant's real shape.

## Worked example: a three-way formula bake-off

Say you track club or team expenses in a spreadsheet and need a formula that totals one
category for one month. You write the criteria first: *works on the first paste, explains itself
in one line, handles empty cells.* Then you send the same prompt, with five sample rows pasted in,
to all three.

One returns a formula that works but silently counts blank dates as January. One asks a
clarifying question about your date format, then gives a correct formula. One gives a correct
formula plus a version for Google Sheets and one for Excel. You test each on your sample rows,
which takes two minutes, and record: `spreadsheet formulas × [winner] × asked about date format,
worked first time`. That row now saves you the decision next time. Note what decided it: not
which answer *sounded* most confident, but which one survived your test data.

## Everyday workflows, routed

The same routing habit applies across ordinary work:

- **Writing.** Draft in the assistant whose voice you edit least. Then ask a *different* one to
  critique the draft against your brief: a second model catches what the first glossed over.
- **Reading and research.** For long PDFs, use an assistant that handles big uploads well and ask
  it to cite page numbers, so you can check. For current facts, use a search-backed mode
  (→ research-workflows).
- **Analysis.** Upload the spreadsheet itself rather than pasting numbers. ChatGPT, Claude and
  Gemini can all run calculations on uploaded files in some form; ask it to show the steps or the
  code it ran, and spot-check one number by hand.
- **Studying.** Ask it to explain a concept at two levels, then quiz you without showing answers.
  Routing matters less here than the habit of making it test you.

## Features that matter more than the logo

Whichever you pick, three features change daily usefulness more than the model does:

- **Standing workspaces** — ChatGPT Projects and Custom GPTs, Claude Projects, Gemini Gems. Put
  instructions and reference files in once, and every chat on that job starts briefed.
- **Connectors** — links to Drive, email, calendars and other tools, increasingly built on **MCP**
  (the Model Context Protocol), one open standard that chat apps and coding agents share. Connect a
  source once and the assistant pulls live data instead of you pasting exports. Building your own
  connections is the → mcp-servers lesson.
- **Images and files** — all three read screenshots, photos and scans. Under the hood an image is
  tokenized like text, so it isn't free in an app you build, and vision misreads small text and
  numbers, so verify any figure it extracts (token mechanics → tokens-and-context).

**If you build software:** chat apps are for feeling out a model. Before you ship a prompt inside
your own app, test it in the provider's workbench (Google AI Studio, the OpenAI Playground, the
Anthropic Console), where you can set a schema for structured output and see the token count per
run. That's where a prompt regression gets caught on your machine instead of in a user's hands.

## Common failure modes

- **You use one assistant for everything and blame "AI" when it struggles** → one tool's weak
  spot became your weak spot → bake off the jobs that frustrate you most.
- **You judge by which answer sounds best** → fluent beats correct in a skim → write two or three
  criteria before reading, and test against real data.
- **Your routing table is a year old** → the lead has changed since → re-run one bake-off per row
  every few months, or when a big release lands.
- **A great answer can't be reproduced next week** → it lived in a one-off chat → save the prompt
  and the winning setup in a Project, Gem or notes file.

## The habit: a dispatch board, not a favorite

You don't marry a vendor; you keep a **routing table** across all three, measured once per job
class and re-checked as they leapfrog. Feature tours expire in weeks; routing judgment, standing
workspaces, connectors and a healthy suspicion of extracted numbers don't. That's the
vendor-neutral core of this pillar, and it's why "which would you use, and why?" is a question
you'll answer with evidence instead of a brand.
