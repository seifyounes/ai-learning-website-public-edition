Meetings, inboxes and long documents are where knowledge work quietly drowns. An assistant can
turn an hour-long transcript into decisions and action items, draft the reply you've been putting
off, and answer questions about an eighty-page policy in seconds. That pays off every week. It
also carries one specific risk: **a summary reads as complete even when it isn't**, and the parts
it drops are often the ones that matter. This lesson gives you the speed and a way to check it.

## From transcript to decisions, owners and dates

A useful meeting summary isn't a shorter version of the meeting; it's a different document. Ask for
fixed headings: **decisions, action items (task, owner, due date), open questions, and caveats and
disagreements.** Two instructions make it trustworthy:

- **Quote the evidence.** Each decision gets a short quote from the transcript, so you can find it.
- **Don't fill gaps.** "No owner named" and "no date given" are correct answers. A guessed owner
  is how someone gets assigned work they never agreed to.

Transcripts have their own errors: misheard names, speakers mixed up, crosstalk lost. Check the
owner names especially.

## Why summaries drop caveats, and how to check

Summarising means deciding what to cut, and models tend to cut **hedges**: "if", "probably",
"pending", "one person disagreed". A tentative plan reads as a decision, and a lone objection
disappears. Long transcripts make it worse, because material in the middle of a long input gets
less attention than the start and end (→ tokens-and-context).

The checks are quick: ask for a **caveats and disagreements** section by name; ask for quotes
behind every decision; read one stretch from the middle of the meeting yourself; and send the
summary to attendees as a draft ("correct anything I've got wrong") rather than as the record.

## Replies in your tone

For email, give the assistant three things: **the thread, what you want the reply to do** in one or
two lines ("decline politely, offer next month instead"), and **two or three emails you've written**
as tone samples. Then read the draft for commitments. Assistants fill gaps with plausible promises
such as refunds, deadlines and apologies that admit fault, and once you hit send, they're yours.
Never let a tool send email unread.

## Long documents: answers you can trace

For a long document, use a **source-grounded** setup: the assistant answers only from files you
supply and shows where each answer came from. Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) is built
around this, with clickable citations, as the video shows. A ChatGPT or Claude Project with the file
uploaded works similarly if you ask it to quote the passage behind each answer. Ask for conditions
and exceptions explicitly, and include a question you suspect the document doesn't answer: a good
setup says so instead of inventing a plausible rule (→ verification-and-grounding).

## How the three differ

**Gemini** is woven into Google's apps: it summarises threads and drafts replies in Gmail, works
in Docs, and Google Meet can take notes on some plans. **ChatGPT** and **Claude** work best with what
you paste or upload, inside Projects, and both can connect to email and drive accounts on some
plans. Zoom, Meet and Microsoft Teams also offer built-in AI notes. Features and plans change
often, so check the current docs, and remember that connecting your inbox lets the assistant read
all of it (→ privacy-and-setup).

> 💡 **Try it:** take a summary you've already received from any AI notetaker and search the
> transcript for "if", "unless" and "depends". Check whether each condition survived into the
> summary.

## Worked example: a 40-minute project meeting

A team lead pastes the transcript and asks for the four headings. The summary lists three
decisions; the quote for one of them turns out to be someone *proposing* it, so it moves to open
questions. One action item has "no date given", and the lead adds a date in the follow-up rather
than inventing one. The caveats section catches "only if the budget is approved", which the
first, unstructured summary had dropped. The follow-up email goes out as a draft for corrections,
and one attendee fixes an owner name the transcript had misheard.

## Common failure modes

- **A condition became a decision** → hedges compressed away → require a caveats section and
  quotes per decision.
- **The wrong person owns a task** → transcription mixed up speakers, or the model guessed →
  allow "no owner named" and confirm owners with attendees.
- **A reply promises something you can't deliver** → the model filled your silence → state what you
  can offer and check every commitment.
- **A document answer is right but incomplete** → the exception was elsewhere in the text → ask for
  conditions explicitly and read the cited paragraph.

## Consent, and what to keep out

**Record only with consent.** Say at the start what's recorded, why and who sees the notes; stop if
anyone objects. Laws differ by country and region, and some require every participant's consent,
so check yours and your organization's policy. Don't add an AI notetaker to someone else's
meeting without the host's agreement. Treat transcripts as records: store them carefully and
delete what you don't need.

Some things shouldn't go to an assistant at all, or need an approved tool: HR, medical and legal
conversations, and anything under a confidentiality agreement. And a summary is never a substitute
for reading a contract or policy before you sign it or enforce it.
