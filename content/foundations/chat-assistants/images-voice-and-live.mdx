Every assistant you use now takes more than typed text. You can photograph a whiteboard, paste
a screenshot, talk instead of type, and in some apps point your phone camera at a problem and
discuss it live. These inputs save real time, but they add a new way to be wrong (the model
misreads what it sees) and a new way to overshare (a photo or a voice carries more than you
meant). This lesson covers both.

## When showing beats typing

Reach for an image when describing the thing would take longer than showing it: a whiteboard
after a lecture or meeting, a chart in a report, an error message, handwritten notes, a form
you don't understand, a product label. Reach for voice when talking is faster or is the point:
dictating a long prompt, rehearsing an interview or presentation, practising a language,
thinking aloud on a walk. Live camera and screen sharing help when the problem is physical or
on screen and you want to talk it through as you go.

## How the big three differ

All three read images you upload or paste. Beyond that they differ, and features move fast, so
check the current docs:

- **ChatGPT** reads images, generates them, and has dictation plus a voice mode with several
  options. Its newest option, Live, doesn't do camera or screen sharing; the older Advanced
  option does, in the mobile app.
- **Claude** reads images but doesn't generate photos or illustrations; it can draw diagrams
  and charts as interactive visuals. Its voice mode is in beta on all plans, on mobile, desktop
  and web. Its help pages describe spoken conversation, not a live camera feed.
- **Gemini** reads and generates images, and Gemini Live adds voice conversation with camera
  and screen sharing in the mobile apps.

Availability varies by plan and region; check current docs before you rely on a mode.

## Worked example: a whiteboard photo you can trust

1. **Take a good photo.** Straight on, in good light, cropped to the board. Glare, angle and
   distance cause most misreadings.
2. **Ask for a transcription only**, with unclear parts marked [?]. No summary yet.
3. **Check it against the board.** Numbers, signs, subscripts and names are where models slip.
   Correct anything wrong in the chat.
4. **Now ask your real question**: clean notes, a worked explanation, practice questions.
5. **Keep the corrected text**, not just the photo, in your notes.

The same steps work for a chart: ask for the values as a table first. Models often estimate
bar heights rather than read them, and may fill a blurry label with a plausible number. When
you have the underlying spreadsheet or PDF, upload that instead (→ analyze-files-and-data).

> 💡 **Try it:** screenshot a table or chart you've been sent this week, paste it into any
> assistant and ask for every number as a table with unclear values marked [?]. Count how many
> it got exactly right.

## Voice for practice

Voice mode turns an assistant into a patient rehearsal partner. Brief it in text first (the
scenario, its role, one question at a time, how to give feedback) and then talk. After the run,
read the feedback and transcript in text, where it's easier to see patterns. It works well for
interview answers, presentations, and speaking practice in a language you're learning, where you
can ask it to slow down, switch level or repeat a phrase. It's a practice partner, not a judge:
its feedback can be generic, so ask for specifics quoted from what you said.

## Generating images honestly

Image generation suits illustrations, mock-ups, icons and slides. The rules are simple: never
present a generated image as a real photo of a real person, place, product or event; don't
create fake documents, receipts or screenshots; don't make someone's likeness without their
consent; and check any text in the image, which is often misspelled. For brand work and
consistency, see → images-brand-assets.

## Privacy: photos and voices carry more than you think

A photo can include faces, names on a whiteboard, an address on an envelope, or a phone
notification. Screen sharing shows whatever is open. Voice sessions are usually saved as
transcripts in your chat history. So crop before you upload, share one window rather than the
whole screen, ask permission before including other people in a live session, and review or
delete transcripts you don't need. The training and retention settings from → privacy-and-setup
apply to images and audio too.

## Common failure modes

- **A number in the answer is wrong** → the model misread the image → transcribe first and check.
- **Small text comes back invented** → resolution too low → crop to the region, or paste the text.
- **Voice mode keeps cutting you off** → background noise or long pauses → use headphones or
  push-to-talk where the app offers it.
- **Practice feedback is vague** → no feedback method given → name one (STAR, three strengths
  and three fixes) in the brief.
- **A shared screen exposed a message** → notifications were on → silence them before sharing.

## What not to use it for

Don't treat a reading of a medical result, a medicine label or an electrical diagram as the final
word; use it to prepare questions for a professional. Don't use a live camera during an exam or
assessment that bans AI. Don't photograph or record people who haven't agreed, or try to
identify strangers from photos. Seeing and hearing make the assistant more useful; they don't
make it more reliable, so the checking habit from → verification-and-grounding still applies.
