Writing is where most people first use a chat assistant, and where most people use it worst:
"write me an email about X" produces something fluent, generic and slightly off. The skill in this
lesson is the reverse. **You keep the thinking (the point, the argument, the facts) and hand the
assistant the friction**: critiquing an outline, catching unclear sentences, matching a tone. It
pays off on anything you write repeatedly or anything that matters: reports, proposals, cover
letters, essays, long emails.

## Outline first, then draft

The most useful prompt in writing comes before any prose: **write the outline yourself**, five to
eight bullets with your point and the order you'll make it in, then ask the assistant to critique
it. "What's missing? What would a sceptical reader object to? Which bullet is doing two jobs?"
This is where AI saves the most time, because a structural problem found in an outline costs one
minute to fix and a structural problem found in a finished draft costs an hour.

Then draft from your outline, typed or dictated, rough is fine. If you let the assistant write the
first draft from a one-line request, it has to invent the argument, and you end up editing its
thinking instead of yours.

## Edit in passes, one dimension at a time

"Improve this" asks for everything at once, so you get a rewrite you can't evaluate. Separate passes
work better:

1. **Structure:** does each paragraph do one job, in the right order? Ask for a reverse outline:
   one line per paragraph saying what it does.
2. **Clarity:** which sentences would a newcomer misread? Ask it to quote them and say why.
3. **Tone:** does it sound like you, and right for this reader? This pass needs your voice
   (next section).

Ask for a **list of changes** with each pass, and accept them one at a time.

## Teach it your voice with examples

Descriptions like "friendly but professional" barely move the output. **Examples do.** Paste two
to five samples of your own writing and ask the assistant to describe how you write, with an
example and a counter-example for each rule, which is the method in the video. Save the result in
a Project or Gem so you don't rebuild it every time, and add a rule whenever you catch a sentence
that doesn't sound like you. This is the general prompting principle of showing rather than
telling (→ prompting-in-2026), applied to style.

## Feedback versus rewrite

Decide which you want before you ask, and say it in the prompt:

- **Feedback** ("diagnose, don't rewrite; quote the problem and explain it") keeps every word
  yours. It's the right default for coursework, anything you'll be assessed on, and anything you
  want to get better at writing.
- **Rewrite** ("rewrite this paragraph for a non-expert, then list what you changed") is fine for
  low-stakes text where you only need the result, such as a routine notice or a first pass at a
  product description, as long as you read it all and stand behind it.

## How the three assistants handle it

All three take samples, follow style notes and keep them in a reusable space: **Projects** in
ChatGPT and Claude, **Gems** in Gemini. The differences are in the editing surface. ChatGPT and
Gemini each have a **Canvas** side panel where you can edit a document alongside the chat and ask
for changes to a selected passage. Claude offers saved writing **styles** you can build from your
samples, and Gemini also sits inside Google Docs and Gmail for in-place help. These features move
quickly, so check the current docs before you build a habit around one. On tone, people often find
the three differ in their defaults, so if your first choice keeps sounding wrong after you've
given it samples, try the same prompt in another (→ big-three-compared).

> 💡 **Try it:** paste a paragraph you wrote and ask "Give me a reverse outline: one line per
> sentence saying what it does. Don't change anything." Any sentence whose line says "repeats" or
> "unclear" is your first edit.

## Worked example: a project update email

Say you need to tell a team that a deadline is moving.

1. **Your outline:** what's moving and to when; why, in one sentence; what it changes for each
   person; what you need from them by Friday.
2. **Critique:** the assistant points out that readers will ask whether the budget changes too.
   You add a bullet.
3. **Your draft**, written fast from the outline.
4. **Clarity pass:** it flags "pushed to the right" as jargon and a sentence with two dates in it.
5. **Tone pass** against your style notes: it suggests cutting the apology from three sentences to
   one. You agree with two of its four changes.

Ten minutes, and the email says what you meant, in your words.

## Common failure modes

- **Everything sounds like a brochure** → no voice samples, open-ended request → give samples and
  ask for one pass at a time.
- **Your point quietly changes** → the rewrite optimised for smoothness → ask for feedback only,
  then compare your one-sentence point with its summary.
- **A statistic appears that you never wrote** → the model filled a gap with something plausible →
  ban new claims in edit passes, and check or delete every added fact (→ verification-and-grounding).
- **You can't explain a sentence when asked** → you accepted a rewrite unread → accept changes one
  at a time, and only ones you could defend.

## What not to hand over

Three things stay yours. **The argument:** if the assistant decides what you think, the writing
can't help you think. **The integrity call:** students, follow your course's AI rules exactly;
they vary from banned to required-with-disclosure, and when unsure, ask. Never submit text you
couldn't explain line by line, and disclose AI use where it's expected. At work, check whether
clients or your employer have a policy too. **The facts:** anything the assistant adds is a claim
to check, not a source. And keep confidential drafts out of consumer accounts (→ privacy-and-setup).
