  one of your courses or an "email reply drafter" for one recurring kind of email) in a ChatGPT

  1. **Write the standing instructions** with all five parts:

  ```prompt
  Role: You are [role] helping [who] with [the one job].
  Audience: Outputs are read by [who], who [what they know / need].
  Format: Always return [structure, length, headings, tone]. Example of a good output: [short example].
  Refuse or flag: Don't [out-of-scope things]. If asked, say so and suggest [alternative].
  Ask before guessing: If information you need isn't in my message or the files, ask me one
  question instead of assuming. When you use a file, name it.
  ```

  2. **Add 1–3 clean reference files** (a style guide, a syllabus, a template, a price list),
     text-forward, one version each.
  3. **Write six test questions** before you try it: two normal requests, one with missing
     information, one out of scope, one the files can't answer, and one that asks it to ignore
     its rules.
  4. **Run all six**, note which failed, edit the instructions, and rerun until five of six pass.
  5. **Open the sharing settings** and confirm who can see it and its files. Leave it private
     unless you meant otherwise.

If you paste the same background into a new chat every week ("I'm a second-year nursing
student, use British spelling, format it like this…"), you're doing by hand what a reusable
assistant does for you. You write the standing instructions once, attach the files it should
draw on, and every chat in it starts briefed. This is the no-code cousin of
→ context-engineering: you decide once what the model sees in every conversation.

## When a reusable assistant beats a fresh chat

Build one when a job is repeated, needs consistent output, or is used by more than one person:
a study-guide maker for one course, a reply drafter for one kind of customer email, a report
summariser that follows your team's format. Use a fresh chat for one-offs, and for anything
where yesterday's context would mislead today's answer. Good candidates often come from the
writing and meeting routines in → writing-with-ai and → meetings-email-documents.

## Three shapes, and what each vendor calls them

The names changed in late 2026, so this is the picture as of October 2026; check the current
docs.

- **Projects: a place you go.** A ChatGPT Project or a Claude Project holds instructions, files
  and the chats you have inside it. ChatGPT's project instructions override your general custom
  instructions, and projects can be shared, including on the free plan. Claude Projects are
  available on free accounts (up to five); on paid plans, large knowledge bases switch to
  retrieval, and sharing is limited to Team and Enterprise organisations.
- **Skills: instructions that come to you.** A skill is a packaged set of instructions, with
  optional files, that you call into any chat. Google is replacing Gems with skills in Gemini
  (personal accounts from November 2026, work and school accounts in 2027): you type "/" and the
  skill's name, can combine several, and Gemini may apply one on its own. Claude also has skills,
  and OpenAI's replacement for Custom GPTs, plugins, bundles skills with reference files and
  connected apps.
- **Standalone custom bots: being retired.** Custom GPTs and Gems were separate, named assistants.
  Personal ChatGPT accounts can no longer create new GPTs, and OpenAI has said GPTs will be
  retired in favour of plugins; Gems are migrating to skills. If you rely on one, copy its
  instructions and files out now.

## Writing standing instructions

Good instructions cover five things:

1. **Role**: who the assistant is and the one job it does.
2. **Audience**: who reads the output and what they already know.
3. **Format**: structure, length and tone, with one short example of a good output.
4. **Refusal rules**: what's out of scope and what to say instead.
5. **Ask before guessing**: when information is missing, ask one question rather than invent.

Put rules in the instructions and reference material in the files. Keep instructions short
enough to read in a minute; a long list of prohibitions works worse than clear positive
directions with an example (→ prompting-in-2026).

## Knowledge files: retrieval, not memorisation

Uploading a file doesn't teach the assistant its contents. For large files it searches for the
passages that look relevant to each question and reads those; for small ones it may read them
whole. Either way it can miss the right passage, mix two versions, or fill a gap with something
plausible. So: one current version of each file, clear headings, text rather than scans, only
the spreadsheet tab you need, and an instruction to name the file and section it used.

## Worked example: a study-guide maker

A student builds one for a statistics course:

1. **Job card:** "Turns one week's lecture notes into a one-page guide plus five exam-style
   questions."
2. **Instructions:** role (study coach for this course), audience (a student who attended but
   is rusty), format (one page, key formulas in a table, questions last), refusal rule (won't
   write graded coursework), ask before guessing (if the notes skip a step, ask).
3. **Files:** the syllabus and one past exam paper, so questions match the real format.
4. **Test:** a normal week, a week with gaps in the notes, a request to write the graded
   assignment, a question about a topic not on the syllabus. The gap test failed (it filled the
   missing step itself), so they made the ask-before-guessing rule more explicit and reran it.
5. **Share:** kept private; classmates get a copy of the instructions instead of access to the
   student's files.

> 💡 **Try it:** open any reusable assistant you already use and ask it something its files can't
> answer. Does it say so, or does it invent? That one test tells you whether it needs an
> ask-before-guessing rule.

## Test before you share

Six tricky questions catch most problems: two normal, one with missing information, one out of
scope, one the files can't answer, and one asking it to ignore its rules. Rerun them after every
edit. It's the same idea engineers formalise as evals (→ evals-and-testing).

## Sharing and privacy

Assume anyone you share with can read the instructions and pull content out of the files, and
choose the narrowest audience and lowest permission. Don't put other people's personal data,
client documents or anything confidential into an assistant you'll share (→ privacy-and-setup).

## Common failure modes

- **Answers ignore the file** → retrieval missed it → clearer headings, name the section.
- **It invents missing details** → no ask-before-guessing rule → add it and test.
- **Output drifts from the format** → no example → paste a short model answer.
- **It worked last month, not now** → model or instructions changed → rerun your test sheet.

## What not to build

Don't build an assistant that gives final medical, legal, tax or financial advice to others
without an expert checking it, one that does graded work your course bans, or one that holds
data you aren't allowed to share. A reusable assistant repeats its mistakes as consistently as
its strengths.
