Here's the thing: **you already live inside trigger→action.** You pay in an online shop and a
receipt lands in your inbox. Someone pushes code and a deploy starts. A meeting is ten minutes
away and your phone buzzes. Each of those is automation — a trigger fired, actions ran. This
pillar gives the pattern a name and puts it in your hands for everything else: **"When _this_
happens, do _that_."**

## Trigger → action

- **Trigger** — the event that starts it: a form submission, a new email, a
  scheduled time, a new row in a sheet.
- **Action(s)** — what happens next: send a message, create a record, call an API,
  update a doc. One trigger can fan out to many actions.

Between the two, most tools let you add **filters** ("only if the amount is over 100") and
**branches** ("if it's a bug, go left; otherwise go right"). Those are still rules you wrote in
advance. Zapier, Make, and n8n (→ zapier-make-n8n) are all just visual ways to connect triggers
to actions across the apps you already use.

## The real skill: what to automate

Tools are easy; judgment is the value. Automate tasks that are:

- **Repetitive** — you do them again and again.
- **Rule-based** — clear "if this, then that" logic, no real judgment.
- **Frequent** — the time saved compounds.

And *don't* (yet) automate rare one-offs or genuine judgment calls — the build cost
outweighs the payoff, or the risk is too high.

The ROI math, worked once so it's real: say summarizing new App Store reviews takes you
**10 minutes a day** (~70 min/week ≈ **60 hours/year**). Building the automation (review
feed → AI summary → notification) takes ~3 hours once, plus maybe 3 hours/year of babysitting.
60 hours saved vs 6 spent — a 10× return, so build it. Counter-example: automating a
quarterly report that takes 20 minutes (80 min/year) can't repay even one 3-hour build.
Run **time saved × frequency − build cost** on every candidate; the arithmetic makes the
decision for you.

## Worked example: auditing one week

Take a hypothetical operations assistant at a small design studio. She lists five things she did
more than twice last week, then scores each one.

1. **Copying website enquiries into the client sheet.** About 5 minutes each, roughly 15 a week:
   75 minutes a week, about 65 hours a year. Pure rules (copy these fields into those columns).
   Build time: about 2 hours. **Clear winner.**
2. **Renaming and filing supplier invoices.** About 2 minutes each, 20 a week: 40 minutes a
   week, about 35 hours a year. Rule-based once she writes the naming convention down. **Second.**
3. **The Friday status email.** 30 minutes a week, about 26 hours a year. The *gathering*
   (pulling numbers from the tracker) is rules; the *writing* needs a little judgment. **Automate
   the gathering now,** and add an AI drafting step later (→ ai-in-automations).
4. **The annual budget review.** A few hours, once a year. Even a quick build can't repay itself,
   and it's mostly judgment. **Stays manual.**
5. **Replying to unhappy clients.** Frequent, but every reply is a judgment call with a
   relationship at stake. **Stays human.** At most, an automation can collect the history so she
   starts each reply with context.

Notice what happened: the task that annoyed her most (the Friday email) came third, and the
boring one she barely thought about came first. Also notice item 3. **Partial automation
counts.** You rarely need to automate a whole job; automating the copy-paste part and keeping
the thinking part human is often the best deal.

> 💡 **Try it:** Open your sent email and calendar from last week and find three things you did
> more than twice. Write each as "When ___ happens, I ___." Five minutes, and you have the start
> of a backlog.

## Hidden costs to count

The formula has a build-cost term, and beginners underestimate it in two ways:

- **Upkeep.** Apps change their fields, passwords and access tokens expire, someone renames a
  folder. A flow that saves 60 hours a year might need a few hours a year of fixes.
- **Process debt.** If the manual process is messy (three people do it three ways), automating it
  just runs the mess faster. Write the steps down first and simplify them; sometimes that alone
  removes a chunk of the work before you build anything.

## Common failure modes

- **Automating chaos** → the flow breaks on every exception → the process was never agreed on
  → write the steps on paper and settle the edge cases before you build.
- **Picking by irritation** → a fun build that saves 20 minutes a year → emotion chose, not
  numbers → score every candidate with the formula before opening a tool.
- **Forgetting upkeep** → a flow quietly fails for weeks → no owner, no alert → log each flow's
  owner and add an error notification (→ reliability).
- **Automating the judgment too** → an embarrassing auto-reply to a sensitive message → the
  human step was removed along with the busywork → automate the prep and keep the decision.
- **Building version three first** → a week spent on branches before anything runs → scope
  creep → ship the simplest trigger→action, then add branches as real cases appear.

## Why this pillar matters for you

Whatever you do — study, build software, run a team, freelance — you're surrounded by
repetitive glue work: onboarding, follow-ups, posting, reporting. Each one is a trigger-action
sentence waiting to be written. Get the mindset now; the next lessons give you the tools, then
we add AI so your automations can handle judgment too.
