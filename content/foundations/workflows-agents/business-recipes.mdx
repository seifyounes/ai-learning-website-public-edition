You've got the whole toolkit now — triggers, tools, AI steps, agents, reliability.
This last lesson is a **recipe book**: the automations actually worth building,
chosen because they save real hours and (bonus) can be **sold** as services.

## High-ROI recipes to steal

- **Lead capture → enrich → follow-up.** A new lead triggers an AI-drafted, tailored
  reply and a CRM entry. Never let a lead go cold.
- **Client onboarding.** On signature: create the folders, send the welcome + intake
  form, and schedule the kickoff — automatically.
- **Content repurposing.** One long video or post → AI spins out a blog, a thread,
  and captions. Multiply your output.
- **Inbox triage.** Classify incoming messages, draft replies, and flag the few that
  truly need you.
- **Weekly reporting.** Pull the metrics, have AI write the plain-English summary,
  send it to the client like clockwork.

Each one is a pattern you've already built: lead follow-up and triage are the AI step from
→ ai-in-automations, onboarding is plain trigger→action, and reporting adds a schedule.

## Worked example: the weekly report, built to reuse

Here's the weekly report as a recipe you could hand to someone else.

1. **Trigger:** a schedule, Monday 8:00.
2. **Settings block:** one step at the top holding everything client-specific: the sheet's
   location, the metric names, the recipient, the report's tone. Nothing else in the flow
   mentions a client by name.
3. **Fetch:** read the last 14 days of rows from the sheet or export.
4. **Compute:** a normal step (a formula, a Code node, or a summarise step) calculates this
   week's totals, last week's totals and the change for each metric. **The model never does the
   arithmetic.**
5. **AI step:** the prompt from "Do this now" receives the computed numbers and writes five plain
   lines.
6. **Check:** a small step confirms that every number in the AI's text appears in the computed
   input. If not, send the plain numbers with a note instead of the prose.
7. **Deliver:** to you for approval in week one; straight to the recipient once you trust it.
8. **Guards:** an error workflow and a heartbeat from → reliability.

Step 4 and step 6 are what separate a recipe from a demo. A language model is good at phrasing
"orders fell slightly while refunds doubled" and unreliable at computing percentages, so you
give it the computed numbers to phrase and check its work afterwards.

> inputs, the AI prompt (if any), output, and how to switch it off. If you can't fill a line,
> that's the gap that would make it hard to reuse.

## Build once, reuse forever

The point of a *recipe* is reuse. Build each as a template, then redeploy across
clients with small tweaks. Your effort compounds: the onboarding flow you build for
one client serves the next ten — **if** the client-specific parts live in one settings block
and nowhere else.

## These are also your offers

Here's the strategic kicker if you freelance, consult, or plan to: **every recipe above is a
sellable service.** Build them for yourself to learn the craft, then package them —
"AI lead-response setup," "automated onboarding," "content-repurposing engine." That
hands you straight into the **Applications → Agency / freelance** track later in the
course (→ productized-services). If you're employed, the same recipes are internal wins with
your name on them.

## Common failure modes

- **Every new client is a rebuild** → client details are scattered through the flow → no
  settings block → move everything client-specific into one step at the top.
- **The report states wrong numbers** → the model did the maths → arithmetic was left to the
  AI → compute first, let the AI phrase, then check.
- **Bugs reach the client first** → you sold it before running it → no time on your own data
  → run each recipe on your own work for a few weeks.
- **Unpaid upkeep** → logins expire and APIs change after handover → maintenance wasn't part
  of the deal → price a monthly maintenance line, or name an internal owner.
- **Five half-finished recipes** → none reliable enough to sell → too many at once → take one
  to "boring and dependable" before starting the next.
