**You need:** no new maths. The gradient-descent loop from → calculus-for-optimization and the
noise-band idea from → probability-statistics-for-ai are enough; everything else here is
counting and percentages.

Before transformers, before RAG, there is one recipe, and every AI system you'll ever ship,
including the LLM APIs you call, came out of it. Learn the recipe once and a hundred buzzwords
collapse into three questions: *what's the data, what's the loss, does it generalize?*

## The recipe

**Supervised learning:** inputs → labels, a **loss** measuring wrongness, and an optimizer
grinding the loss down (→ calculus-for-optimization). A spam filter, a churn model and LLM
pretraining are all this recipe. Pretraining just uses huge amounts of text as data and "the
next token" as the label, at a scale that turned the recipe into general capability.

## Generalization is the only thing that matters

A model that aces its training data has proven nothing; maybe it **memorized**. So we split:
**train** (learn), **validation** (tune choices), **test** (touch once, at the end). The moment
information from test leaks into training (duplicated rows, tuning on test, eval examples
pasted into prompts) your score becomes fiction. Many embarrassing ML failures in industry are
leakage stories.

> 💡 **Try it:** in Google's MLCC overfitting widget, crank model capacity up on noisy data and
> watch the curve contort itself through every point — then watch test error climb. You now
> have a permanent mental picture of memorization vs learning.

## Worked example: a churn model, start to finish

A hypothetical subscription business has 1,000 customers; 150 cancelled last quarter. You want
to predict who cancels next.

1. **Baseline first.** Always predicting "stays" is right for 850 of 1,000: **85% accuracy**.
   Any model has to beat that to be worth anything.
2. **Split.** 800 customers to train, 200 to test, keeping the same 15% cancel rate in both
   halves.
3. **First model:** a deep decision tree. Training accuracy 99%, test accuracy 84%. It
   memorized the training customers and is *worse* than the baseline on new ones. Overfitting.
4. **A too-good second try.** Someone adds a `refund_issued` column and test accuracy jumps to
   98%. Suspicious: refunds are issued *because* people cancel. The column leaks the answer.
   Drop it.
5. **Honest model:** logistic regression without the leaky column. Training 89%, test 88%. The
   two numbers are close (no overfitting) and it beats the baseline by 3 points.
6. **Is 3 points real?** At n = 200 the rough noise band is ±1/√200 ≈ ±7 points. So the gain is
   promising, not proven. Get more test data or use cross-validation, and check how many actual
   cancellers it catches, not just overall accuracy (→ evaluation-metrics).

Nothing in that walk-through needed a large model. It needed a baseline, a clean split, a nose
for leakage and the statistics from the previous lessons.

## The map: classical ML vs foundation models

- **Structured/tabular data** (columns, numbers, categories): logistic regression, random
  forests, gradient-boosted trees. Cheap, fast, explainable, and still the industry workhorse.
- **Unstructured data** (language, images, audio): foundation models, called via API or
  fine-tuned. Training from scratch is a lab's job, not yours.
- **Your role:** rarely *train*, constantly *adapt and evaluate*. Prompting, retrieval, tools and
  fine-tuning are all ways to steer someone else's trained recipe. The judgment lives in
  choosing the cheapest thing that works, which is often NOT the LLM.

## Bias–variance in one breath

Simple models err by being too rigid (**bias**, underfitting); flexible models err by chasing
noise (**variance**, overfitting). More data reduces variance; more capacity reduces bias.
Regularization tricks such as early stopping, dropout and weight decay are variance dials.

## Common failure modes

- **The test score is far better than real-world results** → leakage (a column known only after
  the outcome, duplicates across splits, or a random split on time-ordered data) → drop
  post-outcome columns, dedupe, and split by time when the data has dates.
- **High accuracy, useless model** → it just predicts the majority class → compare against the
  baseline every time and read per-class metrics.
- **Great on training data, poor on test** → overfitting → simpler model, regularization or more
  data.
- **The test set slowly stops meaning anything** → you kept tuning against it, so it became
  training signal → make choices on the validation set and touch test once.
- **An LLM bill for a spreadsheet problem** → the tool boundary was skipped → try boosted trees
  or logistic regression first and make the LLM beat them.

## Why this pillar exists

This is the boundary-drawing lesson: it tells you when an LLM is the wrong tool, why eval
hygiene is non-negotiable, and what "the model learned" actually means. That judgment, not
framework trivia, is what separates an applied AI engineer from an API consumer.
