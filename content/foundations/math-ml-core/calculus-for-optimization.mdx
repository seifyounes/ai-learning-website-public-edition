**You need:** the idea of slope from high-school algebra, and vectors from
→ linear-algebra-for-ai. Derivatives and the chain rule help, but this lesson rebuilds the only
two you need.

If you've met derivatives and the chain rule, this lesson is the AI re-read; if not, the one
idea you need is "slope". Either way: **training is calculus in a loop**, and the three knobs on
every fine-tune dashboard are direct consequences of it.

## The derivative you already know, renamed

A model's **loss** is one number measuring how wrong it currently is. Every weight has a
partial derivative: *if I nudge this weight, how does the loss move?* Collect all of them and
you have the **gradient**, the direction of steepest increase. Training's entire idea:

```
w ← w − learning_rate × ∇loss(w)
```

Step downhill. Repeat a few billion times. Everything else is engineering around this line.

## Worked example: train a one-weight model by hand

A model predicts `prediction = w × x`. You have one training example: x = 2, the right answer
y = 6 (so the perfect weight is 3). Use squared error as the loss: `loss = (w × x − y)²`.

1. **Chain rule for the gradient.** The loss is (something)², and that something is w × x − y.
   The derivative of the outside is 2 × (something); the derivative of the inside with respect
   to w is x. Multiply them: `gradient = 2 × (w × x − y) × x`. With x = 2 and y = 6, that's
   `8w − 24`.
2. **Start at w = 0, learning rate 0.05.** Gradient = −24, so w becomes 0 − 0.05 × (−24) =
   **1.2**. Next step: gradient = 8 × 1.2 − 24 = −14.4, so w = 1.2 + 0.72 = **1.92**. Then
   **2.35**, then about **2.61**. Each step closes 40% of the remaining gap to 3.
3. **Now try learning rate 0.3.** Gradient = −24, so w jumps to **7.2**, past 3. Next gradient
   = 33.6, so w = 7.2 − 10.08 = **−2.88**. Then about 11.2. The error grows by 1.4× every step,
   flipping sign each time. That's the "loss exploded to NaN" story at the size of one number.

For this loss each step multiplies the error by (1 − 8 × lr), so anything above lr = 0.25
diverges. Real models have billions of weights and no tidy formula, which is why learning-rate
choice is found by trial runs and schedules rather than solved.

## The learning rate is the whole game

Too small → crawling, and training costs explode. Too large → overshoot, oscillate, NaN.
Schedules (warmup, decay) exist because the right step size changes as you descend. When a
fine-tune misbehaves, **LR is the first suspect**.

> 💡 **Try it:** sketch a U-shaped valley on paper. Draw the update steps for a small LR (short
> hops down one wall) and a huge LR (leaping across the valley to a HIGHER point on the other
> wall, then higher again). You've just drawn every "my loss exploded" incident ever filed.

## Backprop = chain rule, industrialized

A network is a giant composition of functions. The **chain rule** lets you peel derivatives
through a composition, exactly as in step 1 above. Backpropagation applies it from the loss
backwards through every layer, reusing intermediate results so the whole gradient costs about
one extra forward pass. No magic; the only surprise is that it's *cheap*.

## Reading loss curves like an engineer

- Loss flat from the start → LR too small, or data/labels broken.
- Loss oscillates or explodes → LR too high.
- Train ↓ while validation ↑ → overfitting; stop early or regularize.
- Both plateau above target → model/data capacity problem, not an optimizer problem.

## Common failure modes

- **Loss turns NaN after a few hundred steps** → LR too high, often with no warmup → cut the
  LR 3–10×, add warmup, and turn on gradient clipping if your tool offers it.
- **Loss never moves** → LR far too small, or the labels are missing or masked out → run a short
  LR sweep, and print five training examples exactly as the model sees them.
- **The fine-tune "worked" but the model got worse at everything else** → too high an LR or too
  many epochs on narrow data, so it overwrote general skills → lower the LR, run fewer epochs,
  use LoRA, or mix some general data back in.
- **A run that worked breaks after you change the batch size** → the effective step size changed
  → retune the learning rate whenever you change batch size.

## Why this pillar exists

You will probably never implement backprop, but you WILL set learning rates, read loss
curves, choose epochs, and explain to a stakeholder why fine-tuning costs what it costs.
Optimization is where the money meets the math.
