The pillar ends where your job begins: **measurement**. You've seen evals as noisy samples;
this lesson is the metric toolbox itself: classical classification metrics that never stopped
being the backbone, plus the LLM-era additions layered on top of them.

## The confusion matrix — four boxes, everything follows

Any yes/no decision (guardrail, spam, hallucination flag) lands in one of four boxes: true
positive, false positive, false negative, true negative. Every metric is arithmetic on these:

- **Accuracy:** share correct overall. *Lies under class imbalance.*
- **Precision:** of everything flagged, how much was real? (false-alarm control)
- **Recall:** of everything real, how much did we catch? (miss control)
- **F1:** the harmonic mean of the two, when you must report one number.

## Worked example: one spam filter, two thresholds

A hypothetical inbox gets 10,000 emails; 500 are spam. The filter gives every email a spam
score from 0 to 1, and you choose the cutoff.

**Cutoff 0.5.** It catches 450 spam (true positives), misses 50 (false negatives), and wrongly
flags 100 real emails (false positives). The other 9,400 are true negatives.

1. Accuracy = (450 + 9,400) ÷ 10,000 = **98.5%**.
2. Precision = 450 ÷ (450 + 100) ≈ **82%**.
3. Recall = 450 ÷ 500 = **90%**.

**Cutoff 0.8.** It now catches 400 spam, misses 100, and wrongly flags only 20 real emails.

1. Accuracy = (400 + 9,480) ÷ 10,000 = **98.8%**.
2. Precision = 400 ÷ 420 ≈ **95%**.
3. Recall = 400 ÷ 500 = **80%**.

Accuracy barely moved, yet the experience changed a lot: 80 fewer real emails lost to the spam
folder, 50 more spam emails in the inbox. A filter that flags nothing at all would still score
95%. For a spam filter, losing real mail is usually the costlier mistake, so the 0.8 cutoff
wins. For a guardrail that blocks harmful replies, you'd lean the other way.

## The threshold is a product decision

Two curves chart the menu. The **ROC curve** plots true-positive rate vs false-positive rate at
every threshold; the **PR curve** plots precision vs recall. **AUC** (area under ROC)
summarizes ranking quality across all thresholds (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect separation).
Compare models by AUC; *operate* at the threshold whose error mix matches your costs.

One trap to know: **under heavy imbalance, ROC-AUC flatters.** In the example, 100
false positives out of 9,500 real emails is a false-positive *rate* of about 1%, which looks
tiny on a ROC plot even though it means 100 lost emails. With a 2–3% positive class, ROC-AUC
can look superb while precision collapses. For rare positives, read the PR curve.

> 💡 **Try it:** say this pair out loud until it's reflexive — "expensive misses → maximize
> recall; expensive false alarms → maximize precision." Now classify: airport security,
> spam filter, a chatbot's harmful-reply guardrail, a fraud-refund flagger. (Recall, precision,
> recall, depends on volume — and that "depends" is where engineering judgment lives.)

## LLM-era metrics stand on the same floor

- **Exact match / unit tests:** deterministic checks where possible; cheapest and most honest.
- **pass@k:** at least one of k samples succeeds. The gap between pass@1 and pass@k measures
  capability vs consistency, and justifies best-of-n + verifier designs.
- **LLM-as-judge:** scalable, but the judge is itself a biased classifier: position bias,
  verbosity bias, self-preference. Randomize order, control length, audit a sample by hand.
- Whatever the metric, it's still a **sample mean with error bars**.

## Common failure modes

- **A glowing dashboard and unhappy users** → accuracy reported on imbalanced data → report
  precision and recall on the class you care about.
- **A great ROC-AUC and a flood of false alarms** → rare positives make the false-positive rate
  look small → read the PR curve and the precision at your real threshold.
- **The "best" model changes every time you re-run the judge** → position or verbosity bias,
  plus small n → swap orderings, control length, and grow the sample.
- **The threshold was never chosen, just inherited** → a default 0.5 cutoff → pick it
  deliberately from the curve, based on which error costs more, and write the reason down.

## Why this pillar exists

Metrics are where math becomes management: the number you choose to optimize IS the product
behavior you'll get. Choose accuracy on an imbalanced guardrail and you'll ship a system that
catches nothing while the dashboard glows green. Choose deliberately; you now know how.
