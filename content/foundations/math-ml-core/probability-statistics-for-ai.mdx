applyIt: |
  **Any AI feature or AI-assisted workflow you run has two knobs that are pure statistics: the
  temperature you set, and the pass-rate you read off your checks.** Temperature reshapes the
  next-token distribution before sampling (softmax with a divisor); a pass-rate is a proportion
  estimate with a noise band, not a fact, so a 2-point weekly wiggle is usually the same rate
  wobbling. Take one real use: a support chatbot you're building, a script that drafts product
  descriptions, or the summaries an assistant gives you every week.

  ```prompt
  I use an LLM for [my feature or workflow]. Recommend a temperature for it (consistency vs
  surprise) with a one-line reason. Then compute the rough 95% noise band for a quality check
  that passed [e.g. 96 of 100] outputs, and show the arithmetic.
  Success: a chosen temperature with a reason, plus a ± band around the pass-rate.
  ```

  **If you code:** set the value explicitly in your API call instead of inheriting the default,
  and comment why. **If you don't code yet:** most API playgrounds (OpenAI, Google AI Studio,
  Anthropic Console) expose a temperature slider; consumer chat apps usually hide it, so note
  which of your tools let you set it at all.

**You need:** percentages, fractions and square roots. No calculus. The vector picture from
→ linear-algebra-for-ai helps you see where logits come from, but every calculation here is
plain arithmetic.

You may have met probability before: expectation, variance, Bayes. This lesson re-reads it as
the **operating manual for LLMs**: models output distributions, evals are noisy estimates, and
guardrails live or die by base rates.

## The model is a distribution, not an oracle

At every step an LLM outputs a **probability distribution over its whole vocabulary** for the
next token (softmax over logits). Generation = sample, append, repeat. Every "personality" knob
you're given is distribution surgery:

- **Temperature** divides logits before softmax — sharpens (low) or flattens (high).
- **Top-p** truncates the distribution to the smallest set of tokens covering probability p,
  then renormalizes — it cuts the weird tail without killing variety.

Same model, same prompt, different distribution shape → different behavior. That's why
"the model answered differently this time" isn't a bug; it's the definition.

> 💡 **Try it:** ask the same creative question 3 times at temperature 0 and 3 times at 1.0 in
> any playground. Watch determinism appear and disappear. You've just *seen* softmax reshaping.
> (Even at 0, some providers aren't perfectly repeatable; small variation is normal.)

## Evals are sample means — respect the noise

An eval score is a **sample mean**: an estimate of true quality from n examples. Estimates come
with error bars of roughly **±1/√n** in the worst case (a rough 95% band). n=20 → ±22 points.
n=100 → ±10. n=400 → ±5. Burn this in: **small evals cannot detect small improvements.**
Paired comparisons (both prompts on identical examples) cancel shared noise and are the
cheapest upgrade.

## Bayes: base rates run your guardrails

When the thing you're detecting is **rare**, even an accurate detector produces mostly false
alarms: the huge clean majority generates more false positives than the tiny bad minority
generates true ones. This shows up in hallucination detectors, abuse filters, alerting
thresholds and medical-style triage. Ask "what's the base rate?" before trusting any flag.

## Worked example: a flag, then a prompt change

Your team adds a hallucination detector that catches 95% of hallucinations and wrongly flags
5% of clean answers. Hallucinations are 2% of traffic. Work in counts, not formulas:

1. **Imagine 10,000 answers.** 200 are hallucinations; 9,800 are clean.
2. **True flags:** 95% of 200 = 190.
3. **False flags:** 5% of 9,800 = 490.
4. **Share of flags that are real:** 190 ÷ (190 + 490) ≈ **28%**.

So roughly seven in ten flags are false alarms, from a "95% accurate" tool. That's not a broken
detector; it's arithmetic. Your choices are a stricter threshold, a second-stage check on
flagged items, or a human review queue sized for the real flag volume.

Next, someone tweaks the answer prompt and the pass rate moves from 84% to 88% on 50 test
questions. The standard error of a proportion is √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n) = √(0.86 × 0.14 ÷ 50) ≈
0.049, about **5 points** per measurement. A 4-point gap is inside that noise. Re-run both
prompts on the same 50 questions and count only the questions where they *disagree*: if B wins
most of those, you have evidence; if it's close to even, you don't.

## Calibration: does 80% mean 80%?

A model (or judge) is **calibrated** if, among all the times it says "80% confident", it's right
about 80% of the time. LLM judges and stated confidence scores are often *miscalibrated*,
usually overconfident. Never treat a stated confidence as a probability until you've checked it
against outcomes.

## Common failure modes

- **"B is better" after a handful of runs** → the noise band is wider than the gap → compute
  ±1/√n before you look at the winner; grow n or pair the comparison.
- **A dashboard that "regresses" every other week** → reading sampling noise as signal → pool
  weeks, plot the rate with a band, and investigate only moves outside it.
- **A guardrail muted after one noisy week** → the base rate was ignored → measure what share of
  flags are real on live traffic, then tune the threshold or add a second check.
- **The eval stays green while users complain** → the eval set was sampled from old or
  different traffic (distribution shift) → refresh it from recent real inputs on a schedule.
- **Trusting "I'm 90% sure" from a model** → miscalibration → bucket stated confidences and
  compare each bucket with how often it was actually right.

## Why this pillar exists

Sampling explains generation knobs, standard error explains when eval gaps are real, Bayes
explains guardrail false-positive floods, and calibration explains why "the judge said 9/10"
needs auditing. Statistics isn't a prerequisite here; it's the job.
