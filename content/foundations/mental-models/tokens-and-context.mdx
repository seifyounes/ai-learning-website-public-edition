applyIt: |
  Any prompt you run over and over — the prompt behind an AI feature, a support bot, a report you
  generate every week — is a context budget paid on every call. Four levers from this lesson decide
  that bill:

  - **Pack only what the call needs.** The instructions, the user's data and any retrieved
    documents all count toward the window *and* the per-call bill, multiplied by every user and
    every run.
  - **Don't call when you don't have to.** Cache the answer for inputs you've already seen, and
    skip the model entirely when a plain rule already knows the answer (an empty cart, a week with
    nothing to summarize). You don't pay for tokens you don't send.
  - **Bound the output.** A structured-output schema or a hard length limit isn't only for clean
    JSON: it caps the *output* tokens, which are usually the pricier direction of the bill.
  - **Mind the middle.** Put the rule that matters most at the top or the very end, not buried
    mid-prompt, or it can quietly stop being followed.

  whether it applies and what you'd change. Mark the lever that would save the most.

Two ideas explain half your future "why did the model do that?" moments:
**tokens** and the **context window**. They're not trivia — they're the physical
limits every model works inside, and they quietly set both what the model can
attend to and what you pay.

## Tokens: the unit the model actually sees

A model never reads your letters or your words. It reads **tokens** — the chunks a
*tokenizer* carves your text into before anything else happens. Common words are
usually one token; rare or long ones shatter into pieces, and every space and
punctuation mark counts too.

```diagram
"the"
  -> the
     (1 token)
"tokenization"
  -> token · ization
     (2 tokens, 1 word)
"strawberry"
  -> str · aw · berry
     (3 tokens, not 10
      letters)

1,000 tokens ≈ 750 words
```

(Splits from OpenAI's GPT-4 tokenizer. Every model family has its own tokenizer, so the exact
pieces differ, but they're always chunks of the text itself, never "meaning units".)

This is why a model fumbles "how many r's in strawberry?" — it never saw ten
letters, it saw three chunks. Not stupidity; the wrong unit. It's also why
non-English text and code often cost *more* tokens per idea: the tokenizer was
trained mostly on English prose, so everything else fragments harder.

Keep the rule of thumb in your head — **1,000 tokens ≈ 750 words** — and remember
that *everything* is counted in tokens: your prompt, the system prompt, tool
definitions, files you attach, the conversation so far, and the model's own reply.

> 💡 **Try it:** paste a paragraph of your own writing into the
> [OpenAI tokenizer](https://platform.openai.com/tokenizer) and watch it split. The gap between "words I wrote" and "tokens
> it became" is the intuition this whole lesson rests on.

## The context window: the model's working memory

The **context window** is the maximum number of tokens a model can consider at
once — and the part people miss: it's **shared between your input and its output.**
Think of it as desk space. The system prompt, your instructions, every attached
file, the whole back-and-forth, *and* the room left for the answer all sit on the
same desk. Pile on too much and things slide off the edges.

Modern windows are big (100K to 1M tokens) but neither infinite nor free. Two
consequences you'll feel constantly:

- **It "forgets."** In a long session, the earliest turns scroll out of the window
  (or get summarized away by the app). That's not the model going dumb — it literally
  can't see them anymore. In a chat app the fix is starting a fresh chat with a short
  summary; coding agents add tools like `/clear` and compaction
  (→ **context-engineering**).
- **It degrades before it's full.** As the window fills, attention spreads thin and
  recall gets flaky well before the hard limit — sometimes called *context rot*.
  Headroom on the counter is not the same as headroom in quality.

## Lost in the middle

Here's the sharpest, least-obvious mechanic: models attend best to the **start**
and the **end** of a long context and reliably **overlook the middle**. Put a
critical instruction halfway down a wall of text and it may as well not be there.
So lead with what matters, and if a rule is load-bearing, place it up top or at the
very end — never buried mid-scroll.

## You pay per token — in both directions

On any API you're billed **per token, input and output** — and output tokens are
usually the pricier side. A single call feels like nothing; multiply by 1,000 users
or 100,000 runs and the token count *is* your invoice. Two levers fall straight out
of this:

- **Trim the input.** Shorter, sharper prompts aren't just cleaner — at scale
  they're money.
- **Don't call at all when you don't have to,** and **bound the output** so the
  expensive direction stays small.

Because tokenization is deterministic, you can **count the tokens before you send**
and know the bill in advance instead of discovering it on a dashboard. (Which model
*tier* is worth paying for at a given volume is a separate call →
**models-and-effort**.)

## Common failure modes

- **A long chat gets vaguer and starts ignoring early instructions** → the early turns
  have scrolled out or been summarized, and attention is spread thin → start a fresh
  chat with a five-line brief of what matters, instead of repeating yourself in the old one.
- **A rule in the middle of a long prompt keeps getting broken** → lost in the middle →
  move it to the top or the very end, and keep it short.
- **You paste a whole report "so it has everything" and the answer gets worse** → the
  relevant lines are diluted by pages of noise → paste the sections that matter, or ask
  it to quote the parts it used.
- **A prompt that costs nothing in testing gets expensive in use** → you priced one
  call, not thousands, and forgot output tokens → count tokens, multiply by real volume,
  and cap the output length.

## From limits to strategy

Everything above is the *physics* — tokens, a shared window, an attention curve, a
per-token meter. The engineering discipline built on top of it — deciding what
actually earns a spot on the desk and what to leave off — is **context
engineering**, later in this pillar. → covered in **context-engineering**.
