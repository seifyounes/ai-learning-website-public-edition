You don't have one AI — you have a dispatch board. The same job costs pennies or dollars,
and lands in a second or a minute, depending on which brain you route it to and how hard you
make it think. Picking well is two dials, not one lookup: **which model** and **how much effort**.

## Two dials, not one "best model"

The beginner move is to hunt for the single best model and send everything to it. Dead end.
There's a best model *per task*, and the real control surface is two knobs: **tier** (which
brain) and **effort** (how hard it thinks). Provider is a third, slower-moving knob.

The universal waste is routing every task to the flagship at maximum effort. Deep models cost
many times more and run noticeably slower — pure loss on work a small model clears instantly.

## Dial 1 — tier: the smallest brain that clears your bar

Every provider ships a range, and the names change often enough that you should learn the
*shape*, not the version numbers:

- **Fast and cheap:** Claude's Haiku, OpenAI's "mini" models, Google's Gemini Flash family.
- **Capable middle:** Claude's Sonnet, the standard GPT model in ChatGPT, Gemini Pro on everyday
  settings.
- **Deep and pricey:** Claude's Opus, the thinking or "pro" modes in ChatGPT and Gemini.

The question is never "what's the best model" — it's "what's the **cheapest one that clears MY
quality bar**," escalating only when a tier visibly fails.

Route by the shape of the work: tag 1,000 support tickets or knock out short rewrites → fast
tier. Draft a real report or a page of product copy → the middle. Untangle tricky business
logic, a hard proof or a contract with traps in it → deep tier. In the chat apps you choose with
the model picker or a "thinking" toggle. In Claude Code you switch per session with `/model`
(Cursor, Codex and Copilot have their own pickers), and what your plan defaults to, and when it
falls back to a smaller model under heavy use, varies by plan, so check rather than assume.

- `/model sonnet`: middle tier, for the routine majority.
- `/model opus`: deep tier, for the hard minority.

The per-million pricing and the size of the tier spread are token mechanics →
**tokens-and-context**. Here you only need the decision: cheaper tiers are far cheaper and
faster, so default low and earn your way up.

## Dial 2 — effort: reasoning is a knob, not a magic phrase

This is where the old "think step by step" trick goes to retire. It used to be a real
unlock — force a model to reason out loud and it got sharper. Today's frontier models reason
**by default** (Claude's extended thinking, ChatGPT's and Gemini's thinking modes). You no longer
summon reasoning with a phrase — you **tune how much of it you pay for**.

The lever is the **effort or thinking budget**. Claude Code exposes it as `/effort`
(`low → medium → high → xhigh → max`); the APIs expose it as an effort or thinking-budget
setting; the chat apps as a thinking toggle or mode. More effort means more internal reasoning
spent before the answer — better on hard, multi-step logic, but slower and pricier. Match it to
difficulty: rename a variable or fix a typo → low; trace a nasty bug or plan a data migration →
high. Anthropic's own docs warn that the top setting can show diminishing returns and overthink,
so test before you make it a habit.

- `/effort low`: a rename, a one-liner, a lookup.
- `/effort high`: multi-step work, where one wrong step ruins it.

The one place the old magic phrase still earns its keep: the cheapest **non-reasoning** models,
where step-by-step thinking isn't built in and "reason first, then answer" still nudges quality up.

> 💡 **Try it:** run the same hard task twice: once with thinking off (or `/effort low`) and once
> with it on (or `/effort high`). Watch whether the extra thinking changed the *answer* — or just
> the *wait* and the *bill*. If it didn't move the answer, that task class doesn't need the effort.
> Now you know its route.

## Worked example: routing one week of work

A marketing analyst lists her recurring jobs and routes each once:

| Job | Tier × effort | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rewrite 30 product blurbs to a new length | fast × low | short, repetitive, easy to check |
| Summarize a 40-page industry report | middle × low | long input, but no hard reasoning |
| Find the flaw in a pricing spreadsheet's logic | deep × high | one wrong step ruins it |
| Draft the quarterly narrative for leadership | middle × medium | quality matters; she edits anyway |

She tests the riskiest row both ways once, sees the fast model miss the spreadsheet error,
and keeps the deep route for that class only. Everything else stays cheap. The table took
twenty minutes and ends the daily "which model?" dithering.

## The provider knob — a name-check, not a lesson

Providers are the third dial, and at this level knowing the names is enough: **Anthropic**
(Claude), **OpenAI** (GPT, ChatGPT), **Google** (Gemini), and **open-weights** families such
as DeepSeek, Qwen and Llama when you need to run a model yourself. Which assistant wins which
everyday job → **big-three-compared**; committing to a provider for an API build (latency,
privacy, region tradeoffs) → **choosing-models-providers**; per-vendor chat features → the
[Chat assistants](/foundations/chat-assistants) pillar.

## Common failure modes

- **Bills or waits balloon on simple work** → everything goes to the flagship at max effort →
  default to the middle tier, escalate on evidence.
- **The cheap model quietly drops a detail** → a task with hidden difficulty was routed by its
  length, not its risk → route by how costly a mistake is, and read the output for omissions.
- **"The new model is worse" after an update** → your route was tested on an old version →
  re-run your small test set whenever the model under you changes.
- **Max effort makes answers slower and stranger** → overthinking on a simple task → drop the
  effort until quality stops improving.

## The habit: match brain to job

Every task is a routing decision on two-plus dials — tier × effort (× provider). Default to
the **smallest brain at the lowest effort** that clears your bar, and escalate only on
evidence. Cost-vs-quality isn't a vibe: it's the cheapest `(tier, effort)` pair that passes a
concrete check. Measure it once per task class, write the route down, move on. It's the same
discipline as the harness sensors and loops later in the pillar — set a bar, take the cheapest
thing that clears it, escalate on failure. People who fight their AI reach for one brain and one
effort for everything. You'll route.
