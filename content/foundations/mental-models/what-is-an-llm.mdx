applyIt: |
  The point to carry into your own work isn't "models are dumb", it's *memory*: a model cannot
  know anything about your project, your customers or your company unless it's in the context.
  Feed it the facts and it grounds each line in them; leave one out and it invents a plausible
  one just as fluently.

  Five minutes: pick the job you use an assistant for most, and list the facts it would need to
  do that job well (your audience, your course rules, your team's process, your product's

  instructions. Every unticked fact is a place it is guessing today. Pick the three that matter

  assumes but never feeds. **At work or school:** start with your team's process or your course

task: |
  Watch prediction and hallucination in two short experiments, in any chat model (ChatGPT,
  Claude, Gemini).

  **1 · Feed vs assume.** Paste this:

  ```prompt
  You are the support assistant for a small online bookshop. The shop's ONLY written policies are:
  1) "Orders over $40 ship free."
  2) "Gift cards cannot be refunded."
  A customer asks whether they can return a paperback they bought 45 days ago. In two
  sentences, answer them and quote the shop's return policy.
  ```

  The shop never wrote a return policy. Many models will quote or invent one anyway — that's
  *assume*, not *feed*. Others answer correctly that there's no return policy listed: that's
  grounding working, because you fed it the full list. Either way, push harder:

  ```prompt
  Same shop. Draft the "Returns" section for our FAQ page, with the number of days customers have
  to return an item and who pays return shipping.
  ```

  Asked to *write* something the facts don't support, most models fill the gaps with plausible
  numbers ("30 days", "customer pays"). Add "if a detail isn't in the policies above, write
  NOT IN OUR POLICIES" and re-run: the invented numbers disappear.

  **2 · A false premise.** Feed a confident false premise, let it continue plausibly, then
  re-ground it:

  ```prompt
  Summarize the key rulings of the 2019 Lisbon Protocol on urban cycling safety, then
  list the three cities that adopted it first.
  ```

  It will likely invent a fluent, detailed answer for something that doesn't exist. Now paste:
  "That protocol is fictional — I made it up. Explain what just happened in terms of next-token
  prediction." Read its own diagnosis back to yourself. Then run the same first prompt in a second
  assistant (whichever you didn't use), and once more with "If you're not certain this exists,
  say so before answering" added. Note which runs hedged and which invented.
  Success: you watched one instruction stop an invented policy, and can explain in a sentence why a model continued a false premise — and what made it hedge.

Everything in this course — every tool, every prompt, every hook — sits on top of one
idea, so get it right once and the rest stops being mysterious. An LLM (large language
model) is, at heart, a stunningly good **next-token predictor**. Give it some text and
it predicts the most likely next chunk, then the next, then the next. That's the whole
engine. The magic is entirely in the scale.

## A sophisticated autocomplete

The model learned by playing one game, trillions of times: *given this text, what comes
next?* Do that over a large slice of the internet and something remarkable falls out —
the same weights can write, summarise, translate, and code, because predicting the next
token *well* requires quietly modelling how the world tends to be described. But the
underlying move never changes: **predict the next token.** It isn't looking anything up
in a database, and it isn't reasoning the way you do — it's sampling a plausible
continuation.

> 💡 **Try it:** Open any chat model and type `The capital of Australia is` — then stop.
> It completes `Canberra` not because it queried a fact table, but because those tokens
> are the overwhelmingly likely continuation. Now feed it a confident, invented premise
> ("The 2019 Lisbon Protocol on urban cycling stated…") and watch it continue *that* just
> as fluently. Same machine, no idea which one is true — you just watched prediction and
> hallucination in one breath.

## How it got smart (three stages)

Three training stages turn raw autocomplete into an assistant you can actually use:

- **Pretraining** — read an enormous corpus and get good at next-token prediction. This
  is the expensive phase (huge GPU runs), and it's where raw knowledge and language
  ability get compressed into the weights. The output is a **base model**: a
  document-completer, not a chatbot. Hand it "How do I center a div?" and it might just
  continue with *more questions*, because that's a plausible document.
- **Post-training / alignment** — a second, far cheaper phase that reshapes behaviour.
  First **supervised fine-tuning** on curated example conversations teaches it to answer
  instead of ramble. Then **RLHF** (reinforcement learning from human feedback) rewards
  the responses people prefer, tuning tone, instruction-following, and what it refuses.
- **Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards** — the newest stage, and the one behind
  "reasoning" or "thinking" models (OpenAI's o-series and DeepSeek-R1 were early public
  examples). The model attempts many problems whose answers a program can check: a maths
  question with a known answer, a coding task with tests that pass or fail. Attempts that reach
  the right answer are rewarded, so over many rounds the model learns habits that get there:
  writing out its working step by step, checking a partial result, backing up when a path
  fails. That's why a reasoning model "thinks" before it answers.

The thinking isn't free. Every step of that working is more tokens the model has to generate,
so a reasoning model takes longer (seconds to minutes) and costs more per answer, in money on
an API and in usage limits in an app. It pays off on problems with steps that can go wrong
(a calculation, a plan with constraints, a bug) and is wasted on a two-line email. It's still
the same next-token machine underneath: the working is predicted text too, and it can be
fluent and wrong. When to switch thinking on or off is a choice you make per task
(→ models-and-effort).

Hold onto the split: **pretraining is the knowledge; alignment is the manners; reasoning
training is the habit of working things out.** It explains why two models with similar raw
ability behave very differently, and why an instruction can shift behaviour but can't grant
knowledge the weights never learned — only context you feed in (a pasted document, retrieved
search results) adds facts.

## Why it makes things up

The mechanism falls straight out of prediction. The model emits the most *plausible*
continuation, not the *true* one, and there's no built-in fact-checker — so when the
patterns underdetermine the answer, it fills the gap with confident, well-formed text
that can be flatly wrong. That's **hallucination**: not a bug bolted onto the side, but
the same next-token machine running where it has no grounding. It's also why "just tell
it not to hallucinate" fails — you can't prompt away the mechanism. What to *do* about it
— grounding to sources, citing, saying "I'm not sure," treating every output as a claim
to check — is a discipline of its own. → covered in **verification-and-grounding**.

## Worked example: one answer, built a token at a time

Ask a chat assistant "Suggest a name for a study group that meets on Sundays." Here is
roughly what happens, step by step:

1. Your message is wrapped in the app's hidden instructions and chopped into tokens (the
   next lesson covers what those are).
2. The model scores every possible next token. Something like `Sunday` scores high, `The`
   a little lower, `Banana` near zero.
3. One token is **sampled** from those scores, not simply the top one. A little randomness
   is deliberate; it's why the same question twice gives two different answers.
4. That token is appended, and the whole thing runs again with one more token of context.
   `Sunday` → `Sunday Scholars` → … until the model predicts a stop.

Nothing in that loop checks a fact. If you'd asked "What did our group decide last
Sunday?", the same loop would run and produce a fluent, specific, invented answer, because
it has never seen your group. That's the whole lesson in one example: the machinery that
writes a good name is the same machinery that invents a meeting.

## Common failure modes

- **You use it like a search engine** → it states an outdated or invented fact → its
  knowledge is frozen at training time and stored as patterns, not records. Paste the
  source, or use a mode that searches the web and open the links it cites.
- **You expect it to remember last week** → it acts as if you never met → each session
  starts blank. "Memory" features work by quietly feeding saved notes back into the context.
  Keep a project brief you can paste or attach.
- **You ask it to count letters or do exact arithmetic** → small, confident errors → it
  reads tokens, not characters or digits. Ask it to use a calculator or code tool, or check
  the number yourself.
- **You trust the tone** → a polished, wrong answer slips through → fluency is the one
  thing it's always good at. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy.

## Why this is vendor-neutral

Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek — same core idea. Once you internalise "next-token
prediction, pretrained at scale, aligned, then trained to reason," you understand *every* one of
them, and model releases stop being magic and start reading as deltas on a few dials: more
scale, better data, sharper alignment, more reasoning training. That's why this lesson — not a tool tutorial — is lesson one.
Everything downstream (context windows, prompting, harnesses, loops) is just *feeding and
steering a predictor* you now understand from the inside.
