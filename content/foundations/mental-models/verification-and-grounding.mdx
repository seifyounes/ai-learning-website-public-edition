applyIt: |
  Grounding and verification aren't only chat habits — they're how you make any AI feature or
  AI-assisted workflow trustworthy. Picture a hypothetical support bot for an online shop that must
  answer from the shop's policy pages. The lesson's three ideas become three design decisions:

  - **Grounding, enforced by code:** the prompt feeds the model the *actual* policy text, and a
    check after generation **rejects any answer that doesn't quote a real policy sentence
    verbatim** (a plain substring match) — plus a banned-phrase list and length bounds. That's
    "answer only from the source, cited" turned into a check instead of a hope.
  - **Give it an out, at the system level:** when the question matches no policy, **skip the model
    entirely** and return a fixed fallback ("I'll pass this to a person"). The model never gets an
    empty slot to bluff into.
  - **Verify before ship:** an answer reaches the customer only if the generation finished normally
    *and* it cleared every check — a deterministic sensor gating each output.

  Now plan the same for something of your own, in ten minutes and four lines: the grounding rule
  (what every output must quote or match from the source), the check that gates each output, the
  fallback when a check fails, and where rejected outputs get logged with the reason, so they
  become your future fabrication test set (→ evals-and-testing).

  **If you code:** use an AI feature you're building or plan to build. **If you don't code yet:**
  use a manual workflow such as AI-drafted replies to customers or classmates; the "check" becomes
  a short checklist you tick before anything is sent.

  Success: four lines, and no generated output in your plan reaches anyone without passing a named check.

From "what is an LLM": the model emits the most *plausible* continuation, not a
*verified* one. Confident wrongness isn't a bug you patch — it's the default output of
a system with no fact-checker inside it. So stop trying to *prompt it away*. The 2024
move was clever wording; the 2026 move is **engineering trust**: constrain what goes in
so there's less room to invent, and check what comes out so the inventions get caught.
Two levers — **grounding** (upstream) and **verification** (downstream) — and you own
both. Everything you do here by hand is the manual prototype of the automatic
**sensors** you'll wire in harness engineering.

## Grounding: force retrieval over inference

There are two ways a model can answer "what does clause 4 say?" — **inference**
(reconstruct it from blurry parametric memory, which is lossy and will confabulate) or
**retrieval** (read the clause you pasted and answer from it). Inference is where
hallucination lives. Grounding is the deliberate act of taking inference off the table:
put the real source in the context and say *"answer using only this; if it isn't here,
say so."* You've collapsed the space in which the model can make something up.

That's the picture in this lesson's video: fetch the trusted material first, answer from it,
point to the evidence, and say "I don't know" when the material is silent. The video draws
the automated version, **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation), where software finds the
right passages for you; building that is covered in → rag-fundamentals. Here you do the
retrieval by hand: paste the source yourself, or save the rule once in your custom
instructions, a Project or a Gem so every chat starts with it.

> 💡 **Try it:** paste a real doc, then constrain the answer to it:
> ```prompt
> Answer ONLY from the text between <doc></doc>. Quote the exact sentence you used.
> If the text does not answer the question, reply exactly: NOT STATED.
> Do not use outside knowledge.
> <doc>...paste source here...</doc>
> ```

## Give the model an out

Models bluff because their objective rewards a fluent answer over an honest "I don't
know" — absent permission, uncertainty is literally off the menu. So put it on the
menu: *"if the sources don't answer this, say NOT STATED — do not guess."* One sentence,
and you convert confident fabrication into an honest gap you can act on. It's the
cheapest, highest-leverage line in this lesson, and it only works *paired* with
grounding: bound the input **and** license "unsure," or the model happily fills the gap
for you.

## Citations ≠ proof

Ask for citations and models will happily produce them — perfectly formatted, plausible
authors, dead DOIs. A citation is a **claim**, not evidence. Requesting sources doesn't
*reduce* hallucination; it **relocates** it into a form you can check — which only helps
if you actually check. So resolve every reference: does the quote appear *verbatim* in
the source, does the URL load, does it say what was claimed? The trap is treating a
citation's *presence* as proof of the claim's *truth* — they're independent. Anthropic's
**Citations** feature is the strong version: the model cites spans from documents you
supply, verifiable by construction (linked in the backups); other providers offer similar
source-grounding features — and even then you spot-check.

## Chain-of-verification

Generating and checking are different jobs, and a model doing both in one pass is
committed to its own story. Split them (Dhuliawala et al., 2023): **draft → generate
independent verification questions about the draft's claims → answer each against the
sources → revise.** The fresh, unanchored pass catches fabrications the first pass was
blind to. The manual form is one follow-up prompt: *"List every factual claim you just
made. For each, quote the supporting source span or mark it UNSUPPORTED."* Then delete
or fix everything marked UNSUPPORTED. This doer-then-checker split is the same shape
you'll formalise in **loop engineering** — here you run it with your own eyes.

## Verification is a sensor (the bridge)

Notice what you've been doing: reading an output and ruling pass/fail. **That's a
sensor** — you're just running it manually, and you're the slow, forgetful,
un-scalable part of the loop. The whole of **harness engineering** (next lessons) is the
move that makes this deterministic: a test suite, a typecheck, a schema validator, a
substring check — checks that **fail bad output without you**, every run. Trust is
engineered: *you* decide what "correct" means, then encode a check that enforces it
forever. The manual discipline you practised above is the **spec** for that automated
sensor. (Turning that judgement into a scored, repeatable eval — LLM-as-judge, test
sets — is its own lesson, → evals-and-testing; here we build the instinct, not the harness.)

## Worked example: checking a summary before you send it

Your manager asks for a one-paragraph summary of a 12-page vendor contract. Run the two
levers in order:

1. **Ground.** Paste the contract between `<doc>` tags and ask for the summary with every
   sentence followed by the clause it came from, plus "NOT STATED" for anything the contract
   doesn't cover (say, the termination notice period).
2. **Verify.** In a fresh chat, paste the contract and the summary and ask: "List each
   factual claim in the summary. For each, quote the supporting clause or mark it
   UNSUPPORTED." Then open the contract yourself and check the two claims that matter most:
   price and term.

Say the checker flags "auto-renews annually" as UNSUPPORTED, and the contract actually says
renewal needs written agreement. That's one sentence that would have committed your team to
something false. Ten minutes of checking bought that.

## Common failure modes

- **Answers cite sources that don't say what's claimed** → citations were treated as proof
  → open the cited passage for every claim you'll act on.
- **A grounded prompt still fills gaps with guesses** → no permission to say "unknown" → add
  the NOT STATED line.
- **The model's self-check passes everything** → the same chat that wrote the draft is grading
  it → verify in a fresh chat, or with a different model.
- **You check everything and run out of time** → no priority → verify only the load-bearing
  claims (numbers, names, dates, anything someone will act on) and skim the rest.
