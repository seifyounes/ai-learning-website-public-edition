Every AI engineering interview eventually asks some version of **"would you fine-tune
here, and why?"** It's a cheap way to test whether you understand what each technique
actually changes. Most candidates hand-wave; this lesson gives you a method instead, and
the same method saves real money on real projects.

## The decision ladder

Work upward, stopping at the first rung that solves the problem:

1. **Better prompting.** Clearer instructions, structure, constraints. Free and instant.
2. **Few-shot examples.** Show the model 3–10 examples of what good looks like.
3. **RAG.** The model *doesn't know something* (your data, fresh facts). Retrieval puts
   knowledge into the context window at request time (→ rag-fundamentals).
4. **Fine-tuning.** The model *doesn't behave right* even with good prompts and examples:
   wrong style, inconsistent format, weak at your niche task, or your prompt has grown so
   long that every call is expensive.

The one-liner worth memorizing: **RAG adds knowledge, fine-tuning shapes behavior.**
Fine-tuning does not reliably teach facts, and any facts it does absorb go stale the moment
your data changes. Keeping knowledge current is retrieval's job. The two also combine: a
tuned model that writes in the right format can still answer from retrieved documents.

## The three flavors you should be able to name

- **SFT (supervised fine-tuning).** Train on input → ideal-output pairs. The workhorse for
  tone, format and task skill.
- **LoRA / QLoRA.** Not a different goal, a cheaper method. Freeze the model and train
  small adapter matrices alongside it; QLoRA does the same on a quantized (compressed)
  base so it fits on smaller GPUs. This is how most people fine-tune open models.
- **Preference tuning (DPO, RLHF-style).** Train on "this answer beats that one" pairs to
  shape judgment and style beyond what SFT captures.

Hosted fine-tuning (offered by OpenAI, Google Cloud's Vertex AI and Mistral, among others)
hides the mechanics: you upload examples and get back a tuned model ID. Which models can be
tuned changes often, so check current docs. Open models, tuned with Hugging Face's
libraries or tools like Unsloth, give you full control for less money and more work.

## Worked example: one feature up the ladder

Say you're building the classifier from the task: thousands of feedback messages a day,
tagged into 12 fixed categories. Here's how the ladder plays out. The scores are
illustrative, but the moves are the real ones.

1. **Build the yardstick first.** Label 200 real messages by hand. Set aside 50 as a
   held-out test set that nothing will ever be trained on.
2. **Rung 1.** A prompt with a one-line definition of each category scores, say, 84% on the
   held-out set. Most errors are two overlapping categories ("bug" vs "performance").
3. **Rung 2.** Add two examples for each confusing pair and a sentence on how to tell them
   apart: 91%. Your bar is 90%, so quality is solved here.
4. **Skip rung 3.** Nothing is missing *knowledge*; the categories are all in the prompt.
5. **Now the cost question.** At this volume the frontier model bill is large. That's the
   one good reason left to climb: tune a small model with LoRA on a few thousand labeled
   messages (labels can come from the frontier model, spot-checked by a human), then score
   it on the same held-out 50.
6. **Decide on evidence.** If the small model matches the bar at a fraction of the cost per
   call, switch, and route low-confidence cases back to the frontier model. If it doesn't,
   you've lost a few hours, not a quarter.

Notice that fine-tuning entered for **cost**, not quality, and only after an eval set
could prove it was safe.

> 💡 **Try it:** In any chat assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), ask it to classify five
> messages into categories you make up, with no examples. Then repeat with two examples per
> category. The jump you see is rung 2, and it's often what people think needs fine-tuning.

## What fine-tuning really costs

The GPU bill is the small part. The real costs are **curating hundreds of quality
examples** (garbage in, confidently wrong out), **evals** to prove the tune helped, and
**maintenance**: every base-model upgrade means re-tuning and re-testing, while a
prompt-based system often just improves for free. That's why practitioners default to
prompting until it hurts and tune when the math says so.

## Common failure modes

- **The tuned model states outdated facts confidently** → facts were trained into the
  weights → move them into retrieval and tune only for behavior.
- **Great on inputs like the training data, worse on everything else** → too few, too
  uniform or too-long-trained examples → add varied examples, train for fewer epochs, and
  compare against the baseline per category.
- **Nobody can say whether tuning helped** → there was no baseline → build the eval set
  and score the untuned model before training anything.
- **Suspiciously perfect scores** → test examples leaked into the training data → keep a
  held-out split that training never sees.
- **A newer base model leaves your tune behind** → the tune is pinned to the old base →
  budget for re-tuning, or keep a prompt-only path you can switch to.
