Launching feels like the finish line. It's actually the starting line. Your v1 is a **guess**
about what people want; real usage tells you the truth. The teams that win aren't the ones who
plan perfectly. They're the ones who **iterate fastest** on real feedback.

## Listen in two ways

- **Behavior (analytics)**: what users actually *do*. Add a lightweight tool (**PostHog**,
  **Plausible**, or your platform's built-in analytics) and watch where people drop off, what
  they use and what they ignore.
- **Voice (feedback)**: what users *say*. Reviews, support messages, a one-line in-app feedback
  form. Collect it somewhere central.

Behavior tells you *where* the problem is; voice often tells you *why*.

## Track a few events, not everything

You don't need a hundred events. You need the handful that describe your app's core action
from start to finish. For a to-do app: *signed up*, *created first task*, *completed a task*,
*came back on another day*. Four events give you a funnel and a retention signal; anything
more, add only when you have a question it answers. Keep personal details (names, emails,
the content people type) out of event data, and say in your privacy notice what you collect.

## Small numbers: read people, not percentages

With only a handful of early users, a funnel chart misleads by rounding. "50% drop-off at
step 3" might be two people, and two people are two stories, not a trend. So change the unit
of analysis: read each person's activity as a timeline (what they opened, where they stopped,
what they came back to) and set it next to what that same person told you.

When behavior and words disagree, behavior usually wins, but only after you ask why. "I love
that feature" from someone who never opens it is politeness, not demand; the follow-up ("when
did you last use it?") is the real data. A reproducible bug report outranks any small-sample
percentage, because it's a fact rather than an inference.

Treat every number as a prompt to talk to someone, not a verdict. Aggregate metrics start to
earn trust once one person's odd week can no longer swing the line, which means dozens of
active users rather than a handful. Until then, your best analytics tool is a message to the
person behind the trace.

## Worked example: one iteration cycle

Imagine (hypothetically) a to-do app with fifteen early users after two weeks.

1. **Behavior.** The four-event funnel shows most people created a first task, but few came
   back on a second day.
2. **Voice.** Twelve messages, clustered by an assistant: several people say they "forgot the
   app existed"; two ask for dark mode; one reports that tasks vanish after editing.
3. **Cross-reference.** "Forgot it existed" lines up with the missing return visits: behavior
   and voice agree. Dark mode is two people and no behavior signal. The vanishing-task report
   is a bug.
4. **Decide.** Fix the bug first (it's a fact, and it destroys trust). Then make *one* change
   aimed at return visits, such as an optional daily reminder. Dark mode goes on the later
   list.
5. **Define the check before shipping.** "Do more people come back on a second day over the
   next two weeks? And what do the people who return say?"
6. **Ship, wait, repeat.** One change, a fixed window, then the next cycle.

> 💡 **Try it:** Paste ten real reviews of any app you use (from its store page) into an
> assistant and ask: "Cluster these into themes, count how many separate people raised each,
> and quote one line per theme." Then check two of the quotes against the originals.

## Let AI synthesize it

Feedback is scattered and overwhelming, which makes it a perfect job for AI. Point a coding
agent (Claude Code, Cursor, Codex) that can use an **MCP** connector at your analytics and
feedback, or paste an export into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, and ask: *"Cluster this into
themes and tell me the top 3 things to fix or build next."* A pile of noise becomes a
prioritized roadmap in minutes.

## Common failure modes

- **Building whatever the loudest person asks for** → voice without behavior → count distinct
  people per theme and check whether usage backs it up.
- **Dashboards nobody reads** → too many events and no question → track the core-action
  events and ask one question per cycle.
- **Three changes shipped at once** → you can't tell which one worked → one change per cycle.
- **The AI summary invents a theme or a quote** → summarizers fill gaps plausibly → ask for
  counts and verbatim quotes, and spot-check them against the source.

## Close the loop

The whole game is one loop run quickly: **ship → measure → learn → improve → ship.** An app
that improves every week beats a "perfect" app that ships once and stalls. You've now taken an
idea from spec to a tested app in real users' hands that gets better over time. One lesson
left: put it *all* together in a capstone.
