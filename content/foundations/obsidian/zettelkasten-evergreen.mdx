PARA keeps you organised for *doing*. Zettelkasten keeps you organised for
*thinking* — and it's where a vault stops being storage and starts generating
ideas. The method is old (Niklas Luhmann wrote 90,000 linked notes on paper
cards); Obsidian just makes it cheap.

This is also the lesson where the pillar's `wiki/` folder finally gets its
definition. Everything below describes the shape of a good page in there. Learn it
by hand first — you're about to become the reviewer of a machine that writes
thousands of them.

## Three rules that do all the work

1. **Atomic** — one idea per note. Small notes can be reused and linked in many
   contexts; big notes get filed once and forgotten.
2. **In your own words** — rewriting forces understanding. Copy-paste builds a
   junkyard; rephrasing builds knowledge.
3. **Densely linked** — connect each new note to related ones with `[[ ]]`. The
   links *are* the thinking.

## Evergreen notes

Andy Matuschak's **evergreen notes** sharpen the idea: write notes to be
**developed over time and reused**, concept-oriented and continuously refined —
the opposite of dated, throwaway journal entries. Title each one as a full claim
("Optimistic UI feels faster than it is") so it states something you can build on.

A title that's a claim is doing quiet work. "Optimistic UI" is a folder label; a
claim can be *disagreed with*, which means it can be tested, revised, and linked
to the note that contradicts it. That's the difference between a filing system and
a body of thinking.

> 💡 **Try it:** take the title of any note you already have ("Meeting 3 March",
> "Marketing ideas", "Week 4 lecture") and rewrite it as one claim you could disagree with.
> If you can't, the note holds no single idea yet, which is exactly what splitting fixes.

## Structure emerges — it isn't designed

The video's real argument is not about folders, it's about *order of operations*.
Kirsanov opens by parodying his own past setup — 25 plugins, 253 tags, 39 folders
— and then shows the alternative:

1. Write atomic notes and link them, including links to notes that **don't exist
   yet** (lesson 3's trick).
2. Those ghost notes act as tags for free, and show up in graph view.
3. When one ghost starts collecting a crowd of links, that topic has proven it
   matters. *Now* promote it into a real note.

That promoted hub is a **Map of Content (MOC)** — a hand-made table of contents
made of curated `[[links]]`, and the entrance to a big topic. Folders built up
front encode a guess about your own thinking. Hubs built afterwards encode
evidence. Same for the other half of his system: he keeps a **reference note per
source** (one per book, one per course) separate from the atomic idea notes, so
"where did this come from" never gets tangled with "what do I now believe." That
split is the ancestor of this pillar's `sources/` and `wiki/`.

## Where insight comes from

Here's the payoff: when atomic, linked notes accumulate, **new ideas emerge from
the connections** — two notes you wrote months apart suddenly suggest a third
thing neither said. That's idea generation on tap, and it's why the method
outlasts every app trend.

## The 2026 version: this is the wiki layer

Everything above describes a page an agent can write. That is not a coincidence —
it's the whole design of Andrej Karpathy's **LLM wiki**: you curate raw sources,
and the agent compiles them into concept pages and entity pages that are atomic,
cross-referenced, and continuously maintained. His schema file is a rules document
(`AGENTS.md` for Codex, CLAUDE.md for Claude Code); he reads the results in Obsidian.
Different vocabulary, same object.

So the division of labour in your vault is:

- **`sources/`** — you drop things in. Immutable. The agent reads it and never
  writes to it, because a source you've edited is no longer a source.
- **`wiki/`** — the agent writes and maintains it. Atomic pages, own-voice prose,
  dense `[[ ]]`, one claim per title. Exactly the three rules.
- **You** — curate what goes in, ask the questions, and *judge the pages*.

Obsidian is where you read, link, and catch what's wrong. The agent, whichever you run, is
what writes and maintains the files. Don't let anyone blur those two.

**Don't code?** The division is the same with a chat assistant: paste the source into ChatGPT,
Claude or Gemini, ask for the pages as separate markdown blocks, and save the ones that pass
your review into `wiki/` yourself. The review is identical, which is the point of this lesson.

## The one rule an agent can't keep for you

Look at the three rules again and ask which parts are mechanical.

**Atomic** is mechanical — split on idea boundaries. **Densely linked** is
mechanical, and it's the part humans skip because it's tedious; an agent will
happily maintain a hundred cross-references and never get bored. **Evergreen
shape** is mechanical too: a claim-shaped title is a formatting rule.

Rule 2 is not. "In your own words" was never about the words — it's a trick for
forcing *your* understanding, and nobody can do that on your behalf. An agent's
pages come out fluent and plausible whether or not you understood the source.
That is the failure mode: a `wiki/` that reads beautifully and that you cannot
defend a single line of.

Which is the honest answer to "why learn this in 2026". You learn the craft
because **you're now the reviewer**, and a reviewer who's never written a good
atomic note can't tell a real one from a well-formed empty one. Read the agent's
pages for claims you couldn't explain out loud, and rewrite those by hand. Lesson
10's lint pass automates the other checks — orphans, contradictions, stale claims
— precisely because those *are* mechanical.

## What a property actually is

Your prompt stamps three of them (or the chat assistant writes them into each block you
paste), so be clear what you're asking for. A **property** is a
key-value pair in a small block at the very top of a note, fenced by `---`:

```
---
type: concept
topic: pricing
source: "[[first-source]]"
---
```

That block is YAML front matter — plain text, part of the file, readable by anything. Since
Obsidian **1.4** (2023) the app shows it as a typed **Properties** panel instead of raw
text, but it's the same characters on disk either way.

Properties are what turn a pile of notes into something queryable. Prose can't be filtered;
`type: concept` can. That's the entire reason this rung stamps them and the next one can
build a view in a few clicks — and it's why the three keys have to be spelled the same on
every page. Lesson 7 reads exactly these three back.

That's the human craft. Next: the tooling that reads the properties you just
started writing — **Bases**, Dataview, and where an AI beats both.
