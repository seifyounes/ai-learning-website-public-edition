Before a single keystroke in Obsidian, get the idea straight: a **second brain is
a habit, not an app.** It's a trusted external system where you capture what
matters and connect it, so your knowledge compounds instead of leaking away.

## The problem it solves

You read books, watch tutorials, have great ideas in the shower — and lose almost
all of it within days. Your biological brain is for *having* ideas, not *storing*
them. A second brain takes over the storing, so nothing good disappears and you
can build on past-you's work.

That isn't a productivity slogan. The video grounds it in **active externalism** —
the position Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued in *The Extended Mind* (1998):
that a pocket calculator, long multiplication worked out on paper, or counting on
your fingers are genuinely part of the thinking, not just aids to it.

Their test is the useful part. An external store only counts as part of your mind
when it's **constant, immediately available, and automatically trusted**. That is
precisely what 340 open tabs and a scrollback of old chats are *not* — you can't
find anything in them, so you re-derive instead. A vault is the version that
passes the test. The work of this pillar is getting your notes over that bar.

## CODE — the system underneath

Tiago Forte's framework is four moves:

- **Capture** — keep what resonates, fast and friction-free.
- **Organize** — sort by what it's *for*, not by tidy categories.
- **Distill** — boil notes down to their essence so future-you gets it instantly.
- **Express** — turn knowledge into output. This is the point: a second brain that
  only hoards is a hoarder; one that helps you *create* is a superpower.

The video never mentions CODE, and that's fine — it shows you the **substrate**,
the place a second brain can live. CODE is the **habit loop** you run on top of
it. Substrate without habit is a folder of dead notes; habit without substrate is
what you have right now.

> 💡 **Try it:** open the notes app you already use and find the last note you
> wrote that you have *re-opened* since. Then find one you never re-opened. The
> difference between them (a clear title? a link from somewhere? a reason to
> return?) is the whole problem this pillar solves, in two notes.

## Why Obsidian, specifically

Any app can hold text. The case for Obsidian is about **data, not features**: a
vault is a folder of plain `.md` files sitting on your own disk.

- **You own it.** Obsidian is a deliberately tiny company. If it vanished tomorrow
  your notes would still open in any editor on any machine, forever.
- **The exports are honest.** Notion looks like markdown in the browser, then
  exports proprietary tags and symbols you can't cleanly read back. Plain text
  charges no exit tax.
- **It's scriptable.** Files on disk are the whole reason you can later point a
  coding agent (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI) straight at your vault (lesson 9), or drag
  any note into a chat with ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini without an export step. An API-locked
  notes app can't be read either way.

Strong reasons — and still secondary ones. Get the habit right and the software is
just plumbing.

## Errata — this video is from July 2023

The argument has aged well. Six specific facts have not. Don't act on these:

- **"Free for personal use"** → it's now **free for commercial use too**. The
  commercial licence became optional in February 2025, so yes, you can use it at
  work, no account required.
- **"1,014 plugins"** → **well over 8,000** community plugins and more than 800 themes
  in Obsidian's public
  [plugin registry](https://github.com/obsidianmd/obsidian-releases) (counted October 2026).
- **"The two-person company"** → the team has grown, though it remains deliberately
  small.
- **Dataview "should be built in"** → it effectively is. Obsidian 1.9 shipped
  **Bases**, a *core* plugin that does the common Dataview job natively. Lesson 7
  covers this; reach for Bases first.
- **Raw YAML front matter on screen** → Obsidian 1.4 added a **Properties** editor,
  so your screen won't match his.
- **"GPT-3 Notes"** → a 2023 fossil. AI inside Obsidian is lesson 8, and it looks
  nothing like that today.

Read that list as the lesson, not the footnote. **Plugin ecosystems churn; file
formats don't.** Every claim that rotted was about a company, a price, or a plugin.
Every claim that survived was about plain text — which is exactly the argument for
building on plain text.

## Where this pillar ends up

Tris built his vault by hand, and in 2023 that was the only option. It isn't now.

In April 2026 Andrej Karpathy published an "idea file" for what he calls an
[LLM wiki](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f): you
curate raw sources and ask good questions, and an **agent** writes and maintains the
notes — summaries, concept pages, cross-references, the index. His argument is that the
tedious part of a knowledge base was never the reading or the thinking. It's the
bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is exactly what a model doesn't get bored doing.

That's where this pillar is going, and it changes the division of labour:

- **Obsidian is the human side** — where you read, link, and catch what's wrong.
- **The agent owns the files** — a coding agent (Codex, Claude Code, Gemini CLI or
  similar) writes and maintains the notes on disk. The agent prompts in this pillar work
  in any of them.
- **No coding agent? The same split still works.** A chat assistant (ChatGPT, Claude or
  Gemini) reads the notes you paste or upload and drafts the pages; you save them into the
  vault. Every lesson gives you that path first, with the agent path beside it.

Which is why plain markdown in a folder stopped being a nice-to-have. It's the only
format an agent can read *and* write directly, with no API in the way.

## Common failure modes

- **You capture everything and never look at it again** → Capture without Distill →
  schedule ten minutes a week to boil a few notes down (lesson 10 makes this a habit).
- **You spend the first week on themes and plugins** → setup as procrastination → one
  vault, default settings, real notes on day one.
- **Notes pile up but never become anything** → no Express step → pick one note a week
  and turn it into something: a summary, a post, a better prompt.
- **You rebuild your system every time a new app trends** → the habit lives in the tool,
  not in you → keep plain markdown files and a simple loop you could run anywhere.
