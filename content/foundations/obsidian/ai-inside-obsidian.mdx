This is the lesson where AI arrives in your vault — and the lesson where you learn
what it can't do. Both halves matter. The plugins here are genuinely useful and the
vocabulary is genuinely worth having. But this pillar ends somewhere else, and you
should know why before you get attached.

## The mechanism has a name: RAG

Ask a vault question through Smart Connections and three steps run underneath:

1. **Embed.** Each chunk of each note is converted into a vector, a list of numbers
   that positions it by meaning. Your whole vault becomes a map.
2. **Retrieve.** Your question gets embedded too. The chunks nearest to it come back,
   ranked by similarity.
3. **Generate.** Those chunks go to the model alongside your question, and it answers
   from them.

That is **retrieval-augmented generation — RAG** — the same architecture behind every
"chat with your docs" product and enterprise assistant, and the one you'll build from
scratch in the *Applied AI engineering* pillar (→ rag-fundamentals, then
→ embeddings-vector-databases for what's under the hood). Knowing that your notes app
runs the same architecture as a company's internal assistant is a useful mental model:
whatever you learn about retrieval here transfers.

### Careful: "plugin" no longer means "RAG"

That used to be the whole plugin category. It isn't any more. Copilot for Obsidian (version 4)
can run opencode, Claude Code or Codex as an agent *inside* the vault, on desktop, doing
multi-step tool use rather than embed-retrieve-generate; paid tiers add hosted models and
extras. So a plugin can now host the same kind of agent you'd run in a terminal.

Which means the line this lesson draws is **not** plugin-versus-terminal — that one is
already blurring. The real line is what the next lesson is about: *retrieving* your raw
notes on every question, versus *compiling* them once into pages that keep getting better.
Judge a tool by which of those it does, not by where it runs.

## Local vs. cloud — a real decision

Smart Connections ships its **own embedding model and runs offline by default**, so the
map of your vault is built on your machine and stays there. That's the free, private
route, and it's why this lesson uses it.

The moment you send a bundle to a chat, the fork appears:

- **Cloud** models (Claude, GPT, Gemini) are the most capable, but the note text leaves
  your machine.
- **Local** models via **Ollama** or **LM Studio** keep everything home — slower, a bit
  less sharp, and free.

Make the call per note, not per tool. It's the same call a well-built app makes when it
keeps its AI provider's API key on its own server instead of shipping it inside the app:
decide once where sensitive material is allowed to go, then hold the line.

## What retrieval is genuinely best at

This lesson ends by naming a ceiling, so be fair about the floor first. Retrieval wins
outright at **recall over material you have not curated.**

Dump forty PDFs, a year of meeting transcripts, and a folder of half-read articles into
`sources/` tonight. Retrieval can find the right paragraph in the morning, with zero
upfront work from you. It scales with disk, not with attention. When the job is *"find me
that thing I know is in there somewhere"*, nothing beats it — and no compiled wiki will,
because a wiki only knows what someone has already bothered to compile.

Keep it. Use it. It's the search layer of your second brain, permanently.

> 💡 **Try it:** once Smart Connections has embedded your vault, open a note and look at its
> top three "related" notes. For each, ask: is this related in *meaning* to me, or does it
> just share words? One mismatch is the best two-minute lesson in why similarity is not
> relevance.

## Where it stops

Now the honest part. Watch what happens over a month of real use:

- **Every query starts from zero.** RAG re-derives the answer from raw chunks each time.
  Ask the same hard question in March and in June and the model does the same work twice,
  at the same cost, with no guarantee of the same answer. Nothing accumulated.
- **Synthesis dies in the thread.** You ask something subtle, get a genuinely good
  answer that pulled five notes together — and it lives in a chat log you'll never open
  again. The next question rebuilds it from scratch.
- **You are the curator, forever.** The video is explicit about this. You choose which
  hits go in the bundle, prune the irrelevant ones, and watch the link depth. One project
  bundle hits **35,000 tokens** at [17:39], and at [20:51] he says the quiet part clearly:
  the AI is not searching your vault — it only sees the bundle you handed it.
- **Similarity is not relevance.** Two notes that share vocabulary rank as neighbours even
  when one is a decision you made and the other is an idea you rejected. Retrieval has no
  idea which is which. You do.

None of that is a bug. It's what retrieval *is*: a fast, cheap, un-opinionated way to get
raw material in front of a model. The problem is only that most people stop here and
assume this is what "AI on my notes" means.

## What comes next

Lesson 9 is the alternative, and it's the reason this pillar exists in the shape it does.
Instead of retrieving from raw sources on every query, you have an agent **compile** them
once — into distilled, cross-linked pages it then keeps current. (A chat assistant can do the
compiling too, with you saving the pages; lesson 9 shows both routes.) Ask a question and it
reads pages that already contain the synthesis, instead of rebuilding it from chunks.
Good answers get filed back as new pages, so your explorations compound too.

Retrieval re-derives. A compiled wiki accumulates. Run the race in the task before
you read on — the difference is much more convincing when it's your own vault answering.
