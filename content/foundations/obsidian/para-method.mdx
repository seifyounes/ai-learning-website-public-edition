applyIt: |
  Before any AI sorts anything, write your own four lists on paper or in a scratch note. Ten
  minutes, and it's the judgment the task below leans on:

  - **Projects** — an outcome with an end. **If you study:** "submit the databases coursework",
    "pass the statistics midterm". **If you code:** "ship the login flow for my side project",
    "migrate the blog to a new framework". **At work:** "launch the Q3 newsletter", "finish the
    vendor comparison".
  - **Areas** — responsibilities with a standard and no finish line: "my degree", "health",
    "finances", "the team's weekly reporting", "keeping my portfolio current".
  - **Resources** — what you collect on but are not acting on: "machine learning", "design
    systems", "negotiation". Parked until a project pulls one out.
  - **Archive** — done or dormant: last semester's courses, a finished client job, the side
    project you paused.

  Then trace one thing through its lifecycle. A side project is a good example: building the
  first version is a Project, keeping it running is an Area, and each finished feature's notes
  graduate to Archive. Sorted by what you'll open when you sit down to work, not by subject.

  **With a chat assistant (no code):** paste the four lists into a chat and ask "Using Forte's
  tests (an outcome with an end is a project, an ongoing standard is an area, reference I'm not
  acting on is a resource, done or dormant is archive), which items would you move, and why?
  Don't rewrite my lists." Answer each flag: move it, or keep it with a reason.

  Success: four lists, every flag answered, and one item traced from Project to Area or Archive.

  **With a coding agent:** save the lists as `inbox/para-lists.md` and ask your agent the same
  question, plus which commitments your `daily/` notes keep mentioning that appear in no list.
  Report in chat only; edit no file.

  Success: four lists, every flag answered with the note behind it, and no file changed.

  Classify every uploaded note except index.md, vault-rules.md and log.md by actionability,

Ingest fills your vault fast — now the notes *you* wrote need just enough structure to
stay usable. **PARA** is the lightest system that works: four folders, sorted by how
*actionable* something is, not what topic it's about.

## The four buckets

- **Projects** — an outcome you're committed to, needing several work sessions, with an
  end. "Ship the next feature of my side project", "a client site launch", "submit the
  term paper". Forte runs 15–25 at once and calls that normal.
- **Areas** — a role or responsibility with a standard you maintain and never finish.
  "My degree", "Clients", "Finances".
- **Resources** — topics and reference you're not acting on yet. "AI engineering",
  "Mobile development", "Marketing". Waiting to be activated by some future project.
- **Archive** — anything inactive from the other three. Cold storage, not a bin. You
  never delete; you just get it out of your eyeline.

## The key move: actionability over topic

Most people file by subject and end up with notes scattered across a dozen topic
folders, half-forgotten. PARA flips it: **what's actionable sits closest to you.** A
note about a marketing tactic lives under the *project* it serves while that project is
hot, then graduates to Resources or Archive when it's done. Things flow between buckets
as their status changes — a whole Resources folder can be promoted to a Project the
moment you commit to acting on it.

Forte's own tell is that he numbers his folders 1 to 4 so they sort most-actionable to
least. If your file explorer sorts alphabetically, do the same. The ordering is the
system.

> 💡 **Try it:** open the folder where you keep most of your work files (Documents, a
> cloud drive, your desktop) and sort five recent items into P, A, R or A in your head.
> Notice which ones you hesitate over. Hesitation almost always means "this is a resource
> I'm pretending is a project."

## Which layer PARA actually governs

Here's the part every PARA tutorial skips, and the part that matters most in this
pillar: **your vault has two owners, and PARA only governs one of them.**

Lesson 2 called the top level a contract, and it still is — but the contract is about
*ownership*, not folder count. So the count does change here and the contract doesn't:
PARA adds four folders that are unambiguously **yours**, beside the ones already there.
Nothing the agent owns moves, and nothing new becomes writable by it:

```diagram
vault/
├── vault-rules.md
├── index.md
├── log.md
├── inbox/
├── daily/
├── sources/
├── wiki/
├── projects/   ┐
├── areas/      │ PARA:
├── resources/  │ your notes
└── archive/    ┘
```

The first seven entries are the ones from lesson 2: `vault-rules.md` (the schema), `index.md`
(the catalog, agent-maintained), `log.md` (the append-only record), `inbox/` (raw, unprocessed
capture), `daily/` (dated notes), `sources/` (immutable raw material the agent reads and never
edits) and `wiki/` (agent-owned pages). The bracket marks the four new ones: PARA, your notes
sorted by actionability.

Read that bracket carefully:

- **`projects/`, `areas/`, `resources/`, `archive/`** hold notes *you* wrote and will
  act on. This is the PARA part of the vault, and it's the only part.
- **`inbox/` and `daily/` are not PARA buckets.** The inbox is a loading dock; daily
  notes are chronological. Both sit upstream — what's worth keeping graduates out of
  them into a bucket.
- **`sources/` stays flat.** Raw material isn't more or less actionable, it's just
  source. You reach it through `index.md`, not by walking folders.
- **`wiki/` is never PARA'd.** That's the agent's layer, and its organisation *is*
  `index.md` plus the links between pages. Splitting it across four folders would
  scatter the cross-references and buy nothing.

One line to remember: **you get folders, the agent gets an index.** They answer
different questions. PARA answers *"where do I look when I'm about to act?"* `index.md`
answers *"what does this vault know?"* Give both questions the same structure and you
get a system that's bad at both.

## The thing that must stay in sync

Every move you make in PARA turns some line in `index.md` into a lie, and a stale index
is worse than no index — the agent trusts it and cites paths that no longer exist.

So the rule: **you decide the moves, the AI re-points the index — in the same sitting.**
That's why the task below makes it produce a move *plan* rather than moving anything.
You keep the judgment call and the veto; it keeps the bookkeeping, which is the part
you'd skip at 11pm on a Friday.

**Don't code?** The rule still holds; only the hands change. Drag notes between buckets inside
Obsidian (it rewrites the `[[links]]` for you), then paste a before-and-after list of paths into
a chat assistant and ask it to rewrite the affected `index.md` lines, or fix them by hand while
the moves are fresh.

## How shallow is shallow?

Forte keeps one folder per active project inside his Projects bucket. That's fine, and
it's the line: **one level under a bucket, never two.** Four top-level folders, one
folder per live project or area inside them, and no topic hierarchy below that.

The rule that saves you: if you're about to create a folder *inside* a project folder,
write a note and link it instead. Folders organise storage; links organise meaning
(lesson 3). Deep trees are how a vault becomes a place you file things and never look
again.

## Common failure modes

- **Projects never leave Projects** → nothing moves finished work out → make "move what
  shipped to Archive" part of the weekly review (lesson 10).
- **Resources becomes a giant topic tree** → the old file-by-subject habit crept back → one
  level of folders, and links for everything below it.
- **You can't decide between Project and Area** → you're asking "what topic?" → ask "does
  this have an end date?" If yes, Project; if no, Area.
- **`index.md` points at files that moved** → you moved notes without re-pointing the index
  → move and re-index in the same sitting, as the task does.

## Why it pairs with linking

PARA gives you a calm top-level home so you're never lost when you sit down to work;
`[[links]]` weave meaning across everything regardless of which folder a file happens
to sit in. Keep PARA shallow and let the linking do the heavy lifting.

*insight* — where a long source gets broken into atomic, densely linked concept pages,
